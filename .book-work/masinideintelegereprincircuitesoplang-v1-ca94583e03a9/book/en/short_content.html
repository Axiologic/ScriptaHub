Machines of Understanding Through Circuits

Sufficient interfaces, preserved distinctions and a testable path toward accountable reasoning.

What an answer must justify

A machine may execute a program it could not invent, verify a proof it could not discover, or give a fluent answer without exposing its justification. Machines of Understanding Through Circuits organizes its inquiry around these gaps. SOP Lang is proposed as a common language for knowledge and computation: a way to represent how information is transformed into conclusions and how those transformations might themselves be constructed. The book presents a research architecture, not a completed general machine of understanding.

Here a circuit is an explicit structure of operations and dependencies. Its wires may carry numbers, tables, vectors, relations or alternative interpretations. The circuit source, a particular execution and the content that execution produces are different objects. Preserving those differences permits rules to be reused and conclusions to be traced to the circumstances that produced them. Yet a node named “understand everything” would merely conceal the difficulty. Useful operations need contracts describing their inputs, outputs, assumptions and costs.

Understanding is therefore approached operationally: answering related questions coherently, detecting missing information and revising conclusions when premises change. This does not establish that reality itself is a circuit or settle consciousness. It also does not turn formal validity into worldly truth. A document’s assertion, an interpretation of that assertion and an accepted fact require different treatment. Exact inference from an uncertain premise remains dependent on that premise; a long derivation cannot wash away its uncertainty.

When different histories can share a future

The central proposal concerns the future relevance of the past. Different constructions need not be explored separately if every permitted continuation produces the same relevant observation. A device checking divisibility can forget much of a digit string while retaining the remainder it needs. Likewise, calculating a list’s mean requires its sum and element count, whereas those quantities do not generally determine its median. A sufficient interface preserves what a specified family of operations and questions needs, rather than a universal essence of the data.

The book states a precise condition for this replacement. Summarizing after each admissible operation must give the same result as updating the previous summary, and the final observation must be recoverable from that summary. Repeating this correspondence step by step preserves the answer. For affine functions, coefficients can summarize long construction histories while operations remain within the affine family. Adding an operation that leaves that family requires a richer representation or an explicit restriction. Sufficiency depends on the allowed continuation.

This can turn a branching tree of histories into a shared graph of semantic states. But equal current outputs are insufficient if later actions depend on remaining resources, source provenance or earlier choices. Such information belongs in the frontier that the summary exposes. Nor does the mathematical existence of a sufficient interface establish that it is small, cheap to compute or learnable. Preserving the entire state is exact too, yet offers no compression. The research problem is finding useful smaller interfaces without concealing the work required to obtain them.

This is semantic equivalence, not merely reusing an identical input. Two histories can differ in wording, length and intermediate operations while having the same relevant future. Conversely, agreeing on sampled outputs may conceal a difference that a new continuation exposes. A fingerprint can identify promising candidates for comparison, but exact merging requires a guarantee appropriate to the domain. Interactive systems impose further demands because future actions and environmental responses, rather than only a final value, may be observable. The interface must preserve those possibilities too.

What compression must preserve

Partial information is already usable information. A set of possible values can narrow as constraints arrive without forcing an arbitrary choice. But a conclusion true in one remaining interpretation is merely possible; one true in every remaining interpretation is supported throughout the model. Incompatible premises require a conflict report. They must not become permission to announce that every answer has been proved. Similarly, alternative cases cannot be combined as if their incompatible consequences occurred together.

Abstract interpretation offers disciplined ways to compute with descriptions instead of every concrete state. A sound overapproximation includes all actual possibilities but may add unreal ones. It can safely rule out a branch when even its enlarged possibilities cannot reach the goal. Overlap with the goal, however, does not establish that a concrete solution exists. Acceptance needs a witness or stronger checking. Soundness is consequently different from precision: an analysis allowing almost anything may be sound while deciding almost nothing.

The most revealing losses often concern relations. If two variables are known to be equal, their difference is zero. Retaining only separate intervals forgets that equality and admits differences that cannot occur. A relational representation can preserve the missing constraint without enumerating every value. Circuit frontiers may similarly need equality classes, temporal ordering or other dependencies. Small graph separators can help, while suitable algebra can make some densely connected problems manageable. No single representation is guaranteed to remain compact and useful for every domain.

Refinement narrows a description; revision after a mistaken premise may restore possibilities previously excluded. Fixed-point propagation only says that the available rules can add no further information, not that every solution has been found. These distinctions matter because operations that derive facts, propose alternatives and withdraw assumptions cannot share an unspecified logical meaning merely because all are drawn as circuits.

An answer that can change for the right reason

The proposed language architecture makes these distinctions concrete through a key-transfer example. Ana has a laboratory key and gives it to Maria; a subsequent “she” leaves for the library. Under the stated physical-transfer model, Maria possesses the key immediately afterward whichever person the pronoun denotes. A shared representation can therefore answer the possession question without choosing the departure’s agent. This is selective handling of ambiguity, not a claim that ambiguous language can always be ignored.

Change the question to who left, and the unresolved distinction becomes essential. Ask who later returned the key, and finding it on a table does not identify the person responsible. Correct the original statement from giving the key to showing it, and the possession conclusion loses its support. Other independent information can remain. The intended competence is to change an answer exactly when the text or question warrants it, with a justification identifying the affected dependency. The example describes behavior to build and test, not an already demonstrated prototype.

That behavior requires richer persistent memory to remain accessible behind a smaller question-specific view. A summary suitable for a dominant theme may omit a rare exception that becomes the next question’s subject. Recovering it should not depend on pretending the original summary was universally sufficient. Sources, interpretations and their provenance must permit reconstruction or refinement. Temporal validity, event occurrence and reporting time may differ; missing evidence is not explicit negation; a belief report is not the reported event itself.

Provenance also distinguishes independent support from repeated derivations of the same source. A useful audit offers a short justification, a diagnostic view of alternatives and a way to reproduce the computation. The reason a conclusion holds differs from the reason a search strategy tried it first. An attractive explanation or enormous raw log does not establish practical intelligibility: independent evaluators must actually find errors more reliably or quickly.

Learning representations without hiding the cost

Learning may adjust parameters, compose new circuits or invent a representation that makes many circuits easier to construct. These achievements must be distinguished. A cautious progression begins with supplied interfaces, then automated selection and combination, before asking for invention. Repeated subcircuits can become reusable abstractions, but a library that simply names each solved case is still case memory. Transfer to previously unused combinations is the decisive test.

Counterexamples provide a disciplined improvement mechanism. CEGAR repairs an abstraction when a supposed failure is unrealizable in the concrete model. CEGIS repairs a candidate program that violates its specification. Interface learning asks for a continuation that distinguishes histories previously merged. If each new case requires another exception, the system may have found no economical regularity. Conversely, failure to find a counterexample within a budget is not proof of equivalence. Similarity on examples can suggest a merger without justifying exact acceptance.

The same discipline applies to symbolic, neural and distributed representations. Vectors may retrieve useful candidates from incomplete cues; exact relations may better support a particular contradiction check. These roles can coexist. Comparisons must fix available information, memory and numerical precision, and measure tolerated error. Predictive compression preserves a chosen target under a model’s assumptions, not every future question or causal intervention. Neither a short symbol nor a compact vector makes its interpreter and retrieval costs disappear.

Total efficiency includes building the library, learning interfaces, compiling knowledge, searching, checking answers and updating them. Expensive preparation may pay off across repeated queries and fail to pay off for an isolated question or frequently changing sources. Reporting fast prepared queries alone conceals that distinction. A useful representation earns its cost within a declared usage regime; formal elegance is not enough.

Evidence for a research program

The controlled observations illustrate both sides of the argument. Finite affine circuits compress many construction histories into a much smaller collection of exact functional states. Separate parity-constraint examples show that individual variable domains can miss a conflict that relational algebra exposes. Together they support forgetting irrelevant history while preserving decisive dependencies. The arithmetic representations were supplied, however, and reported extension counts are not measurements of total runtime or speedups against optimized competitors. These experiments do not establish autonomous interface learning or open-language understanding.

The proposed experiments therefore separate exactness, practical savings, ambiguity handling, transfer, learned selection and diagnostic usefulness. Formal tests vary representation richness, topology and algebra, include unfavorable regimes and deliberately seek adversarial counterexamples. Language tests distinguish interpreting the input from reasoning over a correct interpretation. They vary meaningful details and irrelevant wording, test rare exceptions and new combinations, and report coverage and abstention alongside accuracy. A correct answer supported by an invalid proof remains a distinct failure category.

The strongest objections remain part of the program. An interface may be as large as the original problem; a primitive may conceal all the reasoning; verifying meaning may itself demand substantial understanding. A formally correct system may be too costly or narrow to help anyone. Progress requires bounded claims, independent reproduction, visible human contributions and published failures. The ambition is to discover where shared semantic structure supports useful, correctable reasoning—and to identify precisely where it does not.

Research reports should state what observation would overturn each claim before selecting favorable cases. A loss of a valid answer challenges exactness; a lack of savings challenges practical advantage in the tested regime. Failure to diagnose errors challenges the promised usability of the explanation. These failures need different responses. A useful negative result can narrow the domain, reveal an unsuitable abstraction or show that frequent revisions erase the benefits of compilation. Recording those limits gives later researchers a concrete starting point instead of inherited enthusiasm.

The book’s guiding question is what different lines of reasoning may safely share without falsifying their meaning. A smaller frontier is valuable only when it preserves the distinctions that matter, can be reopened when questions change, and remains explicit enough to challenge. Discovering such frontiers is the proposal; establishing when they work is the research still required.
