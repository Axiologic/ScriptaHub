Prologue. The morning it falls
differently
Imagine that tomorrow morning a laboratory announces something
almost ridiculous: two highly precise clocks operating through differ‐
ent mechanisms no longer maintain the same ratio between their
rates. The team checks temperature, power supply and analysis soft‐
ware. Then it replaces the instruments. The difference remains. Other
laboratories reproduce the observation. It is not a fluke, but nobody
yet knows what it is.
One news story will say the laws of nature have changed. Another,
that the universe has received an update. A cautious physicist will say
something has changed in the relationship between measurements
and model. The three sentences do not carry the same weight. The
first interprets; the second, for now, invents a cause; the third defines
a problem.
The scene is imaginary. Its question is real: what would have to hap‐
pen for the expression ‘law of nature’ to lose its meaning? A single de‐
viation? A century of different regularities? The discovery of deeper
rules? Or the discovery of a programmer?
We are accustomed to saying that the universe obeys laws. The verb
hides a small drama. Obeying a human law presupposes that violating
it is possible. A pedestrian can cross against a red light. An electron
does not seem to choose between obedience and revolt. When a result
contradicts a physical law, we usually do not put the electron on trial:
we check the law, conditions and measurement.
Yet the idea of law does something extraordinary. It allows us to use
what has happened to anticipate what has not happened yet. It trans‐
forms experience into a transmissible resource. At the same time, it
can turn a habit into destiny: ‘That is human nature’, ‘That is how the
market works’, ‘The weak always lose’. Sometimes we know a con‐
straint. At other times we hide behind it.
4


This book follows both powers of the concept. The first builds tools,
explanations and possibilities for intervention. The second closes dis‐
cussions before they begin. We have no reason to confuse them or to
give up the first because we fear the second.
We will also reach a possibility seemingly taken from cinema: a simu‐
lated world capable of discovering that it is simulated. The interesting
problem is not whether we can invent such a story. We can invent
countless ones. The problem is whether the story bears the risk of be‐
ing wrong and whether observations exist that separate it from com‐
peting explanations.
Our starting position is deliberately modest: the world can contradict
us. It does not follow that we know what its laws ultimately are. It
does follow, however, that the difference between a good explanation
and a convenient one matters. Perhaps the universe contains no sen‐
tences. But some of our sentences survive encounters with it much
better than others.

How to read this book
Parenthetical citations refer to the bibliography at the end. Philosoph‐
ical texts are presented through interpretations and paraphrases, not
translated quotations claiming a philological precision that is absent.
Examples involving imaginary laboratories, communities, programs
and observers are thought experiments constructed for this book.
They do not represent empirical results.
Three levels will recur: what is well supported experimentally; what
is a disputed philosophical interpretation; and what is a speculative
hypothesis from which we can demand testable consequences. They
are not three rungs of the same ladder. A mathematical proof is rigor‐
ous relative to its premises; an experiment connects a model to part of
the world; a metaphysics asks what kind of world could make such
connections possible.
‘Natural law’ can also designate a moral or legal doctrine. Here, ‘law
of nature’ will primarily designate regularities and descriptive prin‐
ciples about the world. The transition from these to moral obligations
will be discussed separately. That something happens is not, by itself,
an argument that it ought to happen.

I · HOW THE WORLD CAME TO HAVE LAWS
01. A regularity is not yet a law
All the keys on my table are cold. If I declared this finding ‘the first
law of living-room metals’, there would be an immediate argument
against it: I can heat one of the keys. But the problem runs deeper.
Even if nobody ever heated them, the sentence would remain an acci‐
dental description of a collection, not an explanation of how metals
behave.
A law claims more than fidelity to an inventory. It concerns stable re‐
lationships and what would happen in situations different from actual
ones: counterfactuals. This ‘if’ connects law to possibilities, not merely
completed facts. The exact relationship between laws and counterfac‐
tuals remains disputed (Carroll, 2020).
Take a spring. Within a certain deformation range, we can construct a
model in which restoring force is proportional to extension. The mod‐
el does not say that every elastic object behaves this way under all cir‐
cumstances. Stretch the spring too far and the relationship ceases to
be useful. We discover a domain limit, not nature’s decision to become
unlawful.
This yields a practical question worth asking alongside every law
taught in school: under what conditions can it be used? A formula
without a domain resembles a medicine without indications and con‐
traindications. It can be correct in form and misused in the world.
Four things must also be separated. An observation is a situated res‐
ult: the instrument recorded a value. A regularity is a pattern among
results. A law is a relationship to which we attribute a certain stability
and generality. A theory is a structure of concepts, assumptions and
relationships organizing explanations and predictions. A theory does
not turn into a law after accumulating enough confirmations, like an
employee being promoted. The two words serve different functions.
A model is a selective representation used for a problem. It may con‐
tain laws, approximations and conventions. A subway map is not false
because it fails to preserve all distances; it is useful because it elimin‐
7


ates details unsuited to its purpose. A map for evacuating a building
must preserve different relationships, however. Evaluating a model
also means asking what information it must conserve.
Here the first ambiguity of realism appears. Are the letters in the for‐
mula real? They are signs produced by humans. Is the described rela‐
tionship real? It may be: two teams can use different notation and
build devices that work the same way. Is there, additionally, an entity
called a ‘law’ compelling things to behave that way? This is a further
metaphysical thesis. The formula’s success does not automatically de‐
cide it.
Confusing these levels produces two exaggerations. The first says: be‐
cause equations are invented, the world they describe is invented. But
a house’s address is also a convention, without making its walls con‐
ventional. The second says: because some equations work remarkably
well, they are precisely the language in which the universe was writ‐
ten. This conclusion is not mandatory either. Multiple representations
can capture the same structure without any being reality’s ultimate al‐
phabet.
It is reasonable to consider a law good when it makes precise predic‐
tions, works in new situations, connects previously separate phenom‐
ena and allows us to identify when it can no longer be used. No cri‐
terion is sufficient alone. A very vague statement can ‘explain’
everything precisely because it excludes nothing. A relationship fitted
impeccably to known data may fail at the first new observation.
The decisive question becomes: what risk does the explanation take?
If every possible result can be reinterpreted as confirmation, we may
have a worldview but not yet a tool for discriminating among possible
worlds. A law’s power does not lie in the word’s solemnity. It also lies
in the number of things it allows us to declare incompatible with it.

02. When nature had no
legislator
A person can anticipate seasons without believing there are ‘laws of
nature’ in the modern sense. They can build, cultivate and navigate
using practical regularities. The history of this concept must therefore
not be confused with the history of intelligence. The absence of our
vocabulary does not mean an absence of observation, reasoning or
technology.
In Physics, Aristotle distinguishes natural things by their having with‐
in themselves a principle of motion and rest. A bed is made by an art;
a tree develops through its nature. Aristotelian explanation pursues
several kinds of ‘why’: material, form, source of change and purpose.
These are the four causes, and ‘cause’ here has a broader meaning
than the blow setting a ball in motion (Aristotel, Fizica, II).
This is not a clumsy version of Newtonian mechanics. It is a different
distribution of questions. Why does an organism grow in a particular
way? What is it made of? What structure characterizes it? What is a
part’s function? Contemporary science does not answer all these ques‐
tions through a single type of explanation, even though it does not ad‐
opt Aristotelian physics.
It would be equally wrong to treat Chinese thought as Western physics
lacking equations. In Dao De Jing, order is conceived through Dao, re‐
lationships, transformations and ways of acting that do not force
things. Chapter 25 culminates, in common translations, in the idea
that Dao follows what is so of itself, associated with the term ziran.
Here we have neither a quantifiable instruction code nor a demon‐
strated prototype of systems theory. We have a conceptual alternative:
order can be imagined without the metaphor of a sovereign issuing
commands (Laozi, Dao De Jing, cap. 1, 25, 37).
The comparison is fruitful if we preserve the difference. We can ask
what we gain by conceiving order as spontaneous regularity and what
we lose by trying to translate all of nature into obedience. We cannot
9


announce that an ancient text discovered quantum mechanics merely
because it uses suggestive words about transformation and relation‐
ship.
Another challenge appears in Nāgārjuna’s Indian philosophy. His cri‐
tique of existence through an independent intrinsic nature, svabhāva,
emphasizes dependencies and the difficulty of isolating an absolute
foundation. This is not the claim that nothing works or every outcome
is possible. The analysis concerns how concepts and things are de‐
pendent within a specific philosophical and liberating project. It is not
an experimental anticipation of relational physics (Westerhoff, 2026).
It is nevertheless worth using as a question directed at our own re‐
flexes. When we say an electron ‘has a nature’, do we mean an isol‐
ated essence or its behavior in a network of interactions? When we
call an object ‘independent’, independent of what exactly? Philosoph‐
ical comparison can open questions without retroactively granting sci‐
entific certificates.
In theological traditions, order and necessity have likewise been sep‐
arated. Debates about al-Ghazālī show how risky the summary ‘reli‐
gion denied causality’ is. A central question concerned whether the
connection between events is necessary in itself or depends on God.
Scholars’ interpretations differ regarding the relationship among oc‐
casionalism, secondary causes and his other writings. Challenging the
connection’s metaphysical necessity does not simply amount to deny‐
ing observable sequences (Griffel, „Al-Ghazali”).
These traditions do not offer the same doctrine in different costumes.
Aristotle, Laozi, Nāgārjuna and Islamic theologians discuss partly dis‐
tinct problems in contexts with different aims. Mixing them into a
‘universal wisdom’ would be as reductive as declaring them mere er‐
rors preceding modern science.
The common lesson is more sober: the metaphor of law was not inev‐
itable. We could have organized experience around powers, relation‐
ships, rhythms, forms or dependencies. That the language of laws has
proved highly productive does not mean all other questions have be‐
come meaningless.

This history also has a critical advantage. When someone says ‘the hu‐
man mind cannot think of nature otherwise’, we can reply: it already
has. What remains to be established is not whether alternatives are
imaginable, but what they can explain, what they allow us to measure
and where they mislead us.

03. God, geometry and the
world machine
A human law has an author. Where, then, does the law obeyed by
planets come from? In a culture for which creation and divine order
were central questions, the vocabulary of the cosmic legislator was
not mere decoration. It offered a bridge between theology, metaphys‐
ics and the new mathematical physics.
For Descartes, matter is characterized by extension and its changes
are explained mechanically. The stability of order is linked to God’s
unchanging character. In Principles of Philosophy, the laws of motion
do not appear merely as practical summaries of observation. They
have a justification within his philosophical architecture. This confid‐
ence did not prevent some Cartesian collision rules from being wrong.
A metaphysical justification strong for its author does not guarantee
good physics (Descartes, 1644, partea a II-a; Slowik, „Descartes’ Phys‐
ics”).
Newton radically changes the power of mathematical unification.
Principia connects celestial and terrestrial motions through a com‐
mon framework of principles and demonstrations. Its importance lies
not merely in writing formulas but in showing how very different
phenomena can be related through explicit assumptions, approxima‐
tions and comparisons with observation (Newton, 1687/1726).
This unification contains an intellectual promise: we do not necessar‐
ily need a separate explanation for every region of the world. But the
promise does not close every question. Successfully describing gravit‐
ational attraction does not, simply by writing the relationship, explain
gravity’s ultimate mechanism. Newton himself distinguishes what can
be deduced from phenomena from hypotheses about causes that he
cannot establish in the same way.
Imagine two astronomers. The first has an intuitive story about cos‐
mic vortices but cannot obtain sufficiently good trajectories from it.
The second has a precise mathematical relationship, yet the underly‐
ing mechanism troubles him. Whom do we follow? For navigation and
12


calculation, whoever predicts better. For the question about the
nature of interaction, the dispute can continue. This is an important
moment: predictive usefulness and metaphysical satisfaction can part
ways.
The modern concept of law thus gains autonomy. We need not solve
every question about the universe’s author to compare two models of
motion. Believer and nonbeliever can share a laboratory if they ac‐
cept the same procedures of measurement and correction. This is a lo‐
gical possibility of the method, not a claim that the actual history of
science was free of religious and political conflicts.
The machine metaphor also changes what we consider explained. If
understanding means showing the parts and their interactions, pur‐
pose risks being excluded from nature and relocated to the designer.
The machine has a purpose for its builder, not necessarily for its
gears. Such a model clarifies many phenomena but may become too
narrow when used as a premature verdict on organism, mind or soci‐
ety.
A historical qualification is also necessary: ‘science simply replaced
religion’ is too linear a story. For these authors, mathematical, theolo‐
gical and philosophical questions coexist. What gradually changes
also includes where evidence is demanded. A claim about trajectories
can be confronted with measurements, however venerable the meta‐
physics from which it began.
Who benefits from this shift? In principle, anyone who can use a re‐
producible result without belonging to the circle that formulated it. A
public relationship can weaken an authority’s monopoly over explan‐
ation. Who loses? The authority claiming that the speaker’s status suf‐
fices to decide how nature behaves. But a new risk immediately ap‐
pears: mathematical expertise can be used as authority in matters cal‐
culation does not resolve.
Secularizing the method therefore does not eliminate the priestly
temptation. It can change its uniform. The relevant difference is not
whether the speaker wears a robe or a lab coat, but whether they
show how they can be contradicted, what they assumed and how far
their result extends.

04. Hume and Kant: the rift
between world and mind
Every day we light fires, press switches and cross bridges assuming
known regularities will continue. We do not hold a seminar on induc‐
tion before every gesture. David Hume nevertheless observes some‐
thing uncomfortable: that a sequence has repeated does not entail,
through pure logic, that it must repeat tomorrow (Hume, 1748, sec.
IV–V).
The argument is not that everything will become chaotic tomorrow. It
is that the justification ‘it has always worked’ already uses a transfer
from past to future. If we justify the transfer by saying such transfers
worked in the past, we return to what needed justification. Experience
provides extraordinary practical support, but not a deduction making
every other future impossible.
Hume thus separates logical certainty from expectation formed
through experience. Habit plays a central role in how the mind con‐
nects events. I see the first event and expect the second. But where do
I see necessity itself? In observation I encounter events and se‐
quences, not a metaphysical force labeled ‘must’ (Hume, 1748, sec.
VII).
This critique does not demolish research. It removes an overly con‐
venient justification. We can seek more robust models, vary condi‐
tions, test in new situations and organize confidence by degrees. The
philosophical problem of induction remains, but it does not make all
forecasts equal. Lacking an absolute guarantee does not mean having
no reason.
Imagine two people planning to build a bridge. One uses experiments,
calculation and independent checks. The other rolls dice to choose di‐
mensions. Hume does not make them epistemically equivalent. The
first person’s inability to prove nature’s eternal uniformity does not
erase their advantage relative to the goal. Skepticism becomes con‐
fused when it demands of the prudent person an infallibility the im‐
prudent one does not even try to offer.
14


Kant responds by shifting the question: what conditions make experi‐
ence of an objective world possible for us? We do not simply receive a
ready-organized stream for the mind to photograph. In his view, ex‐
perience presupposes forms and categories through which objects can
be known; causality holds a central place. Knowledge of nature is
linked to this contribution of the subject (Kant, 1783, §§14–38;
1781/1787).
This does not mean individual desire can decide an experiment’s out‐
come. When Kant discusses the mind’s contribution, he does not claim
that each person arbitrarily invents gravity. He examines the general
conditions of possible experience. The distinction between such condi‐
tions and a law’s empirical content is essential: the structure of know‐
ledge does not by itself supply every equation of physics.
We can formulate the tension through an example. A camera has a
sensor selecting part of the light. The photograph depends on the
world and the device. Noticing the device’s role does not prove that
the photograph shows nothing real. But neither does it allow us to
confuse the photograph with the photographed thing in every respect.
The analogy has limits: Kant discusses conditions far more funda‐
mental than a technical device. Yet it shows why ‘depends on the
mind’ and ‘is arbitrary’ are different propositions.
Later criticism may ask whether certain structures considered neces‐
sary are actually revisable. Conceptual progress sometimes forces us
to learn ways of thinking that contradict everyday intuition. We need
not declare the entire Kantian dispute settled to acknowledge this pos‐
sibility. But developing a new physical theory does not automatically
resolve the philosophical problem of our access to the world either.
Hume prevents us from confusing past success with absolute neces‐
sity. Kant prevents us from forgetting the mind’s organizing contribu‐
tion. Between them opens the space in which we still work: trying to
know something independent of our desires with conceptual tools
that are not independent of us.
It does not follow that truth is impossible. It follows that a mature pic‐
ture of knowledge must be able to say two things simultaneously:
there is resistance from the world and selection by the mind. Without
15


the first, any story would suffice. Without the second, we would not
understand why discovering a law sometimes also means inventing a
new way to ask the question.

05. Do laws govern or
summarize?
Suppose we have before us the entire history of a universe: every
event, without mistakes or gaps. Would anything remain to learn
about its laws? The question separates several philosophies that can
accept the same experiments and interpret differently what those ex‐
periments tell us exists.
The Humean tradition begins with the distribution of facts. In the
best-system approach associated with David Lewis, laws are not addi‐
tional commands issued to events. They belong to the system achiev‐
ing a privileged balance among simplicity, informative power and, for
probabilistic theories, fit with the facts. Not every convenient sum‐
mary becomes a law (Carroll, 2020).
The advantage is ontological economy. We do not introduce an invis‐
ible administrator of necessity alongside events and properties. The
difficulty is that simplicity sometimes seems vocabulary-dependent:
what is complicated in one notation may be short in another. Another
concern arises: if laws depend on the entirety of history, in what sense
do they explain the very history they summarize? A defender of this
position may reply that an explanation need not be a separate cause
of the facts.
Armstrong proposes another direction. Laws are relations among gen‐
eral properties, called universals, not merely coincidences among par‐
ticular cases. The relationship among universals would support the
pattern’s necessitating character. This conception tries to distinguish a
genuine regularity from a merely repeated accident within the
world’s own structure (Armstrong, 1983).
The price is a new question: what makes the relation among univer‐
sals compel particular instances? If we answer only ‘it is a relation of
necessity’, have we offered an explanation or named the problem?
The objection is not fatal by definition; every theory reaches elements
it no longer explains through others. But where explanation stops
must be made visible.
17


A third family emphasizes powers and dispositions. An object would
not be a mere bearer of labels awaiting external commands; its prop‐
erties would include ways of reacting. A glass’s fragility refers to what
would happen in certain circumstances even if the glass is never
broken. Laws might express these powers. Within the broader debate,
positions on dispositions are not identical and do not all imply the ex‐
istence of separate fundamental laws (Carroll, 2020).
This picture has an intuitive merit: it places order within things. It
also has a difficulty: if we identify a property only by what it produces
and explain the production through the property, we risk circularity.
To avoid mere wordplay, we must show independent relationships
among manifestations, conditions and structures.
Maudlin defends treating fundamental laws as irreducible elements
of ontology. Not every fundamental notion in physics must be recon‐
structible from an inventory of lawless facts. Some things may be
primary in explanation. His position directly challenges the Humean
reduction project (Maudlin, 2007).
The answer is intellectually respectable but unsatisfying to anyone al‐
ways asking ‘why?’ Laws would be what explains, not what still needs
explanation. Yet the opponent does not automatically escape a stop‐
ping point: they may leave the total distribution of facts unexplained.
The dispute is partly about where we accept a basic fact.
Nancy Cartwright challenges the assumption that the success of laws
in controlled arrangements automatically authorizes a uniform pic‐
ture of the entire world. The metaphor of the ‘dappled’ world fore‐
grounds capacities, local conditions and mechanisms producing regu‐
larities. The lesson for science is to investigate conditions for trans‐
porting a result, not assume a formula travels without baggage. This is
a philosophical thesis about the power and limits of laws, not an ex‐
perimental verdict that any unification is impossible (Cartwright,
1999).
Finally, we can be cautious about all these ontologies. Constructive
empiricism associated with Bas van Fraassen demands empirical ad‐
equacy without requiring belief in the complete truth of the unobserv‐
able description. Structural realism, in different forms, places special
18


trust in relationships or structures preserved by theories even when
the picture of objects changes. They are not the same program: one
limits commitment; the other selects it (Chakravartty, 2017).
This book’s working position is a critical realism of constraints. Some
relationships resist us and permit stable interventions. This is a reas‐
on not to treat the world as arbitrary fiction. But from a constraint’s
robustness we do not automatically deduce that we have discovered a
legislator, an ultimate necessity or the only possible description. We
can have excellent reasons to use a law and insufficient reasons to de‐
clare its metaphysics settled.

II · HOW A WORLD’S REGULARITIES ARISE
06. An atlas of laws
The expression ‘law of nature’ shelters claims that do not all work the
same way. If we judge them by a single template, we will declare some
disciplines insufficiently scientific and grant others a precision they
do not possess.
A dynamical law describes how a state evolves. We know the state at a
given time, specify interactions and try to calculate what follows. In a
deterministic model, complete initial data and the same rule yield the
same evolution. The word ‘complete’ does much work. In practice,
data are finite and the problem may be highly sensitive to errors.
A probabilistic law describes distributions of outcomes, not necessar‐
ily the individual outcome. If a model assigns an event a probability of
one quarter, its occurrence does not refute the model, nor does its
nonoccurrence in a single trial. Evaluation requires series, compar‐
able conditions and procedures accounting for random variation.
Claiming that every individual surprise violates a probabilistic law
means demanding something other than what the model asserts.
A conservation principle says a quantity is preserved under relevant
conditions. It is not necessarily a complete instruction for every move‐
ment. Knowing the total money in a system of accounts does not alone
tell us who makes the next transfer. Conservation restricts possibilities
without always choosing a single one. The accounting analogy ex‐
plains a constraint’s role; it does not turn energy into currency.
Constitutive laws describe the response of a particular material type:
the relationship between stress and strain, or current and voltage un‐
der a particular regime. Some are excellent approximations. Their do‐
main is not a hidden defect but part of their meaning. A material mod‐
el can have parameters dependent on temperature and history
without making every prediction impossible.
Then there are scaling regularities in which one quantity varies as a
power of another. Such a fit may suggest a mechanism but does not
identify it alone. Multiple processes can produce similar mathematical
20


forms. A straight line in a convenient representation begins an invest‐
igation; it does not prove that the same principle governs cities, organ‐
isms and markets.
Another type of principle formulates limits or impossibilities: which
operations cannot be performed within a theory with particular re‐
sources and conditions. Such a prohibition can be stronger than a spe‐
cific prediction. But the premises must be retained. ‘It cannot be built
in this model’ does not automatically mean ‘it cannot exist in any lo‐
gically conceivable reality’.
These differences are supplemented by a separate axis: fundamental
or effective. An effective law describes a domain of energies, scales or
conditions without claiming to enumerate all ultimate mechanisms.
Effective theories are not mere patches awaiting truth. They can sys‐
tematically organize which effects matter and how large neglected
corrections are (Burgess, 2007).
In research practice, ‘fundamental’ does not mean we possess the uni‐
verse’s final certificate. It may mean fundamental relative to the cur‐
rent framework or a level of explanation. We should be alert when a
methodological adjective turns, without argument, into a metaphysic‐
al verdict.
Physical laws must also be distinguished from mathematical truths. A
theorem shows what follows from axioms and definitions. To use it
about the world, we must still justify the correspondence between the
formal structure and the investigated system. We can prove a result
perfectly about a model that describes its object poorly. Deductive rig‐
or does not automatically repair the choice of premises.
No definition is an empirical discovery merely because it carries sym‐
bols. If we define a quantity as the ratio of two others, the relationship
is true by convention; what needs investigation is how those quantit‐
ies behave and why the definition helps. Sometimes confusion arises
precisely when an accounting identity or definition is presented as a
causal explanation.
This atlas offers a simple discipline. Before asking whether a law is
true, we ask what kind of claim it is, what domain it has, what data
would count against it and which part of the conclusion comes from
21


definitions. The word ‘law’ does not replace these questions. A science
book becomes more useful by explaining differences than by collect‐
ing formulas under the same solemn label.

07. Symmetry: what remains
when we change our viewpoint
Rotate a round table about its center. Its shape stays the same. This is a
simple geometric symmetry. In physics, the idea can become much
more powerful: we systematically change an experiment’s description
or arrangement and ask what remains unchanged in the theory’s
structure.
Not every change is a symmetry. If we move an experiment from one
environment to another, we may have changed physical conditions,
not merely its position on a map. To formulate an invariance, we must
specify what we transform and what we preserve. A vague symmetry
is as unhelpful as an equality in which we do not know what we com‐
pare.
Emmy Noether’s theorems show, under precise conditions of the vari‐
ational formulation, a link between continuous symmetries and con‐
servation relations. In standard examples, time-translation invariance
is associated with energy conservation, spatial-translation invariance
with momentum, and rotational invariance with angular momentum.
But there is no universal rule that ‘any symmetry produces any con‐
servation’: the nature of the symmetry, action and problem conditions
matter (Noether, 1918/1971).
The important intuition is this: if shifting the time origin does not
change the dynamics’ structure for a relevant closed system, the the‐
ory does not arbitrarily privilege one moment through a special in‐
struction. This formal uniformity can yield constraints on evolution. A
conservation principle can thus be linked to the theory’s architecture
rather than added as an isolated rule.
There is a popular trap. People sometimes pass directly from ‘energy
is conserved’ to claims about the entire universe without checking
whether total energy and the required symmetry are defined in that
context. In a general spacetime, local and global problems are not in‐
23


terchangeable. The theorem teaches precisely that conditions must be
preserved, not forgotten once the slogan is memorized (Noether,
1918/1971).
Symmetry may exist in the rules without existing in the chosen state.
Balance an idealized pencil on its tip in a perfectly symmetric ar‐
rangement. Surrounding directions are not favored by the descrip‐
tion, yet the fallen pencil will have a particular direction. The example
is physically imperfect but shows the logical difference: the state can
select something the law does not select.
This is one route by which new phenomena arise without rewriting
fundamental rules. If many components reach an ordered collective
state, the useful description may include variables not obvious when
looking at a single component. Symmetry breaking forces us to distin‐
guish law, state and observable response (Burgess, 2007).
We can now formulate a question about mind. Why do we like a the‐
ory’s symmetry so much? Because it reduces the number of cases
needing separate treatment and reveals what is structural rather than
accidental. But this preference is epistemically justified only when the
structure produces testable results. An elegant equation can organize
a wrong idea as elegantly as a good one.
In a thought experiment, a researcher discovers that their instrument
always gives the same result after rotation. They might conclude that
the world is isotropic, without a privileged direction at that level. But
the instrument might instead be insensitive to the difference sought.
Invariance of observation is not automatically invariance of reality.
We must know which variations the instrument could have detected.
This problem will return in the simulation hypothesis. If a program
computed space on a lattice, certain implementations might introduce
preferred directions. If we do not find them, it does not follow that
every simulation is excluded; it follows that we have not found the
signature that this model class promises in the tested domain.
Symmetry is thus both a unifying power and a lesson in precision. It
does not merely say the world is beautiful. It says which changes
should not matter within a particular framework and sometimes of‐
fers an exact way to be wrong.

08. The arrow of time and order
born of probability
A broken glass does not spontaneously reassemble on the table. Per‐
fume released into a room does not ordinarily gather back into its
bottle. In our experience, time has a direction. How does this asym‐
metry arise if some microscopic descriptions allow reversible evolu‐
tion?
Before the physical answer, we can understand a counting idea. Con‐
sider twenty ideal, independent coins with equally probable faces.
There is a single configuration in which all show the same chosen
face. There are 184,756 configurations with exactly ten faces of one
type and ten of the other. The calculation is the binomial coefficient
‘20 choose 10’. We have not conducted a study: this is a mathematical
result for the specified model.
If an observer records only how many coins show each face, very
many distinct configurations become the same coarse state for them.
Here lies the idea of microstate and macrostate. The microstate pre‐
serves details; the macrostate groups situations agreeing in the quant‐
ities tracked. A macroscopic state may be much more frequent not be‐
cause each realization is favored, but because it has far more realiza‐
tions.
This example does not by itself prove the second law of thermody‐
namics. Coins are not molecules, and the choice of distribution and
dynamics matters. It does explain, however, why order at the distribu‐
tion level does not require special instructions to every component to
produce the collective result.
Boltzmann entropy links, within the appropriate framework, a macro‐
state to the logarithm of the number or volume of microstates compat‐
ible with it. Entropy is not simply ‘disorder’ in the sense of an untidy
room. The notion depends on the physical description and relevant
variables. An alphabetically ordered library has no special thermody‐
namic value for that reason alone.
25


There is another decisive condition: the past. Starting from a special
state and examining typical evolutions compatible with it can explain
the direction of macroscopic processes. But why did that special state
exist? Discussions of the hypothesis of a low-entropy past show that
dynamical laws and cosmological conditions are not the same piece of
the explanation. Various philosophical programs try to integrate them
differently (Chen, 2020).
This is a general lesson about the origin of observed laws. A regularity
may depend on the rules of evolution plus the state from which the
world begins. If we forget the second term, we attribute to dynamics a
power it does not possess alone. Two universes with the same equa‐
tions and different initial conditions need not have the same macro‐
scopic history.
In small systems, fluctuations cannot always be treated as negligible
details. Relations such as Jarzynski’s equality link far-from-equilibri‐
um processes to free-energy differences through precise statistical av‐
erages. This does not authorize miraculous machines; it refines how
we relate thermodynamics to outcome distributions (Jarzynski, 1997).
Notice the change of perspective. A highly reliable law at everyday
scale may have a statistical formulation at another level. This does not
cancel its practical reality. Nor does it mean every improbability be‐
comes a reasonable hope. ‘It is not forbidden in every microscopic de‐
tail’ and ‘it is worth expecting to happen’ are radically different
claims.
Life does not refute entropy increase merely because organisms main‐
tain ordered structures. An organism exchanges matter and energy
with its environment; looking only at local order means conveniently
cutting out the system. Analysis must track exchanges and balances,
not merely compare two pictures, one of a cell and another of a mix‐
ture.
More philosophically important: if the arrow of time also depends on
a special condition of the world, part of what we experience as every‐
day necessity carries the memory of a history. The universe can pro‐
duce stable regularities not readable solely in its list of interactions.
Sometimes visible law is the meeting of dynamics, statistics and the
past.

09. How new rules arise without
a new legislator
Imagine inhabitants of a universe containing only a large network of
locally interacting elements. They discover persistent objects, waves,
boundaries between regions and abrupt transitions. One declares:
every new phenomenon proves that the world’s author added a rule.
Another asks: might the same rule, repeated over many components,
produce all this?
The second possibility is real in mathematical models. Cellular auto‐
mata update cell states through local rules. Some systems with ex‐
tremely simple descriptions have rich behavior. Matthew Cook proved
the computational universality of Rule 110 in suitable configurations.
The result concerns a formal system and its ability to simulate compu‐
tation; it does not prove that our universe is a cellular automaton or
that an arbitrarily chosen initial state will generate civilizations (Cook,
2004).
It does not follow that every complexity comes from a short program.
A two-line program replaying a gigantic file does not explain the file’s
information. Rule simplicity must be evaluated together with the sim‐
plicity of the initial state and boundary conditions.
Consider a more modest model: on an unbounded line, independent
particles take equal steps left or right with equal probabilities. There
is no individual instruction to ‘spread out the concentration’. Yet the
collective distribution has calculable properties.
If each step has length a, mean displacement is zero and mean
squared displacement after n steps is n·a². Independence and equal
probability are premises, not discoveries about every particle. The cal‐
culation follows because cross-correlation terms between steps aver‐
age to zero. The characteristic distance thus grows as the square root
of the number of steps. We have obtained a collective regularity
without a coordinator tracking the entire cloud.
27


In appropriate limits, such models lead to diffusion descriptions. The
point is not to identify every real molecule with a game coin, but to
see the explanation’s form: specify the microscopic mechanism,
choose collective variables, justify approximations and derive a rela‐
tionship at another scale. ‘Emergence’ becomes informative only
when we can show such transitions.
Sometimes multiple microscopic mechanisms lead to similar macro‐
scopic behaviors. This is the idea of universality in precise contexts.
Details differing between systems may become unimportant for cer‐
tain quantities at certain scales. The renormalization group provides a
framework for studying how descriptions transform when scale
changes and for separating relevant details from those that matter in‐
creasingly less (Burgess, 2007).
Coarse-graining represents multiple fine states at a common level; it
does not physically erase details. Sometimes their effects are sup‐
pressed in the observables tracked; at other times ignored informa‐
tion returns and the model fails. The justification depends on the sys‐
tem.
This relative autonomy of levels explains why we can do science
without knowing the ultimate basis. An inhabitant of a simulated
world could discover correct fluid laws without knowing the pro‐
cessor. We can describe a material through collective variables
without tracking each constituent. Being effective does not mean be‐
ing fictional. It means being true or useful at a specified resolution
and under specified conditions.
At least two meanings of emergence must nevertheless be distin‐
guished. In the weak sense, collective behavior depends on compon‐
ents and rules but is difficult to anticipate or requires a new descrip‐
tion. In the strong sense, properties or powers arise that would not, in
principle, be determined or explainable by the complete basis accord‐
ing to the adopted criterion. The second meaning is a far more de‐
manding thesis. Complexity and our surprise alone do not prove it.
Saying water ‘invents’ liquidity can be a good metaphor but does not
replace analysis. First we must establish what we call a property, what
‘derivable’ means and what information about the basis we have al‐
28


lowed. Sometimes we cannot calculate. Sometimes we lack suitable
concepts. At other times we claim ontological independence. These
problems must not be crammed into one word.
Can new regularities arise without new fundamental rules? Yes, and
models show us how. Emergence does not automatically explain why
basic rules, an initial state or something rather than nothing exists.
Demanding all these answers would turn explanation into a magic
word.

10. More than the sum of
particles
Saying that a novel consists of characters is true. Saying that under‐
standing the alphabet suffices to explain betrayal in the novel is ab‐
surd. Yet the fictional characters require no additional letters outside
the text. The example shows that dependence on a substrate and ex‐
planatory sufficiency of the substrate’s vocabulary are not the same
thing.
‘Reductionism’ designates several theses. One is ontological:
everything in the domain discussed depends on physical components
and their relations. Another is methodological: to understand, we
break the system into parts and investigate mechanisms. A third is ex‐
planatory: all good explanations can be replaced without significant
loss by explanations at the lowest level. We can accept the second’s
usefulness without having proved the first, and accept the first
without being committed to the third.
A molecular explanation of a substance may be indispensable for one
question and needlessly detailed for another. If I want to know why a
bridge collapsed, I may need material properties, geometry, loads and
maintenance history. Even an available complete list of particles does
not automatically tell me which comparisons matter for design and
responsibility.
Think of a program bug. It is physically realized by circuits and
memory. But the explanation ‘this branch does not handle the zero
case’ may identify the problem better than a description of every elec‐
trical switching event. We invoke no supernatural power of software.
We choose a level at which regularity and intervention are intelli‐
gible.
The same principle helps us discuss top-down causation without mys‐
ticism. A channel’s shape constrains water flow. The channel’s geo‐
metry is realized through matter; it does not come from outside phys‐
ics. Yet to explain the difference between two flows, the relevant vari‐
30


able may be shape rather than the complete microscopic inventory.
Some debates on emergent causation pursue a stronger thesis, but the
ordinary example of constraints does not prove it alone.
The critic of reductionism is right to demand attention to organiza‐
tion, context and levels. They are wrong to think ‘the whole’ explains
something before specifying through which relations it does so. A sen‐
tence such as ‘the system has a holistic energy’ does not become an ex‐
planation merely because it sounds more generous than the vocabu‐
lary of mechanisms. It must show effects, conditions and observable
differences.
There is also a mathematical argument against an overly naive reduc‐
tion: knowing microscopic rules does not guarantee a general al‐
gorithm answering all macroscopic questions. Gu and colleagues con‐
struct models in which certain properties of infinite systems are unde‐
cidable. The result has technical premises and does not automatically
transfer to every finite material, but it destroys the simple inference
‘known rules, therefore every property can be computed’ (Gu et al.,
2009).
It does not follow that the higher level introduces new matter. It fol‐
lows that ontological reduction, if true, is not the same as having a
computational method or short explanation available. We may live in
a world entirely dependent on a few rules and still need different dis‐
ciplines to understand it.
Rigorous pluralism does not put all explanations on an equal footing.
Biological explanation must be compatible with relevant physical con‐
straints; economic explanation cannot ignore resources; psychological
explanation cannot turn every adverse result into confirmation. But
compatibility does not require eliminating the vocabulary of func‐
tions, information, institutions and motives.
The useful question is not ‘are you a reductionist or a holist?’ as
though choosing a camp. It is: for this problem, which details are ne‐
cessary, which relationships are robust and which intervention would
distinguish the explanations? Sometimes we must descend to compon‐
ents. At other times we must raise our gaze until we see the configura‐
tion that makes them relevant.
31


A world of simple rules is not necessarily a world with simple explan‐
ations for every question. This may be the most important correction
to the dream of a ‘theory of everything’: even if we obtained an ex‐
traordinary fundamental theory, we would still have to discover what
happens when everything is put together.

11. Life: regularities with memory
If an organism survives, has it obeyed the ‘law of the strongest’? Only
if we retrospectively call every surviving organism ‘strong’. But then
we have explained nothing. We have renamed the result.
In evolutionary theory, the relevant success is not reducible to muscu‐
lar strength or individual survival. What matters is differential trans‐
mission of traits across generations in a concrete environment. An ad‐
vantage in one environment can become a cost in another. Selection
can favor resilience, fecundity, cooperation, conflict avoidance or ex‐
ploitation of others, depending on relationships and conditions. It
needs no conscious goal and guarantees no moral progress (Darwin,
1859/1872).
This changes the meaning of a biological law. Part of the explanation
is general: variation, heredity, reproductive differences. Another part
is historical: which variations existed, in what order they appeared,
which environment they encountered and what other traits accom‐
panied them. The general principle does not replace a case’s causal
history.
We can see the difference in an elementary genetic model. If two vari‐
ants, A and a, have frequencies p and q, with p + q = 1, and the union
of the two genetic contributions is independent and random, pair
probabilities are p², 2pq and q². The calculation is the expansion of (p
+ q)². To interpret it as a Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, biological as‐
sumptions must also be specified, such as the absence of forces alter‐
ing frequencies in the idealized model. The conditional equation is
useful precisely because deviations force us to seek which condition is
unmet.
Biology is no less scientific for giving conditions importance. The
problem would be presenting conditions as optional details. No pre‐
diction about a trait’s success is serious if it forgets the environment in
which success is measured.
33


There is another complication: organisms modify the environment se‐
lecting them. A human community can change its institutions, re‐
source access and support for children or vulnerable people. Without
attributing precise genetic mechanisms to particular social changes,
we can observe the logical relation: if environment matters for ad‐
vantage, changing the environment can change the advantage. ‘Natur‐
al’ does not mean ‘independent of what we do’.
Darwin also discusses social instincts, sympathy and sexual selection.
His writings nevertheless contain judgments marked by his era’s hier‐
archies and prejudices. Reading them as foundational texts does not
require turning every passage into contemporary authority. The prin‐
ciple of selection must be separated from speculative historical applic‐
ations and moral hierarchies attributed to human groups (Darwin,
1871/1874).
When someone invokes ‘the laws of biology’ to declare that social as‐
sistance contradicts nature, the answer need not be denying evolu‐
tion. We ask which model, variable and evidence support the claim.
Support, cooperation and institutions are also processes in the natural
world. The existence of selection does not entail that we ought to let
someone suffer, just as the existence of infections does not entail that
we ought to avoid treatment.
Who benefits biologically from the ability to identify regularities? In
an environment where anticipation changes opportunities for action,
a system learning useful relationships may have an advantage. But
‘useful’ and ‘completely true’ are not identical. A simplified rule can
guide local action well and mislead in another domain. This is a pos‐
sibility argument, not proof that every human error is a selected ad‐
aptation.
This is precisely where improvised evolutionary stories must be
avoided. We can quickly invent a story about the advantage of a be‐
lief, generosity or cruelty. Empirical support requires comparisons,
mechanisms, measurements and alternatives. That an explanation
seems plausible retrospectively does not prove the process happened
that way.
34


Nor do economic and biological success overlap by definition. Income,
status, number of children, survival of descendants and well-being
are different variables. Saying a group ‘wins’ without specifying the
indicator may conceal a change of subject. Having more descendants
does not demonstrate intellectual or moral superiority; having more
money does not demonstrate a hereditary advantage.
Life ultimately teaches us to conceive laws without erasing history.
General regularity and contingent event are not adversaries. A rule
can explain why certain differences matter, while history can explain
why precisely those differences were available. Between them ap‐
pears a world where success is real, contextual and in no way a cos‐
mic verdict on a being’s worth.

12. Can laws change?
If we melt ice, the world after melting seems to have different rules. A
rigid object has become a liquid. We can attribute the change to tem‐
perature and collective state without assuming fundamental interac‐
tions were rewritten. This is the first, common and important sense in
which ‘new laws appear’: the domain and effective description
change.
The second sense concerns parameters. A relationship may retain the
same form, but a quantity we considered constant may depend on
time, location or an unknown field. In a broader theory, what was a
fixed parameter can become a dynamical variable. We have not elim‐
inated the idea of law; we have moved the boundary between rule
and state.
Searches concerning the constancy of fundamental quantities are
genuine physical research. Atomic-clock comparisons, spectral obser‐
vations and other systems allow variations to be tested with different
sensitivities and systematic errors. Uzan surveys both methods and
the need for coherent theoretical frameworks to interpret results. This
literature does not identify variation with proof of simulation (Uzan,
2011).
One technical detail has enormous philosophical importance: we
measure ratios. If we say a dimensional constant ‘changed its value’,
we must specify units and standards. A unit change can change the
written number without changing the world. Dimensionless quantit‐
ies, such as certain ratios and dimensionless constants, avoid this dir‐
ect ambiguity (Uzan, 2007).
Return to the two imaginary clocks. If both accelerated by exactly the
same ratio, together with every process through which we observe
and remember, we would have no unchanged internal standard
against which to detect the acceleration. If one changes its rate relat‐
ive to the other, there is a measurable difference. An experiment has
no access to ‘time outside the universe’ merely because we call an in‐
strument a clock.
36


The third sense of change would be more radical: the fundamental
rule itself would change. But we can immediately ask whether the
modification follows another rule. If so, we have a metalaw. If not, we
must explain what ‘follows no rule’ means and how we would distin‐
guish this from a rule we do not yet know.
A universe in which parameter k increases according to k(t) = k₀ + at is
not a lawless universe. It has a richer rule than k = constant. A uni‐
verse in which k is modified by an external operator would have un‐
controlled inputs from its internal perspective. But the operator’s be‐
havior might itself be regular, random, goal-dependent or entirely
opaque to observers.
Thus, ‘laws evolve’ is not yet an explanation. We must specify what
evolves, relative to which time, through what mechanism and with
what consequences. Otherwise we move the mystery from one noun
to another. An explanation of change presupposing a possibility space,
update rules and a probability measure already has a lawlike struc‐
ture it must acknowledge.
There are also hypotheses attempting to explain why we observe par‐
ticular parameter values. In a hypothetical ensemble of different re‐
gions or universes, observers would appear only under conditions
compatible with their existence. This observational selection may
matter but does not itself produce the ensemble or establish how
probable its variants are. Saying ‘we had to observe a world in which
we can exist’ is less than explaining that world’s mechanism.
Lee Smolin proposed cosmological natural selection: in a speculative
scenario, black holes give birth to descendant universes with para‐
meter variations. Universes producing more black holes would have
more descendants. The analogy tries to replace arbitrary choice of
constants with a mechanism of variation and reproduction. It is not a
confirmed observation of universes being born or proof that life is the
cosmos’s purpose (Smolin, 2006).
The dispute shows why precision matters even in speculative re‐
search. In a 2006 critique, Vilenkin argues that changing the cosmolo‐
gical constant could increase black-hole production, contradicting the
invoked optimization criterion. Smolin responds by challenging the
physical assumptions used in that argument. We cannot turn this con‐
37


troversy into an experimental verdict on universe reproduction. We
can see, however, that a hypothesis gains content when it produces a
constraint and critics can say exactly where it would fail (Vilenkin,
2006; Smolin, 2006).
In such scenarios, effective laws can have a genealogy. But the mech‐
anism of generation, variation and selection itself presupposes rules.
The problem is displaced and may become more precise without dis‐
appearing. A mechanism for the emergence of constants would be a
major discovery even if it did not explain why the possibility space it‐
self exists. Partial explanation is not useless merely because the ulti‐
mate question remains open.
We can also conceive a situation with no simple ultimate level. The
world might allow successive descriptions without an available final
foundation. This is a metaphysical possibility, not an established con‐
clusion of physics. Likewise, a very simple fundamental structure may
exist while we cannot prove there is no deeper one.
What would living with this uncertainty mean? Not nervously check‐
ing gravity at every step. Practical confidence must relate to evidence
and the domains where it works. But neither should we declare an an‐
omaly impossible merely because it would contradict the textbook.
Textbooks are documents about knowledge, not contracts signed by
the universe.
A law can be revised for three very different reasons: we measured
incorrectly; we discovered the domain was narrower; we found dif‐
ferent dynamics. Only the last could support certain senses of pro‐
found physical change, and even it does not automatically identify an
external author. When we say ‘nature changed its rules’, we must be
sure we are not merely describing the moment we understood them
better.

III · THE MIND TRYING TO UNDERSTAND
13. The brain that demands
explanations
You hear a noise in an empty house. The mind immediately seeks
something that produced it. In many situations this reflex is useful.
But ‘what produced it?’ can turn too quickly into ‘who wanted to pro‐
duce it?’ Between detecting a relationship and attributing an intention
lies a difference that language easily hides.
Psychology offers no single verdict on why people believe in laws of
nature. Research studies more bounded processes: pattern identifica‐
tion, explanations of properties, responses to uncertainty and how
people generalize. Applying these to the metaphysics of laws is an in‐
terpretation that must be announced, not a direct experimental result.
Cimpian and Salomon propose the ‘inherence heuristic’: a tendency to
explain some patterns through supposedly inherent characteristics of
things without sufficiently examining history and external conditions.
It is a theoretical framework supported by several research lines, not
a universal law of judgment. A social pattern may seem natural be‐
cause we attribute its cause to a group’s nature, although the conven‐
tions producing it should also be investigated (Cimpian și Salomon,
2014).
The connection to our topic is clear, but it is an extrapolation: ‘that is
how things are’ can stop exploration of mechanisms. If a child asks
why a social role is more often occupied by a particular group, ‘be‐
cause those people are made for it’ sounds explanatory. In reality, it
may close precisely the investigation of access, selection, learning and
expectations.
Whitson and Galinsky reported, across six experiments, a connection
between lack of control and illusory pattern perception in different
tasks. The result became attractive for the idea that the mind com‐
pensates for uncertainty with order. But this story must not be presen‐
ted as definitive fact (Whitson și Galinsky, 2008).
39


Van Elk and Lodder reported seven conceptual-replication experi‐
ments in which they did not find expected effects on the measures
used. Their analyses questioned the robustness of control manipula‐
tions and indicated publication-bias problems in the examined literat‐
ure. This does not prove that the need for control never matters. It
shows that an elegant psychological explanation can be more fragile
than its popularity (Van Elk și Lodder, 2018).
It is a perfect lesson for a book about laws: we cannot combat naive
belief in regularities by ourselves using an insufficiently verified psy‐
chological regularity. The psychology of science must undergo the
same discipline it demands of science.
There is also a deeper logical error. Suppose someone believes in a
law because they need security. This psychological origin does not im‐
ply the law is false. A person may believe something true for a weak
reason and something false for an intelligent reason. A belief’s genea‐
logy and testing its content are different investigations.
How might the effect of the word ‘law’ be studied more directly? Here
is a proposal, not an experiment already conducted: participants re‐
ceive the same numerical regularity labeled either ‘law’, ‘provisional
model’ or ‘observed pattern’. They are then asked how far they gener‐
alize, which counterexamples they accept and which interventions
they consider possible. Wording, numerical competence and source
prestige should be controlled, and hypotheses and analysis estab‐
lished before seeing results.
An interesting result would be if the label ‘law’ changed confidence
even when data and precision were identical. Absence of an effect
would be equally important. It should be repeated across languages,
contexts and populations before we speak of a general mechanism. A
student study cannot directly establish the psychology of all societies.
Education could train two complementary reflexes: courage to seek
regularities and patience to put them to the test. Telling students
‘think critically’ is not enough. We must give them cases where a se‐
ductive explanation and a correct one make different predictions,
then let them observe the difference.
40


The mind needs compression: we cannot preserve every experience
as an episode unrelated to the rest. The danger is not the existence of
mental patterns. It is forgetting the distinction between a reassuring
pattern and one that survived a serious attempt to contradict it.

14. What does understanding
actually mean?
One person may correctly calculate an object’s orbit without intuit‐
ively imagining it in every detail. Another may draw a highly convin‐
cing but incorrect picture. Which one understands? The question
shows that we use the same word for different competencies.
We can recognize a pattern, predict, explain a mechanism, identify
what would change if we intervened and connect the result to a more
general structure. These capacities sometimes support one another
but do not coincide. A results table can predict without explaining. A
causal story can seem clear and fail numerically.
Understanding also has an aspect of transfer. If I memorize an answer
for one situation without knowing when it applies, my competence is
fragile. If I can systematically modify conditions and anticipate which
part of the answer remains, I have grasped something deeper. A well-
understood law is not merely a correctly reproduced formula, but a
map of dependencies and limits.
Mathematics extends this ability because it allows precise tracking of
relationships, including when visual intuition gives way. But it does
not eliminate the representation problem. We must decide which
quantities correspond to the world, what we ignore and which meas‐
urements can check the result. An impeccable calculation may answer
an unsuitable question.
Why does the world seem so accessible to mathematical descriptions?
We can consider their success evidence of its structure. We can also
notice selection: we call precisely the descriptions that worked ‘dis‐
covered laws’, while failed attempts disappear from textbooks. The
two observations do not cancel each other. Selection explains part of
our retrospective picture without making the existence of exploitable
regularities trivial.
42


Nor is there a simple reason why an evolved brain had to understand
the entire universe. For many biological functions, distinguishing op‐
portunities and dangers at the scale of its life would suffice. Micro‐
scopic or cosmological understanding is achieved through cultural ex‐
tensions: languages, instruments, mathematics, institutions of verific‐
ation. This observation about supports’ role establishes no fixed limit
to mind; it shows that ‘what humans can do’ is not reducible to an un‐
aided individual’s intuition.
Relativity offers a historical example: understanding required revis‐
ing assumptions about simultaneity, clocks and geometry, not merely
more precise measurements in the old vocabulary. In his exposition
for the public, Einstein connects concepts to measurement procedures
and shows why time cannot simply be treated as an identical back‐
ground for every observer. In general relativity, spacetime geometry
participates in describing gravity. These conceptual changes do not
mean nature changed its rules when physicists changed the theory
(Einstein, 1916/1920).
Our ability to learn such a framework matters for the question of
mental limits. An intuition formed in everyday circumstances is not
an infallible judge of physical possibility. But not every counterintuit‐
ive idea deserves adoption either. The gain comes from testable rela‐
tionships enabled by the new framework, not its difficulty or distance
from common sense.
Artificial intelligence makes the difference between finding a relation‐
ship and human understanding of it more visible. The AI Feynman
system uses symbolic regression and physics-inspired heuristics to re‐
cover formulas from data, evaluated partly on a set of known equa‐
tions. The results show the power of guided-search methods; they do
not demonstrate automatic discovery of the universe’s foundations
(Udrescu și Tegmark, 2020).
SINDy seeks dynamical descriptions with few nonzero terms chosen
from a candidate library. It makes a fruitful assumption explicit: dy‐
namics may be simple in suitable coordinates and bases. Success nev‐
ertheless depends on representation, data and the list of possibilities
supplied to the method. Finding the equation in a chosen vocabulary
is not identical to inventing the relevant vocabulary (Brunton, Proctor
și Kutz, 2016).
43


Now imagine an AI predicting an experiment better than humans but
producing a model nobody can explain in one page. There are at least
three options. We can reject the model because it does not resemble
our understanding. We can trust it without limit because it predicts
well. Or we can separate use from justification: test it outside its con‐
struction data, investigate stability and seek partial explanations of
important mechanisms.
The third option is most promising. It does not demand that every
truth fit our working memory, but neither does it transfer authority to
an opaque box. We can understand conditions under which a result is
reliable even without holding every operation that produced it in
mind. At the same time, for risky interventions, prediction without
knowledge of limits may be insufficient.
Can there be a true law the human mind cannot understand? It de‐
pends on ‘understand’. Perhaps we cannot visualize it. Perhaps we
cannot compute its consequences unaided. Perhaps we cannot formu‐
late suitable concepts. These difficulties do not prove a definitive cog‐
nitive frontier exists. But past success does not guarantee that every
structure of the world will become transparent to us either.
The most productive question is more concrete: what extension would
let us understand the next relationship? A notation, a new experi‐
ment, a simulation, a verification tool or a collective concept? Some‐
times the mind does not overcome a limit through greater effort in the
same form. It changes its way of working.
44


15. Are there things even
intelligence cannot compute?
‘If we knew all the laws, we could predict everything.’ This sentence
compresses at least three assumptions: that we also knew the relevant
state, that we could perform the computation and that the question
had a solution accessible through a general method. None comes free
with the list of equations.
The first limit is observational. We measure with a certain precision
and do not control every condition. In a sensitive system, very small
initial differences can grow over time until detailed predictions di‐
verge. Lorenz’s model shows how simple deterministic equations can
produce nonperiodic evolutions sensitive to initial state. Determinism
is not equivalent to practical predictability (Lorenz, 1963).
This does not mean we can say nothing. Some statistical properties or
outcome ranges may be predictable even when the exact trajectory is
not. Moving from ‘I do not know the detail’ to ‘everything is equally
probable’ is an error. The prediction limit must be formulated at the
level of the question and time horizon.
The second limit concerns resources. A computation possible in prin‐
ciple may require too much time, memory or energy for a concrete
observer. Seth Lloyd’s research on physical limits to computation links
computational capacity to resources and physical theory. These are
not proofs about every metaphysically possible substrate; they are
limits formulated within specified physical frameworks (Lloyd, 2000).
The third limit is more severe: absence of a general algorithm for a
precise problem class. Cubitt, Pérez-García and Wolf proved undecid‐
ability of the spectral-gap problem for certain families of quantum lat‐
tice systems in the thermodynamic limit. This does not mean every fi‐
nite material has a property impossible to investigate. It means the de‐
sired universal procedure solving every instance of the constructed
class does not exist (Cubitt, Pérez-García și Wolf, 2022).
45


It is tempting to use such theorems as a general certificate of mystery.
That would be a mistake. An undecidability proof has a domain and
premises. It does not tell us the particular problem on our desk is in‐
soluble, that intuition solves it miraculously or that science has
reached its end. It forces us to relinquish a universal promise, not the
investigation of cases.
Nor do Gödel’s theorems authorize ‘every important truth exceeds
reason’. They concern limits of formal systems with precise proper‐
ties. Leaping from arithmetic to claims about soul, cosmos or an AI’s
omniscience requires additional arguments. Invoking a mathematical
name is not one of them (Raatikainen, 2025).
There is another limit, often more relevant than computational diffi‐
culty: identifiability. Can two different mechanisms produce exactly
the same available observations? If so, an observer cannot separate
them merely by analyzing the same data more carefully. This is not
lack of intelligence but lack of a difference in accessible information.
An elementary example: a thermometer displays 20, and we do not
know whether the temperature is 20 and the instrument correct, or
the temperature is 18 and the instrument has a constant two-unit er‐
ror. More identical records do not resolve the ambiguity. An independ‐
ent standard or another intervention type may resolve it. The problem
requires not merely more computation, but different information.
In the simulation hypothesis, this distinction becomes decisive. A sim‐
ulated and a nonsimulated world might have the same results for
every permitted internal experiment. In that case, lack of evidence
would not necessarily stem from a simulator’s conspiracy. It would
stem from observational equivalence of the descriptions.
There is also selection among the questions we can formulate. An ob‐
server built from a world’s processes can access only certain vari‐
ables. But we should not turn this observation into a grand paradox
before describing a model. We can prove limits in a well-defined
formal system; we cannot assume every imagined limit applies to our
universe.
Being rigorous about limits paradoxically means being more ambi‐
tious. Instead of saying ‘it cannot be understood’, we ask: can it not be
measured, not be computed with available resources, does no general
46


algorithm exist, or is there no observable difference? Each diagnosis
suggests a different research program. Confusing them produces
needless resignation and impossible promises.

16. Faith, meaning and
alternatives to mechanism
When someone says the universe cannot be ‘just a machine’, they may
express very different objections. They may demand an explanation
for consciousness, defend freedom, assert a creator’s existence or re‐
ject the idea that a life’s value is exhausted by a physical description.
We should not answer all of them with the same argument.
Theism can conceive order as dependent on a God who creates and
sustains the world. Deism, schematically, emphasizes a creator and
the autonomous operation of the created order. Other positions identi‐
fy the divine with nature or attribute a more complex relationship to
it. These orientations do not in themselves make the same prediction
about experiments. A religious conception can be compatible with the
same regularities accepted by a naturalist.
The differences may be metaphysical and existential without an im‐
mediate measurable consequence. But if a doctrine asserts that a par‐
ticular physical event occurs under specified conditions, that compon‐
ent can be confronted with observation. Shielding an empirical claim
with a general conception’s prestige is an error, whether that prestige
is religious or scientific.
The problem of miracles shows the tension. If we define a miracle as
violating a true and absolutely universal law, we have a contradiction
by definition. If we define it as an unexpected event relative to known
laws, it remains open whether we have an error, unknown mechan‐
ism or intervention. Hume analyzes justification for belief in miracles
by weighing testimony against experience of regularities. Even those
rejecting his conclusions can retain the methodological question: why
should this account weigh more than alternative explanations?
(Hume, 1748, sec. X.)
An advocate of a created world can legitimately ask why order exists.
A naturalist may reply that introducing a creator moves the question:
why does that creator exist and have those powers? Nor does ‘the laws
48


simply exist’ eliminate explanation’s stopping point. The dispute also
concerns what kind of endpoint is acceptable, not merely whether
current knowledge has a gap.
Panpsychism, broadly, assigns fundamental status to experiential as‐
pects or precursors of experience. It does not necessarily imply that
stones think like humans or that our wishes alter instruments. It may
try to answer a question about consciousness’s nature while remain‐
ing compatible with the same physical predictions. But that compatib‐
ility is not experimental confirmation of the thesis (Goff, Seager și Al‐
len-Hermanson, 2022).
Holism may demand that relationships and organization not be elim‐
inated from explanation. This demand is sometimes indispensable. It
becomes weak when it refuses to specify what difference the whole
would make and how it could be investigated. ‘Everything is connec‐
ted’ says too little: how, how strongly, at what scale and with what
consequences?
Another alternative is process philosophy: what we call objects can be
understood through the stability of processes rather than as sub‐
stances wholly independent of change. We need not declare this ori‐
entation the final truth to use its question. A whirlpool persists
through matter’s movement; an institution persists through activities
and rules. When stability is produced, explaining it requires more
than identifying a substrate (Seibt, 2022).
In Beyond Good and Evil, §22, Nietzsche attacks how ‘nature’s lawful‐
ness’ is presented as an uninterpreted text of the world. His challenge
is that the concepts through which we read nature may carry prefer‐
ences and values. We need not accept every consequence of his philo‐
sophy to hear the warning: even the ideal of a completely regular
world may have a cultural history (Nietzsche, 1886).
But this genealogy does not make every interpretation equal either.
Two vocabularies may be culturally laden, yet one may enable far bet‐
ter predictions and interventions. Acknowledging interpretation does
not mean abandoning reality’s resistance. The strongest critique pre‐
serves both levels.
49


Instead of automatic contempt or admiration, we ask what problem
the alternative solves, what additional assumptions it makes and what
would count against it. Some ideas will remain metaphysical; others
will suggest experiments or disappear when precision is demanded.
Perhaps the world has a meaning physics does not formulate. Perhaps
meaning is something beings construct in the world. Neither possibil‐
ity entitles us to falsify an experimental result. Respect for great ques‐
tions begins by not using answers too large for evidence too small.

IV · WHO BENEFITS WHEN NATURE SEEMS TO HAVE DECIDED
17. Who speaks in nature’s name
‘It is not my decision. That is how the world works.’ Few formulas can
move responsibility so quickly. Sometimes the speaker correctly
warns us of a constraint. At other times they try to turn a contestable
choice into an inevitability.
There is no single class of people benefiting from the concept of a law
of nature. A reproducible result can protect a vulnerable person from
arbitrariness and give a powerful organization a new instrument of
control. The same physical principle can make a device safer or a
weapon more effective. The distribution of benefits depends on ac‐
cess, aims and institutions, not merely the law’s truth.
The first gain is reduced dependence on the authority’s person. If we
can show how a result is obtained and under what conditions, com‐
petence can be transmitted. A student need not inherit a teacher’s
prestige to verify a calculation. This is the ideal. Realizing it neverthe‐
less requires instruments, time, education and freedom to contradict,
all unequally distributed.
The sociology of science shows why declared universality of criteria
does not automatically eliminate hierarchies of recognition. Merton
analyzed the ‘Matthew effect’: contributions associated with already
prestigious researchers may receive disproportionate credit, while
similar contributions from lesser-known researchers remain less vis‐
ible. His analysis concerns recognition mechanisms, not proof that sci‐
entific results are merely products of reputation (Merton, 1968).
The distinction is crucial. The social origin of trust does not automatic‐
ally establish the truth or falsity of its object. It may explain why a cor‐
rect idea is ignored or a weak one receives more attention. Evaluation
also requires content, replication and independent results.
Nosek, Spies and Motyl discuss the tension between incentives for
publishable results and incentives for accuracy. Favoring novelty and
positive results can affect analytical choices and the visibility of negat‐
ive findings. Their proposals concern research practices and rewards.
51


Here we see that trust in laws is supported not only by intelligent
minds but also by institutions making correction advantageous
(Nosek, Spies și Motyl, 2012).
The second gain is anticipatory power. Whoever knows a regularity
before others can design, prevent or exploit. This asymmetry is not in‐
herently illegitimate: competence is valuable. It becomes problematic
when affected people cannot see assumptions, contest errors or nego‐
tiate consequences.
Imagine a model estimating a person’s probability of dropping out of
a training program. The organization uses it to deny access to those
deemed ‘risks’. The denial may prevent precisely the experience that
would have changed the outcome. Prediction becomes part of the
mechanism. We no longer merely observe a relationship: we use it to
modify the world in which it was measured. The example is hypothet‐
ical, but shows why a social regularity cannot automatically be
treated as a planetary orbit.
The third gain is legitimacy. When a hierarchy is called natural, its op‐
ponent seems to fight reality rather than an institution. We must de‐
compose the claim: there is an observed difference; there is an ex‐
planation of causes; there is a judgment about what should be done.
The first alone does not yield the second, and the first two do not auto‐
matically yield the third.
Saying a result has a biological component does not establish what
moral treatment the person deserves. Saying a social measure has
costs does not establish that it is unjustified. Saying an ideal is good
does not prove the proposed mechanism will realize it. Rigor demands
that facts, causes and values remain distinct, then enter explicit dia‐
logue.
Who loses when the notion is misused? The person turned into a stat‐
istical average, the community told change is impossible and the re‐
searcher whose results do not fit the dominant story. Who loses when
we reject the notion altogether? Those who depend on verifiable
standards for safety, fair access and challenging arbitrariness.
The alternative is not between obedience to experts and distrust of all
expertise. It is an organization of trust in which competence has au‐
thority within its domain while decision-making power remains ac‐
52


countable for values, distribution and errors. Nature does not vote on
the budget or decide who deserves to suffer. Humans make these
choices, sometimes using knowledge of nature, sometimes hiding be‐
hind it.

18. Profit, merit and the
supposed laws of the market
A company cannot produce material goods without resources and en‐
ergy. But it does not follow that a particular profit distribution is a
physical law. Between material constraint and economic outcome lie
rights, contracts, technologies, information and institutional choices.
An economic model can be highly useful without the status of a uni‐
versal law. It can show what consequences follow from particular
preferences, rules and constraints. The problem arises when premises
disappear from the presentation and the conclusion is promoted to
humanity’s eternal nature.
Construct a simple example. Two people divide ten resource units. If
one receives seven, three remain for the other. This is an arithmetic
constraint. But arithmetic did not decide who receives seven. There
may be a contract, an auction, an urgent need, a prior contribution or
a threat. Invoking the fixed sum does not justify the distribution rule.
Moreover, the total resource can sometimes be changed through co‐
operation, investment or technology, and sometimes cannot within
the relevant horizon. Thus, the opposition between ‘everything is
zero-sum’ and ‘we can always increase the total’ is too crude. We need
a model of the concrete constraint, costs and time, not optimism or
pessimism turned into law.
The economic benefit of knowing physical laws can be analyzed
without mythology: it allows anticipation of material responses,
design and reduction of some errors. But a process’s predictability
does not determine who owns its result. Technological advantage and
the right to exploit it are distinct levels. An invention may increase
total well-being yet produce losses for particular people.
Suppose automation reduces the time needed for a product. No single
social order follows from this change. Prices may fall, profits may rise,
working hours may decrease or different combinations may emerge.
54


Competitive conditions, negotiation and rules matter. Saying ‘techno‐
logy decided’ removes from the story the agents who chose the effects’
distribution.
Some regularities change precisely because people react to them. If an
institution rewards the number of documents produced, people may
produce more documents without improving the activity for which
documents were an indicator. In our hypothetical model, the target
changed behavior; the old correlation between indicator and quality
is no longer guaranteed. This is a general reason to test relationship
stability after intervention, not a claim that every evaluation is use‐
less.
Merit is equally subtle. Success can include competence and effort, but
these are not the only possible explanations of a difference. Initial ac‐
cess, the order of encounters and others’ reactions can alter the tra‐
jectory. Before proclaiming a ‘law of talent’, we must ask which altern‐
atives were eliminated and how talent was measured.
Salganik, Dodds and Watts’s cultural experiment involved over 14,000
participants who could listen to and download unfamiliar songs.
Knowing others’ previous choices increased inequality and unpredict‐
ability of success across experimental worlds. Quality, operationalized
through participants’ independent choices, did not become irrelevant;
but it did not determine the ranking alone. The result has the limits of
an artificial market and is not a complete theory of merit (Salganik,
Dodds și Watts, 2006).
The implication for our discussion is that an outcome can be produced
by stable rules yet not reflect a unique, inevitable hierarchy. The same
interaction types can amplify early differences. We need not choose
between ‘everything is merit’ and ‘everything is luck’. We must identi‐
fy contributions, interactions and possibilities for change.
Who benefits from the rhetoric of a perfectly natural market? An act‐
or wanting their advantage no longer discussed may benefit. Who be‐
nefits from denying all economic regularities? An actor promising res‐
ults without acknowledging required resources and incentives may
benefit. Both can be ways of evading responsibility.
55


Mature economic language says: under these conditions, this mechan‐
ism tends to produce that result; if conditions change, we must ree‐
valuate the prediction. This is not a weakness. It is the difference
between a tool for choice and an oracle claiming choices have already
been made by nature.

19. Art between constraint and
invention
A violin cannot produce every imaginable sound through any gesture.
A sculptural bridge must bear its own weight. A painter works with
light, pigments, surfaces and a visual system. Art is not a territory out‐
side nature. But these constraints do not yield a single legitimate style
or an eternal canon of beauty.
Three levels must be separated here: physical possibility, perceptual
effect and cultural value. Physics can help us understand sound pro‐
duction. Psychology can investigate what particular people distin‐
guish or prefer under particular conditions. Art criticism and history
discuss meaning and value in context. No level can automatically re‐
place another.
A numerical ratio between frequencies is a measurable property. The
claim that the ratio suits every music, listener and purpose is much
stronger. Even if we found a robust perceptual preference, it would
not follow that art ought to maximize it. The artist may pursue un‐
ease, ambiguity, tension or surprise, not merely comfort.
The same happens with visual symmetry. It can be an organizing tool.
Asymmetry can express movement or conflict. Our ability to recog‐
nize a pattern does not oblige every work to reproduce it. A constraint
is a condition of the game; an aesthetic norm is a choice about how it
is worth playing.
Claims about universal proportions supposedly explaining beauty on
their own also deserve caution. Retrospectively finding a ratio in a
sufficiently complex object is easy if we can freely choose measure‐
ment points. A test would require measurements fixed beforehand,
comparisons with alternatives and an effect criterion. Without these,
mathematics can ornament an impression rather than explain it.
The concept of law also influences art through order’s prestige. An
artist may seek the world’s essence in geometric forms, repetition or
rule variations. Another may think precisely the rule’s rupture makes
57


experience visible. We need not choose either intention as nature’s
truth. We can judge what the work accomplishes and what question it
forces us to formulate.
Generative art offers an especially clear conceptual laboratory. We fix
a few rules and obtain a family of results. From this family we select
some works. What part of the value comes from the rule, what part
from the initial state and what part from selection? If we show only
successful results, the audience may attribute to the algorithm a
power the complete process does not possess to the same extent.
This is not an accusation against algorithmic generation. Artists work‐
ing manually also select, revise and abandon. The question is what we
present as the creative mechanism. ‘A simple rule produced this im‐
age’ can be true and incomplete if thousands of failed attempts pre‐
ceded the choice. The full explanation also includes the filter.
This observation returns us to emergence. A complex form may arise
from simple rules, but not every complexity is meaningful to an ob‐
server. Meaning may depend on experience, conventions and inten‐
tions. Explaining the pixel distribution does not mean explaining why
someone sees loss or promise in it.
Who benefits when an aesthetic norm is called ‘natural’? Those who
master it and institutions using it as a filter may gain legitimacy. Who
loses? Forms of expression evaluated by a criterion concealing its con‐
textual character. But pluralism does not require asserting every ob‐
ject is equally well made either. We can assess coherence, difficulty,
precision and effect without pretending our preference is written in
the atom’s structure.
Perhaps the most interesting link between art and laws of nature is
not that art illustrates them. It is that art teaches us to notice how
many worlds of meaning can be built without changing basic con‐
straints. The same sounds can belong to different songs. The same ma‐
terials can become shelter or monument. Freedom does not necessar‐
ily begin where rules disappear; sometimes it begins where we under‐
stand what a rule permits without commanding.

V · THE UNIVERSE AS A PROGRAM: POSSIBILITY, EVIDENCE, RESPONS‐
IBILITY
20. The simulation hypothesis
without cinema
If we learned the universe is simulated, what exactly would we have
learned? That matter has a different substrate from the one imagined?
That authors exist? That they can intervene? That the world’s purpose
is an experiment? These conclusions are often packaged together al‐
though the first does not prove all the others.
First, three ideas must be separated. The world can be described com‐
putationally. The world can have fundamental dynamics resembling
computation. The world can be implemented in another system as a
simulation made for particular purposes. Using computers to describe
gravity does not prove gravity is produced by an external computer.
Nor is opposition between emergence and simulation mandatory.
Emergent effective laws can arise within a simulation. A nonsimu‐
lated system could have simple discrete rules. A simulator could im‐
plement continuous or probabilistic processes through methods un‐
like our video games. The two questions differ: how do a level’s regu‐
larities arise, and on what substrate is that level realized?
Nick Bostrom’s argument is more precise than the popular formula
‘we probably live in a game’. In a conditional scheme, it links three
possibilities: civilizations like ours rarely reach the relevant capabil‐
ity; those reaching it produce very few ancestor simulations; or simu‐
lated observers of the relevant type greatly outnumber nonsimulated
ones. Moving to a belief about ourselves nevertheless requires as‐
sumptions about consciousness, resources, civilizations’ behavior and
the observer class from which we consider ourselves a sample
(Bostrom, 2003).
It does not follow that the three alternatives have equal probabilities.
A disjunction containing three statements does not allocate one third
of reality to each. Nor does technical ability to simulate a process
59


prove that the simulated process has conscious experience. These dif‐
ficulties are part of evaluating the argument, not details to leave until
after the conclusion.
We can construct an arithmetic example without claiming it describes
the cosmos. If there are a thousand nonsimulated observers and a mil‐
lion simulated observers of a comparable type, a uniform sample
from their union will almost always be simulated. But why should our
situation be equivalent to that uniform sample? How do we define
‘comparable’? Do we count people, moments of experience, identical
copies or worlds? The arithmetic is simple; justifying the sampling
scheme is not.
Another vocabulary error appears. A simulation need not be ‘unreal’
to its inhabitants. Chalmers argues that a Matrix-type hypothesis can
change the world’s metaphysics without making all ordinary judg‐
ments about its objects and relations false. If an object has stability
and causal roles in our world, discovering a computational substrate
may change what we believe it fundamentally is, not the fact that we
can bump into it (Chalmers, 2003/2005).
Nevertheless, simulating an environment for an external brain must
be distinguished from minds themselves implemented within a simu‐
lation. The first presupposes a mind on another substrate; the second
presupposes that simulated organization can sustain experience. Ar‐
guments about consciousness cannot be resolved simply by counting
characters generated in a program.
Intervention is not mandatory either. A program can be left to evolve,
receive inputs or be modified. Its creator may have complete control,
limited control or no longer exist. Deducing ‘constantly supervised by
an omnipotent intelligence’ from ‘implemented’ imports a theology
without argument.
What do we gain intellectually from the hypothesis? It forces us to dis‐
tinguish internal law from implementation mechanism, prediction
from ontology and experience from substrate. What do we risk? Turn‐
ing every surprise into a message and every absence of evidence into
evidence of simulation’s perfection. In the latter case, the hypothesis
becomes immune to experience.
60


The serious question is therefore not ‘can it be imagined?’ It can. The
question is which precise version we choose, what it adds to an in‐
ternal physical description and which observations should differ.
Without this step, we have a possible metaphysics. With it, some vari‐
ants can become research programs.

21. What would count as
evidence?
Two observers find a perfect square in a particle distribution. The first
says ‘the simulation grid’; the second, ‘the structure of space’. The ob‐
servation is the same. What must be separated is not two exclama‐
tions, but models with different predictions.
This is the central difficulty. ‘We are in a simulation’ designates an
enormous family of possibilities, not a unique mechanism. A simula‐
tion may approximate some processes or reproduce them exactly at
the level accessible to observers. It may have a regular lattice, an ad‐
aptive representation or an implementation unlike a video game. That
we use particular architectures does not oblige every possible simulat‐
or to use them.
Let us accept the generous condition that the simulator does not act‐
ively try to hide the implementation. Even then detection is not guar‐
anteed. Lack of camouflage does not automatically produce an in‐
formation leak. Two different mechanisms can have the same observ‐
able consequences, just as two programs can compute exactly the
same function. If every accessible experiment has the same outcome
distributions in the two models, no statistical ingenuity can create an
absent difference. This is an identification limit, not a limit of the re‐
searcher’s courage.
An example of a more precise hypothesis was explored by Beane, Dav‐
oudi and Savage. They analyzed consequences of implementation on a
cubic spacetime lattice under a particular Wilson-type discretization
scheme. Such a construction can privilege certain directions and af‐
fect very energetic particles. The paper discusses constraints and pos‐
sible signatures of that model; it offers no universal test for every sim‐
ulated universe (Beane, Davoudi și Savage, 2014).
The distinction is decisive. If we found anisotropy, a difference
between directions, we would have a physical structure to explain. It
could come from the proposed implementation model, a nonsimu‐
lated fundamental structure, particle sources, propagation or instru‐
62


ments. A positive result would not arrive labeled ‘external author’. A
negative result would constrain certain implementations, not exclude
every imaginable simulation.
Nor are quantum phenomena, by themselves, evidence that the world
is computed only when we look. In physics, measurement involves
physical interactions, not necessarily a human mind. Decoherence de‐
scribes the loss of local access to interference through a system’s inter‐
action with the environment and explains important aspects of clas‐
sical behavior’s emergence. It does not alone resolve every dispute
over quantum mechanics’ interpretation. But neither does it require a
conscious spectator activating reality’s ‘rendering’ (Zurek, 2003).
Indeed, a test attempting to discover whether the world computes de‐
tails only on demand faces a problem: the very device intended to re‐
cord the detail becomes a relevant interaction. We must specify what
‘on demand’ means in the simulation model and what different out‐
come distribution it produces. The analogy with saving resources in a
game is not yet a physical prediction.
We could seek rounding errors, generator periodicity or resolution
limits. But every proposal requires a sufficiently precise architecture.
A finite sequence cannot alone prove that its source is fundamentally
random; it can be reproduced by a program containing it. Conversely,
unexpected regularity in a sequence does not prove a programmer’s
existence. It may indicate an ununderstood physical process, data se‐
lection or an instrument defect.
More promising, at least logically, would be an anomaly that not only
surprises but anticipates. An implementation model could predict a
family of correlated deviations in advance: a particular energy de‐
pendence, orientation and ratio between quantities. If these relation‐
ships appeared in independent data, the model would gain support. It
would still need comparison with physical explanations producing the
same family of effects.
There is also the message scenario. Suppose a repeatable signal ap‐
pears that responds to new challenges through controlled physical
channels. After ordinary explanations were excluded, it would be an
extraordinary discovery of a source capable of intelligent interaction.
63


But ‘an intelligence communicates’ and ‘this intelligence is outside our
universe’ are different conclusions. External origin must be supported
separately, not extracted from spectacular content.
A project can be valuable without finding a simulation: it improves
clocks, characterizes noise or tests symmetries. A weak project
changes its explanation after every result and leaves nothing verifi‐
able. Research productivity matters, not the amazement produced.
Some implementations might leave traces; others would remain un‐
detectable. Research begins when the proposed mechanism precisely
indicates what we should observe differently.

22. A protocol for a suspicious
world
Return to the prologue’s clocks. The ratio between two frequencies
starts changing. One team announces an error. Another, an unknown
physical field. A third, intervention in the simulation. How do we or‐
ganize research so that more than the story with the best headline can
win?
First we specify the phenomenon. Not ‘time runs differently’, but a
particular dimensionless ratio between two transition types, meas‐
ured through an identifiable protocol, deviates from a model by a cer‐
tain magnitude and temporal structure. Choosing the ratio prevents a
mere unit change from being confused with changed physics. Re‐
search on varying constants uses precisely comparisons where physic‐
al dependencies and model assumptions are explicit (Uzan, 2011).
Then we formulate sufficiently concrete competing explanations. One
model describes thermal drift in the instrument. Another, a synchron‐
ization-chain problem. A third introduces a field affecting the two
mechanisms differently. A fourth proposes a discrete modification of
an implementation rule. The list need not be complete; we must not
treat our preferred model as the sole alternative to standard theory.
For each explanation we ask what else should change. Does the effect
appear in every instrument or only one batch? Does it depend on tem‐
perature, position, material or software version? Does it have the
same form in laboratories sharing neither instruments nor code? In‐
dependence means more than multiple addresses. Ten teams using
the same faulty library can perfectly reproduce the same mistake.
Predictions must be recorded before choosing the data they will eval‐
uate. If we examine thousands of frequencies, periods and directions
and report only the unusual combination, chance can acquire the ap‐
pearance of law. We separate exploration from confirmation: find a
pattern in one observation set, then put it at risk on new data. We also
65


retain negative results. Such practices address a general research
problem: incentives for publishable results do not automatically coin‐
cide with incentives for true results (Nosek, Spies și Motyl, 2012).
The intervention model must also pay the price of precision. ‘Someone
changes anything, anytime, however they want’ offers no determinate
probability of observations. A table of exceptions invented after the
result can reproduce any history. By contrast, a hypothesis specifying
a particular class of modifications and predicting as-yet unmeasured
relationships can be compared with rivals. It need not claim to ex‐
plain everything to deserve testing.
We can build a small research program without waiting for a cosmic
anomaly. The project proposed here would begin with artificial
worlds whose mechanisms are known only to the evaluator. We do
not claim this experiment has been conducted. Its purpose would be
to test whether our methods distinguish possible causes of a change
before applying them to the universe.
In the first family, rules remain fixed but the observer sees only col‐
lective variables. In the second, a regime change brings other effective
relationships into view. In the third, an internal parameter evolves
under a stable rule. In the fourth, the evaluator modifies a rule ex‐
ternally. In the fifth, the world does not change at all: the sensor or
processing software fails. The observer receives the same interface
types and a comparable experimental budget.
The difficulty must be constructed correctly. If every external inter‐
vention is abrupt and every internal process smooth, an algorithm
will learn the distinction we artificially chose. Internal jumps, slow ex‐
ternal interventions and errors resembling both must be included.
The mechanism’s true label must not be guessable from a data-gener‐
ator artifact.
Evaluation occurs on new worlds, not merely new rows from the
same worlds. Otherwise the method may memorize a simulator’s pe‐
culiarities and seem to discover general principles. We compare the
detector with simple methods and measure false alarms, detection
delay and the proportion of cases where it justifiably refuses to de‐
cide. Saying ‘these two explanations cannot be separated by the avail‐
able data’ may be an epistemic success, not a failure.
66


We deliberately introduce pairs of observationally equivalent worlds
as well. In one, a hidden variable changes the dynamics; in the other,
the evaluator externally applies exactly the same change. If interfaces
produce the same observations under every permitted intervention,
no detector should reliably distinguish them. A system announcing
certainty in such a situation has not overcome the theoretical limit.
Most likely it exploited an information leak or evaluation error.
This laboratory would produce useful results even if it showed how
little we can identify. We would learn which new experiments reduce
ambiguity, which conclusions depend on hidden-variable access and
when classification must be replaced with a set of compatible explan‐
ations. In an AI program for scientific discovery, the ability to declare
underdetermination should be evaluated alongside the ability to find
formulas.
For real observations, the next step would be a public register of pre‐
dictions, protocols and data, with independent checks and explicit
stopping rules. A campaign’s result must be formulated at the level it
supports: ‘this model class is incompatible with the data in this do‐
main’, ‘there is an unreproduced deviation’, or ‘the new model pre‐
dicts these observations better’. None of these statements should auto‐
matically become ‘we found the programmer’.
There is also a mundane but important ethical limit: no simulation hy‐
pothesis justifies causing disasters to force the system to ‘react’. Exper‐
iments must meet the same safety requirements as other research. A
method claiming to test reality by sacrificing people already starts
from a moral conclusion the data cannot authorize.
The program is useful because it shifts emphasis from fascination to
discrimination. The question is no longer whether we can tell a plaus‐
ible story after seeing a change. It is whether we can anticipate which
observation would distinguish two mechanisms and honestly acknow‐
ledge cases where no accessible observation distinguishes them.

23. If the rules are rewritten
tomorrow
In a novel, an author can change the world’s rule from one page to the
next. An inhabitant of the novel has no access to the manuscript. For
them, the question is not only what changed but whether change can
still be described through something stable. This is a thought experi‐
ment about access to explanation, not proof that the world has an au‐
thor.
At least three intervention types must be separated. Someone can
modify the system’s state without modifying evolution rules: move a
particle, then let it evolve. They can modify a parameter that seemed
constant in the internal description. Or they can replace the rule link‐
ing the present state to the next. The three operations have different
consequences, and observers may struggle to identify them from a fi‐
nite outcome sequence.
Take a simple game. A ball abruptly changes position, then continues
under the same dynamics. This resembles a state intervention. If all
balls begin responding differently to the same force, we suspect a
parameter or mechanism change. If, under certain circumstances, the
dependency itself takes another form, we suspect a rule change. But a
richer model could describe all these phenomena through fixed rules
and hidden variables.
Thus, ‘a fundamental law changed’ is more ambitious than ‘our con‐
stant-parameter model failed’. The second conclusion can be experi‐
mentally supported. The first requires knowing that no more general
level exists where the change follows a stable rule. Finite experience
does not usually provide such a guarantee.
But what if the simulator stops everything for an hour and then re‐
sumes? If precisely all accessible processes are suspended, including
clocks, memory and every external interaction, that external hour
leaves no internal trace. We would not feel a wait, because feeling re‐
quires an unfolding. The situation would change if one channel con‐
68


tinued functioning and could be compared with suspended ones. De‐
tectability depends on a difference preserved in observations, not a
duration imagined from outside.
The same argument applies to uniform slowing of the entire simu‐
lated world relative to implementation time. Internally, there is not
necessarily an independent clock to measure the slowdown. ‘The uni‐
verse runs more slowly’ may designate a relationship between two
levels without designating an observable change at ours. By contrast,
a slowdown affecting processes differently could leave measurable ra‐
tios.
Could we exclude simulation through its computational cost? Only
conditionally. Physical limits to computation link processing to re‐
sources such as energy, time and degrees of freedom within the as‐
sumed physics (Lloyd, 2000). Transferring these limits to an external
simulator requires justifying why its implementation would obey the
same physics and possess the assumed resources. Invoking an extern‐
al universe with whatever capability we want does not prove simula‐
tion; but neither does an internal limit automatically exclude every
imaginable external world.
Then comes the metalaw problem. If our world’s rules are changed by
a program, the program operates under other rules. If those are
changed by an intelligence, that intelligence and its acts presuppose
some mechanism or structure of possibilities. Explanation through
simulation can move ‘why these laws?’ to another level. It need not re‐
solve it all the way down.
Nor does an omnipotent-author hypothesis eliminate every question.
We can ask what its power means, whether it acts for reasons, wheth‐
er those reasons offer predictions and why such a capability exists.
Some theological answers consider this a foundation no longer requir‐
ing the same kind of explanation. Other philosophies reject the stop‐
ping point. We have no simple experiment arbitrating among all these
forms of ultimate explanation.
The practical consequence is not abandoning knowledge. We already
live using models with domains and conditions. If we discover a
change, we seek what remains stable: more general relationships, pre‐
69


dictable intervals, verification procedures. A world in which some
regularities change is not identical to one in which every statement
has the same value.
The moral consequence requires still more attention. If simulated
people have conscious experiences, being implemented does not make
their suffering nonexistent. This is an ethical conditional: it does not
claim every program is conscious. It says that where experience ex‐
ists, we cannot morally erase it by renaming its substrate. A creator’s
power to stop a system does not, by that fact alone, confer a moral
right to do anything to its inhabitants.
Who would benefit from the contrary interpretation? An authority
able to declare others’ lives mere dispensable elements of a scenario.
Who would lose? Precisely those whose experience is ignored. The
risk is not unique to simulation: the same operation occurs whenever
a metaphysics is used to diminish beings’ moral status without an ar‐
gument about their interests and experiences.
In a world with uncertain ontology, we can prefer robust decisions:
reducing avoidable suffering, preserving the ability to correct mis‐
takes, protecting research and avoiding catastrophic wagers on fragile
hypotheses. These priorities are arguable normative proposals, not
new laws of nature. Precisely for that reason, they must be owned as
responsible choices.
If tomorrow we discover that rules can be rewritten, the difference
between truth and invention does not disappear. What we must dis‐
cover changes. And the most urgent question may not be ‘who owns
the code?’ but ‘which relationships can we understand well enough to
protect one another?’

24. How to live without the final
explanation
We can now answer the opening question without pretending to close
it. Are laws of nature real or imagined? Statements, equations and
classifications are human constructions. In many tested situations, the
constraints they try to describe do not seem to depend on their formu‐
lator’s desire. The existence of additional metaphysical necessity re‐
mains disputed. These three answers can be held simultaneously be‐
cause they answer three different questions.
We need not choose between two caricatures: a universe with a per‐
fect legal code on its walls and a mind arbitrarily inventing every or‐
der. There is a world that resists and beings that select, compress and
interpret that resistance. Knowing means making selections that re‐
main useful when the world has an opportunity to contradict us.
What did we gain by calling stable relationships ‘laws’? The ability to
coordinate experience across generations, build instruments and de‐
mand more from an explanation than the speaker’s prestige. What did
we risk? Confusing an approximation with destiny, a local regularity
with universal necessity and a power relationship with existence’s in‐
evitable order.
This dual power is no reason to abandon the concept. It is a reason to
use it with an identification sheet. What does it describe? Under what
conditions? With what precision? What observation would challenge
it? Who can verify? Who bears the error? The last two questions do
not replace physics, but prevent its use as an excuse for unacknow‐
ledged allocation of risks.
On emergence, the conclusion is also dual. Stable basic rules can pro‐
duce new collective relationships and indispensable descriptive
levels. It does not follow that every novelty requires breaking physics.
But neither does a list of components automatically provide a useful
explanation of the whole. Sometimes we must discover suitable vari‐
ables before recognizing the law.
71


On mind, it would be wrong to confuse lack of an intuitive image with
lack of understanding. We can understand through relationships, con‐
sequences and limits, supported by mathematics, instruments and co‐
operation. It would be equally wrong to confuse a prediction’s success
with possession of the final explanation. Mature understanding also
knows what it has not obtained.
On simulation, the fruitful question remains conditional. Some imple‐
mentations might leave traces; others might be observationally indis‐
tinct from nonsimulated explanations. Absence of intent to camou‐
flage does not negate the second possibility. Research begins when we
choose a precise mechanism class and explain what differences it
could produce, not when we declare every anomaly confirms our
story.
On morality, neither natural selection, the market nor a cosmic pro‐
gram decides by itself what we owe others. A description of mechan‐
isms helps choose means, anticipate effects and avoid impossible
promises. Aims require arguments about values, interests and rela‐
tionships. Refusing to confuse the two does not weaken science; it asks
that science not be used in place of deliberation it cannot replace.
My position is that we need more realism about constraints and less
certainty about their ultimate interpretation. Realism prevents us
from treating every desire as a solution. Caution prevents us from
treating every temporary solution as the universe’s final ruling.
Between them lies room for intellectual courage, not merely modesty.
Let us return one last time to the imaginary laboratory. The clocks still
disagree. After months of work, perhaps we discover a trivial error.
Perhaps we find a physical phenomenon. Perhaps the relationship
between observations and explanations changes in a way we cannot
yet name. In every variant, the good researcher has gained some‐
thing: more precise knowledge of what happens and what they are
not yet entitled to claim.
The world need not read equations for some equations to describe it
well. We must carefully read the conditions under which we use them.
And when someone says ‘nature decided’, perhaps the most human
question is this: what exactly have we discovered about the world,
and what exactly are we trying to stop discussing among ourselves?

TOOLS · FOR READERS WHO WANT TO CHECK
A. Four tools for not confusing
story with law
1. Simplicity of the rule is not simplicity of the world
A complete computational explanation must specify at least the rule,
initial state and how the result relates to observation. A program of a
few characters displaying a huge file does not explain that file through
a few characters: the information has been stored elsewhere. This ele‐
mentary example shows why ‘can a few rules produce the universe?’
must be accompanied by ‘what have we already put into the initial
conditions?’
We can compare two explanations of the same sequence. The first
says ‘read the next entry in this table’, and the table contains all res‐
ults. The second offers a short rule and short initial state from which
results can be generated. Both reproduce the past; the second com‐
presses information more. Yet past compression alone does not guar‐
antee future prediction. We need testing on data unused in choosing
the rule.
The same caution applies to emergence. If we already hide in the gen‐
erator the complex structure we wish to explain, we have not ex‐
plained its appearance. If a robust structure appears across many ini‐
tial conditions and a class of mechanisms, we have better reason to
speak of an emergent regularity. Robustness must be measured, not
suggested by a spectacular animation.
2. A provable limit to simulation detection
Let S denote a precise simulated-world model and N a precise compet‐
ing model we call nonsimulated. D is an observable event and E an ex‐
periment accessible to the observer. Assume the models give the same
observation distribution: for every permitted experiment and every
event D, they assign the same probability:
P(D | S, E) = P(D | N, E).
73


For any event with nonzero probability, the likelihood ratio is then 1.
Those data favor neither model over the other. If we begin with differ‐
ent preferences, they may remain different, but this experiment has
not made them better supported.
Adaptive experimental choice does not help either if equality holds
after every possible observation history. The probability of an out‐
come sequence is obtained by multiplying successive conditional
probabilities. If every factor agrees between models, so does the
product. The adaptive strategy therefore produces the same distribu‐
tion of observable histories. This is an elementary proof of indistin‐
guishability under the stated conditions, not a claim that every simu‐
lation is undetectable.
The way out is not a miraculous statistical method. We must find an
experiment for which equality’s premises no longer hold, acquire a
new channel or restrict the model class. Creative research can begin
here. The clear limit shows where the difference must be sought.
3. Why a spectacular result does not eliminate
alternatives
Invent two models, A and B. Before a test, assign A a probability of 1%
and B the remaining 99%. Assume, strictly for this example, no other
possibilities exist. An outcome D is ten times more probable under A
than B. The initial A:B odds were 1:99. After the outcome they become
10:99, so A’s probability reaches approximately 9.2%, not certainty.
The calculation does not establish what probability should be as‐
signed to simulation. The numbers are chosen only to show the differ‐
ence between ‘the data favor a model’ and ‘the model is now almost
certain’. A real problem requires justifying both observation probabil‐
ities under models and treatment of alternatives. A vague model that
does not say how probable D is cannot receive a numerical advantage
merely because the outcome can be narrated in its vocabulary.
If the observer searched hundreds of possible outcomes and chose the
one favoring A, even the ten-to-one ratio cannot be accepted without
examining selection. Data must be analyzed alongside the procedure
by which they were sought and selected. Arithmetic precision does
not repair a poorly defined comparison.
74


4. The profile of a purported law
A useful profile begins with the exact statement and domain: which
objects, scales and conditions? It continues with observable quantit‐
ies, units and measurement procedure. It includes error tolerance,
auxiliary assumptions and the difference between an exact relation‐
ship in the model and an approximation in application. These few de‐
tails are worth more than an unexplained adjective ‘universal’.
The second part records support: what was measured, derived or
merely assumed? Which new prediction succeeded? Which laboratory
or independent method verified it? What result would seriously weak‐
en confidence? When such a result appears, we explain why we revise
the law, conditions or instrument; we do not automatically choose
whichever option preserves the author’s authority.
The third part examines transfer and social power. Was the relation‐
ship tested in the context where it is now used? Is it a physical con‐
straint or an institution-dependent regularity? Who gains resources,
prestige or impunity through this formulation? Who bears an error’s
cost? These questions do not decide whether the law is true, but clari‐
fy why it deserves independent testing.
The final field separates descriptive from normative conclusions. ‘Un‐
der conditions C, behavior X produces outcome Y’ does not mean ‘we
must preserve C’ or ‘Y is good’. A law may show how an effect is ob‐
tained; choosing the effect and allocating costs require additional ar‐
guments. This is the minimal discipline by which law remains a tool of
knowledge rather than a command to stop thinking.

B. Glossary: words that change
the question
Empirical adequacy. A theory’s ability to correctly represent relevant
observable phenomena. It is not by definition identical to the com‐
plete truth of its ontology.
Anisotropy. Dependence of a property on direction. An observed an‐
isotropy requires explaining source, propagation and measurement
before attributing it to a fundamental structure.
Causality. The relationship through which some changes matter for
producing others. Philosophical definitions and identification meth‐
ods differ; mere correlation does not alone fix causal direction.
Initial condition. Description of the state from which we apply an
evolution rule. Information placed here must not be confused with in‐
formation explained by the rule.
Dimensionless constant. A quantity whose numerical value does not
depend on unit choice. It is particularly important when asking
whether physical relationships, rather than measurement conven‐
tions, have changed.
Counterfactual. A statement about what would happen in a situation
different from the actual one. Laws are often used for such questions,
not merely to summarize the past.
Decoherence. A process through which interaction with the environ‐
ment makes certain interference locally inaccessible. It does not in it‐
self presuppose a conscious observing mind.
Determinism. A model property in which the relevant state and rules
determine a unique evolution. It is not synonymous with practical
predictability.
Domain of validity. The set of conditions under which a relationship
can be used with the stated precision. An approximation can be excel‐
lent within its domain and inadequate outside it.
76


Weak emergence. The appearance of collective behaviors from basic
interactions without postulating additional fundamental powers. Nov‐
elty of description does not imply violation of the mechanism.
Strong emergence. A family of positions holding that a whole intro‐
duces properties or powers irreducible in an ontological sense. This is
a stronger thesis than computational difficulty.
Epistemology. The study of knowledge, justification and their limits.
‘How can we know?’ is not identical to ‘what exists?’
Deterministic chaos. Behavior in which sensitivity to initial condi‐
tions can severely limit predictions although the rule introduces no
fundamental randomness.
Identifiability. The ability to distinguish models or parameters
through accessible observations and interventions. More data of the
same type do not resolve every lack of identifiability.
Induction. The passage from observed cases to more general or fu‐
ture expectations. Its practical usefulness is not a deductive guarantee
of nature’s uniformity.
Effective law. A relationship formulated for particular scales and rel‐
evant variables that need not describe every detail of the underlying
mechanism.
Statistical law. A relationship about distributions, frequencies or col‐
lective values. A rare individual outcome can be compatible with the
law; specified probabilities matter.
Metalaw. A proposed rule for how other rules are established or mod‐
ified. Explanation at this level does not automatically close the ques‐
tion of foundations.
Model. A selective representation constructed for a purpose. It can
combine laws, approximations, conventions and assumptions about
conditions.
Nomological necessity. What is necessary relative to a world’s laws.
It must not be confused with logical necessity, where the alternative
would produce a contradiction.
77


Ontology. Inquiry into what exists and existence’s fundamental cat‐
egories. The same predictions can be compatible with different ontolo‐
gies.
Explanatory reductionism. The idea that one level’s explanation can
be obtained from a more basic level’s description. It differs from the
thesis that everything has a physical substrate.
Symmetry. A transformation leaving a model’s relevant structure un‐
changed. It is not reducible to a drawing’s visual symmetry.
State / rule. The state describes the system’s situation; the rule spe‐
cifies relationships or transformations. Changing state does not auto‐
matically change the rule.
Underdetermination. A situation in which available evidence is com‐
patible with multiple explanations. It may be temporary or persist for
every accessible observation.
Universality. In collective-system studies, the appearance of common
behaviors in different microscopic mechanisms under specified condi‐
tions. It does not mean every detail is irrelevant.
Likelihood. The probability of data under a specified model. It is not
the same as the model’s probability after observing the data.
