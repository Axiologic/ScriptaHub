The Life and Adventures of Robinson Crusoe

Daniel Defoe

This concise reading follows all twenty chapters of Defoe’s original novel. It preserves the narrator’s religious interpretation and the book’s historical language around slavery and colonial power without endorsing them.

1. Start in Life

Born in York, Crusoe longs for the sea despite his father’s warning that a settled middle life offers safety. He leaves without parental consent. Storms frighten him into promising to return home, but relief wears away his resolve. The chapter makes his restlessness, and his habit of disregarding warnings, the starting point of the voyage.

2. Slavery and Escape

A voyage toward Guinea ends with Crusoe captured by corsairs and held in slavery. He escapes by boat with the boy Xury, throwing a pursuing man overboard, and travels south along the African coast. Their improvised survival depends on water, food, navigation, and encounters whose risks Crusoe cannot fully judge.

3. Wrecked on a Desert Island

A Portuguese ship rescues Crusoe and Xury. Crusoe establishes a plantation in Brazil, then joins a voyage connected to the trade in enslaved Africans. A violent storm wrecks the ship. He alone reaches an island alive, exhausted, without shelter, and uncertain whether the remaining wreck can yield anything useful.

Read Chapter 3 in full

4. First Weeks on the Island

Crusoe repeatedly visits the wreck and moves tools, food, weapons, powder, and other supplies ashore. He chooses a defensible place to live and begins making shelter and storage by hand. His early work turns panic into an inventory of needs, while the sea and weather remind him that each trip to the ship may be his last.

5. Builds a House—The Journal

Crusoe records his days, beginning with the wreck of 1659. A cave, enclosing wall, furniture, and improvised implements slowly make the island habitable. His journal attends closely to labour, accidents, weather, and scarcity. He cannot simply reproduce the comforts of home: even ordinary objects require time, materials, and repeated trial.

6. Ill and Conscience-Stricken

Illness interrupts Crusoe’s practical routine and sharpens his fear and remorse. He reads the Bible recovered from the ship and interprets survival through providence and repentance. The chapter joins bodily vulnerability with religious self-examination. Recovery does not end his isolation, but it changes how he understands his position and his obligations.

7. Agricultural Experience

Exploring farther inland, Crusoe discovers new ground and tends grains that began from a few accidental seeds. He learns that planting, harvesting, and preserving food require seasons of patient work. The island becomes less mysterious as he studies its resources, yet food security remains a long-term task rather than an instant triumph.

8. Surveys His Position

Crusoe crosses the island, sights distant land, and considers what escape might require. He finds a seasonal retreat and continues planning the tools needed to turn grain into bread. Surveying the terrain changes his mental map, but the distant shore also intensifies the question of whether other people are near.

9. A Boat

Crusoe makes a canoe too large to move to the water, a costly lesson in planning beyond what one person can accomplish. He eventually makes a usable craft, but currents around the island nearly carry him away. The episode contrasts ingenious labour with the limits imposed by geography and by his own earlier calculations.

10. Tames Goats

Over years of routine, Crusoe cultivates grain, dries fruit, improves his boats, and domesticates goats. Herds give him a more dependable supply than hunting alone. He measures his changed circumstances against his first despair, repeatedly interpreting what he has gained through providence. His self-sufficiency is real but remains fragile.

11. Finds Print of Man’s Foot on the Sand

A single footprint overturns Crusoe’s confidence that the island is uninhabited. Fear drives him to re-examine the shore, strengthen defenses, and hide signs of his presence. He imagines threats before he knows their source. The chapter shows how one small piece of evidence can reorganize an entire life.

12. A Cave Retreat

Crusoe shelters part of his goat herd elsewhere and finds a hidden cave suitable for refuge and supplies. Signs of visitors deepen his anxiety, and he plans how to protect himself without knowing when danger will arrive. Preparation now takes as much of his attention as food production once did.

13. Wreck of a Spanish Ship

After many years on the island, Crusoe sees a ship wrecked offshore. He reaches it but finds no living person to bring home. Salvaged objects have practical value, while money recovered there seems nearly useless in isolation. The wreck renews his hope of contact and exposes the distance between a castaway’s needs and ordinary measures of wealth.

14. A Dream Realised

Crusoe’s long wish for company takes a violent form when he intervenes during a raid and saves a captive. He names the man Friday, begins teaching him words, and gains a companion. Their bond grows inside an unequal relationship shaped by Crusoe’s authority and by the colonial assumptions of the narrative.

15. Friday’s Education

Crusoe teaches Friday his language, food customs, and Christianity while learning more about the nearby mainland and its peoples. Friday’s knowledge changes what Crusoe thinks possible. Their conversations reveal both affection and hierarchy: Crusoe depends on Friday’s help but often interprets him through the beliefs he carried from Europe.

16. Rescue of Prisoners from Cannibals

The two men plan to reach the mainland, then confront another group on the shore. They free captives, including Friday’s father and a Spaniard, and hear of other stranded Europeans. The island community expands, but decisions about defense, provisions, and a possible passage home become more complicated.

Read Chapter 16 in full

17. Visit of Mutineers

Before a voyage to the mainland can begin, an English ship appears with a captain held by mutineers. Crusoe and his companions help the captain and use the island’s terrain to divide the rebels. Their immediate goal becomes recovering the ship. The arrival offers a path home, but only if they can outmaneuver armed opponents.

18. The Ship Recovered

Crusoe’s group works with the captain to regain control of the vessel. He weighs punishment, mercy, and the practical problem of men left on the island. After decades away, departure becomes real; he prepares supplies and instructions for those who remain. The island is no longer merely a prison but a place he has organized and altered.

19. Return to England

Back in Europe, Crusoe learns what became of his Brazilian property and settles accounts with people who had acted for him. His gains do not erase the years spent away. He travels overland with Friday through Spain and France, where winter, wolves, and an attack on their party make the journey home another episode of danger.

Read Chapter 19 in full

20. Fight between Friday and a Bear

Friday confronts a bear in the mountains, using speed and wit to draw it from the travelers before killing it. The narrator then closes with his finances, family, a return visit to the island, and a promise of further adventures. The final pages keep the work’s mixture of travel, property, violence, and restless departure in view.
