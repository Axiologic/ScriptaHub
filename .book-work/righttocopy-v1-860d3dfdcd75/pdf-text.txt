Prologue — The competitor who 
copied nothing
Mara spends three years writing a book that teaches people how to rea­
son about uncertain decisions. She invents a memorable sequence of 
examples: a flooded archive, an unreliable witness, a mistaken insur­
ance claim, and a map that becomes less useful as it becomes more pre­
cise. Readers tell her that the examples finally made a difficult subject 
intelligible. A competing book appears. Its sentences are different. Its 
central journey is strangely familiar. The same misconceptions arrive in 
the same order. Even the archive has survived, although it is now a 
basement laboratory.
Adrian has a different problem. His small software company has 
spent years building a reliable application for organizing research evi­
dence. A competitor releases an application with the same useful fea­
tures. Its code is different, its name is different, and its developers say 
that an AI coding agent wrote it. Customers ask why they should pay 
Adrian more. He asks a lawyer whether years of engineering effort 
must count for something.
These are fictional situations, not disguised accounts of real disputes. 
They will accompany us through this book because they expose a ques­
tion that slogans conceal: what exactly was taken? A sentence, a se­
quence of creative choices, a method, a reputation, a customer, an op­
portunity, or merely the advantage of having arrived first? Those are 
not interchangeable losses.
A copyright rule can protect the expression in Mara’s book without 
giving her ownership of rational decision-making. A trademark can 
help customers distinguish Adrian’s application from an impersonator 
without granting him the exclusive right to organize research evidence. 
That separation is foundational, not a loophole discovered by artificial 
intelligence. Copyright protects certain expressions; trademark law 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
4
protects certain source-identifying signs. Neither is a general promise 
that a useful product will remain difficult to compete with. [1, 3, 36]
Large language models make this uncomfortable distinction econom­
ically vivid. Suppose a machine can produce a convincing substitute for 
some of a book’s practical value without reproducing its protected ex­
pression. Suppose an agent can independently implement an unpatented 
function from a lawful specification. In those cases, better enforcement 
of existing copyright cannot necessarily restore the original creator’s 
market advantage. Conversely, replacing words, changing variable 
names, or using a model does not establish that protected expression 
has disappeared. The central task is to investigate the relationship be­
tween the works, not to admire the transformation tool.
This book argues for a disciplined middle position. Creators should 
have effective remedies against unlawful appropriation and deception. 
Users should retain the ability to learn, criticize, interoperate, and com­
pete. Technical systems should make the relevant evidence easier to 
preserve and examine. But society should resist converting all useful 
meaning into private property merely because machines have become 
better at translating meaning into new forms.
The journey begins with the basic concepts. It then follows rights 
across borders, explains trademarks as a system of trustworthy identifi­
cation, examines books and software separately, and develops practical 
responses. The final chapter asks what institutions should do when law­
ful competition itself becomes more disruptive. The objective is not to 
replace a lawyer. It is to make a conversation with one precise enough 
to be useful.

Reader’s orientation
This is an educational work, not legal advice for a particular transaction or 
dispute. Its research cutoff is 11 September 2026. The jurisdictional survey 
is selective: the international framework, the United States, the European 
Union, Romania, Switzerland, the United Kingdom, and Japan receive par­
ticular attention. It does not claim to catalogue every national exception or 
every pending AI case. For action, verify the current authoritative text, ap­
plicable national implementation, contractual terms, procedural deadlines, 
and later judicial developments with qualified counsel.
Numbered references identify legislation, judicial decisions, official 
guidance, technical standards, and selected research. They lead to the 
source register at the end. A treaty is not a national statute; a policy report 
is not a judgment; a settlement is not a general precedent; and a technical 
specification is not a guarantee of legal compliance. Those distinctions 
matter throughout the course. Historical decisions are dated. Recent policy 
statements are described as policy, not silently promoted into law.
Mara and Adrian, their organizations, their works, and all uncredited 
teaching examples are invented for this book. Example names have not 
been cleared as trademarks. Illustrative figures are assumptions, not indus­
try measurements. Proposed workflows and policy mechanisms are analyt­
ical recommendations, not existing legal safe harbours.
The manuscript was researched and drafted with AI assistance. No claim 
is made that every sentence is a human-authored contribution or that AI 
output automatically attracts copyright. Publication under a human or orga­
nizational name should follow substantive human editorial review and an 
accurate assessment of rights. This disclosure is especially important in a 
book about the difference between directing a tool, accepting its output, 
and creating protectable expression.

1 — What, exactly, can you own?
A copyright is not ownership of everything a reader 
learns.
1.1 — Why a copy is not a stolen chair
Imagine two different intrusions into Mara’s office. Someone takes her 
only chair. She no longer has a chair. Someone photographs a page of 
her manuscript. She still has the page, but she may have lost control 
over an economically important use of it. Both acts can be wrongful; 
they are not wrongful for the same reason. The difference explains why 
intellectual property needs carefully delimited rules instead of a simple 
transplantation of rules about physical possession.
Copyright is a legally defined set of exclusive rights over qualifying 
works. International instruments recognize economic rights and, 
through the Berne framework, moral rights associated with authorship 
and integrity. The protected object is a work expressed in a legally rele­
vant form, not every idea or useful result connected with it. Protection 
normally arises without registration in the Berne system, although na­
tional procedures can greatly affect enforcement. [1, 2]
A physical object and a work can therefore have different owners. A 
museum may own a painting without owning the right to reproduce it 
commercially. Mara’s reader owns a printed copy without becoming 
the copyright owner of the text. Adrian’s customer may receive permis­
sion to use software without acquiring its source code or the power to 
redistribute it. Thinking in layers is less intuitive than thinking in ob­
jects, but it prevents expensive mistakes.
There are several ways to justify these rights. An incentive argument 
asks whether creators and publishers will invest when competitors can 
copy cheaply. A personality argument emphasizes the connection be­
tween expression and the person who created it. A fairness argument 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
7
asks whether others should profit from someone’s labour without con­
sent. An institutional argument asks what arrangements produce a rich, 
accessible, trustworthy culture. These are different arguments. They can 
support different durations, exceptions, and remedies. None makes un­
limited control over every influence a necessary conclusion.
Consider an imagined town with one hundred readers. Mara will 
write a specialized book only if she expects enough revenue to cover 
her time. Cheap verbatim copying could undermine that expectation. 
Yet the town also needs teachers who explain the subject differently, 
critics who challenge her conclusions, libraries that preserve books, and 
researchers who apply what they learn. Protecting Mara too little could 
reduce future books. Protecting her too much could make the first book 
the last authorized starting point for everyone else. The relevant goal is 
not maximum exclusivity but a defensible balance between creation and 
subsequent use.
That balance cannot be settled by calling one side selfish. A reader 
who cannot afford a book has a real interest in access. A writer who 
cannot afford to continue writing has a real interest in compensation. A 
developer who wants to build a compatible tool may be serving cus­
tomers trapped by an incumbent. An incumbent who has invested in 
careful documentation may reasonably object to literal repackaging. An 
educational discussion must admit these interests before deciding which 
legal instrument can address them.
The history of the international system also matters. Berne, adopted 
in 1886 and subsequently revised, helped create protection across na­
tional boundaries. The later WIPO Copyright Treaty addressed the digi­
tal environment, including communication and technological measures. 
These instruments are not evidence that every new technology must be 
prohibited; they show that institutions have repeatedly adjusted the rela­
tionship between dissemination and control. [2, 4]
A useful classroom exercise is to ask what would happen without a 
particular restriction. Would the disputed act substitute for a licensed 
edition? Would it enable criticism? Would it reveal a fact? Would it let

a competing program read the user’s own files? The question does not 
determine legality on its own. It reveals the interests that a legal rule is 
supposed to reconcile.
Adrian’s frustration now becomes more precise. “We worked hard” 
is a morally relevant claim, but not a complete legal theory. He must 
identify an asset and a protected act: copied source code, confidential 
material improperly obtained, a patented invention, misleading brand­
ing, or a breached agreement. Years of work do not become one undif­
ferentiated right called ownership of the product category. The disci­
pline of specifying the right is the beginning of both effective protec­
tion and legitimate competition.
1.2 — Originality, expression, and the layers of a work
A book about probability contains facts, mathematical relationships, 
terminology, examples, diagrams, sentences, and a teaching sequence. 
Some of these may be protectable expression; some are not. A com­
puter program likewise contains algorithms, constraints imposed by its 
environment, conventional structures, and expressive implementation 
choices. The fact that the finished object is protected does not make ev­
ery element inside it exclusive.
The United States Supreme Court’s decision in Feist explains origi­
nality as independent creation with a minimal degree of creativity, not 
mere effort or novelty. Facts themselves are not created by the person 
who discovers them. An original selection or arrangement can be pro­
tected without converting the underlying facts into property. The case 
rejected protection for the ordinary white-pages listings at issue. This is 
a United States decision, not a universal formula for every national 
originality test. [54]
Suppose Mara writes: “The witness remembered the red car because 
the photograph showed a red car.” Another writer explains that later in­
formation can alter a witness’s recollection. The second writer has used 
an idea or factual proposition. Now suppose the second writer repro­
duces Mara’s invented village, the witness’s peculiar mistake, the 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
9
staged sequence of revelations, and the final reversal, merely replacing 
red with blue. That looks different from independently explaining the 
general phenomenon. The issue has moved from information to the cre­
ative way in which the information was embodied.
The same analysis works at several levels. At the lowest level, strings 
of words may match. Higher up, paragraphs may reproduce a distinc­
tive progression. Higher still, an elaborate fictional scenario or selection 
of unusual teaching examples may have been appropriated. At a suffi­
ciently abstract level, however, what remains is often an unprotected 
theme, topic, technique, or method. Legal analysis must find the rele­
vant level rather than simply choosing the one most favourable to the 
speaker.
This is why both popular shortcuts fail. “The words differ” ignores 
nonliteral expression and adaptation. “The meaning is the same” ig­
nores the freedom to communicate ideas. Neither conclusion follows 
from a single similarity score. A legal comparison asks what was 
copied, whether it was protected, whether enough was taken under the 
applicable test, and whether permission or an exception applies.
Fixation adds another distinction. United States copyright generally 
requires an original work fixed in a tangible medium, and its statute ex­
cludes ideas, systems, procedures, processes, and methods from protec­
tion as such. Other national systems have their own requirements and 
may protect some unfixed expression. A practical creator should pre­
serve a stable version rather than assume that an undocumented conver­
sation will be easy to prove later. [8]
Derivative works make the importance of expression especially clear. 
A translation changes almost every word while remaining recognizably 
a version of the earlier work. An adaptation can move a narrative into 
another medium. The addition of creative material does not necessarily 
extinguish the rights in the material adapted. Conversely, a new work 
about the same topic is not automatically a derivative work merely be­
cause it learned something from its predecessor. The legal categories

concern a particular relationship, not the fact that culture has influ­
ences. [8]
A useful method is to mark a disputed passage in three colours on a 
private working copy: facts and methods; conventional or constrained 
elements; and potentially distinctive expressive choices. This is an ana­
lytical exercise, not a courtroom algorithm. The colours force the ana­
lyst to explain why an element belongs in one category rather than an­
other. If everything receives the “protected” colour, the analysis proba­
bly has not begun. If nothing does, the analyst may be confusing a 
work’s factual subject with the author’s treatment of it.
For Adrian, the exercise could identify a standard file format, a re­
quired protocol sequence, a public algorithm, a peculiar decomposition 
into modules, and original documentation. For Mara, it could distin­
guish a commonplace definition from an elaborate explanatory scene. 
In each case the commercially valuable part may not coincide with the 
legally exclusive part. That mismatch is one of the central difficulties of 
creative enterprise, and AI intensifies it rather than inventing it.
1.3 — The bundle of rights and the chain of ownership
Mara’s publisher sends a contract that says it wants “all rights in all me­
dia.” The phrase sounds efficient. It is also a warning to slow down. A 
printed edition, an audiobook, a translation, a film adaptation, a search­
able database, and a model-training licence are not the same commer­
cial opportunity. Treating them as interchangeable can surrender op­
tions that neither party has priced or understood.
Ownership and authorship are separate questions. Under United 
States law, copyright initially belongs to the author, subject to statutory 
rules such as work made for hire; ownership of a physical copy is dis­
tinct from copyright ownership. Transfers of copyright ownership gen­
erally require a signed writing. Joint ownership, employment, commis­
sioned work, and corporate ownership introduce special rules. A con­
tractor is not automatically an employee, and labelling a contract “work 
for hire” does not remove the statutory conditions. [9]

A licence permits specified uses while leaving ownership where the 
agreement places it. An assignment transfers identified rights. An 
exclusive licence may prevent even the licensor from authorizing 
another party within its scope; a nonexclusive licence can coexist with 
other permissions. The operational questions are concrete: which work, 
which rights, which territory, which language, which duration, which 
media, which sublicensing powers, and which payment mechanism?
Imagine that Mara granted only English print rights five years ago. A 
later publisher wants to supply the entire manuscript to an AI service 
that produces personalized study guides. The earlier contract’s wording, 
the relevant law, and the proposed operations must be examined. It is 
not enough to say that both uses involve a book. Nor should Mara as­
sume that the word “digital” in an old contract definitely excludes or 
definitely includes every future computational use. An amendment that 
names the intended activity may be safer than litigation over a vague 
phrase.
Moral rights create another layer. Recognition of authorship and pro­
tection against certain harmful distortions are not simply payment 
claims. Their scope, duration, transferability, and waivability vary sig­
nificantly. A broad economic assignment does not automatically re­
solve them. The Berne framework recognizes attribution and integrity 
interests, while domestic law supplies important details. [2]
For a practical rights ledger, the unit of analysis should be the com­
ponent rather than the product. A book can contain commissioned illus­
trations, licensed photographs, a translator’s contribution, quoted mate­
rial, and a separately designed cover. A software package can contain 
employee code, contractor code, open-source dependencies, fonts, sam­
ple datasets, and documentation. “Our company owns the product” is 
not a substitute for tracing each relevant permission.
Consider Adrian’s acquisition review. A contractor created a central 
module before the company existed. The founder later paid an invoice 
but never obtained a clear assignment. A contributor included a library 
with obligations the team did not record. A designer supplied a logo but

retained copyright in the artwork. None of these facts proves that the 
business cannot operate. They show why a buyer will ask for evidence 
rather than accept a confident ownership declaration.
Duration is equally easy to misunderstand. For many modern United 
States works, the general term is the author’s life plus seventy years; 
special categories, including qualifying works made for hire, use differ­
ent calculations. Older works can require a separate historical analysis. 
Copyright is not renewed indefinitely like a maintained trademark. A 
publication date alone often cannot tell a reader whether a particular 
edition is free to reuse. [10]
The right habit is to treat the chain of title as a living record. Keep 
signed agreements, their amendments, the covered versions, licences 
that arrived with dependencies, and evidence of the capacity in which 
contributors worked. Record uncertainty openly. A ledger with three 
unresolved entries is more useful than a folder labelled “all cleared” 
whose contents have never been examined. For AI-assisted work, in­
clude the human contribution and the permitted uses of the supplied in­
puts. Ownership begins to become manageable when it is a traceable 
relationship rather than an assertion.
1.4 — Permission, exceptions, and the public domain
A lecturer wants to criticize a paragraph. A reviewer wants to display a 
small image of a cover. A library wants to preserve a deteriorating 
work. A student wants an accessible format. These are different activi­
ties, and legal systems do not treat “using someone else’s work” as one 
uniform transaction. Some uses require permission; some fall within 
exceptions; some involve no protected material at all.
United States fair use considers four statutory factors: purpose and 
character, nature of the work, amount and substantiality, and effect on 
the relevant potential market or value. It is a contextual analysis, not a 
numerical allowance or a rule that educational use always wins. The 
Copyright Office’s index illustrates how outcomes depend on facts. 
Other jurisdictions often use more specifically defined exceptions or 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
13
fair-dealing categories rather than importing this open-ended United 
States doctrine. [57]
Suppose a reviewer quotes a short passage because its wording is the 
object of criticism. Compare a subscription service that gives readers 
the most memorable pages of each new book so they need not buy it. 
Both may claim to educate. Their purposes, amounts, and relationships 
to the original market differ. Now compare an independent explanation 
of the book’s general thesis that takes no protected expression. That 
third activity may not need a fair-use defence for the thesis at all. Be­
fore analysing an exception, identify what exclusive right would other­
wise be implicated.
The Supreme Court’s Warhol decision illustrates the danger of treat­
ing new meaning as a universal defence. The dispute concerned a par­
ticular commercial licensing use of an image derived from a photo­
graph. The Court’s analysis of the first fair-use factor did not make ev­
ery alteration transformative merely because it conveyed something dif­
ferent. The holding must be read in relation to that use, not as a general 
prohibition on artistic adaptation or a complete ruling about every use 
of the artwork. [17]
Attribution does not replace permission. A copied chapter with an 
impeccable bibliography can still be unauthorized. Equally, a legally 
permissible use can be academically dishonest when it hides intellectual 
dependence. Copyright infringement and plagiarism overlap but answer 
different questions. The first concerns legally protected rights and acts; 
the second concerns norms of credit, honesty, and scholarly contribu­
tion. This distinction is essential when a model paraphrases an argu­
ment without naming its source.
The public domain is not a low-quality corner of culture. It is the 
space in which exclusive copyright either does not arise or has ended, 
subject to jurisdictional and component-specific qualifications. The un­
derlying text of an old work may be free while a recent translation, an­
notation, photograph, or recording has separate rights. A platform’s ac­
cess terms may raise additional questions without recreating expired

copyright in the underlying work. A publisher should distinguish the 
public-domain work from the particular file or edition it obtained.
Digital locks complicate this picture. United States law separately 
regulates circumvention and certain dealings in circumvention technol­
ogy, with statutory exceptions and periodically defined exemptions. It 
also addresses certain misconduct involving copyright-management in­
formation. The practical lesson is not that every lock creates unlimited 
rights, but that a permitted downstream purpose does not automatically 
answer every question about how access was obtained. [13]
Mara can publish a sample chapter under a clearly defined licence 
while reserving other uses of the full book. A reader can distinguish 
quoting that sample, sharing it under its licence, and uploading the en­
tire commercial edition to an external service. Adrian can permit inter­
operability testing without permitting redistribution of his source code. 
Specific permissions often reduce friction better than absolute slogans.
The classroom test is simple to state and difficult to answer well: 
what is the legal basis for this use? “It was online,” “I paid for a copy,” 
“I named the author,” and “a machine did it” are not complete answers. 
A sound answer identifies an absence of protected subject matter, a li­
cence, an exception, expiration of rights, or another relevant legal basis, 
and then states the factual conditions on which it depends.
1.5 — The other rights that people confuse
When someone says “protect my idea,” the necessary response is a 
question, not a form. Do they want to prevent code copying, preserve a 
confidential process, stop an impersonator, control a patented invention, 
or negotiate the terms of a collaboration? Choosing the wrong instru­
ment can produce both wasted expenditure and exaggerated expecta­
tions.
Trademark law concerns signs that distinguish the source of goods or 
services. Its central orientation differs from copyright’s protection of 
expression. A name can be a powerful trademark even when the word 
itself is not a copyrighted work. A complex logo may involve both 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
15
trademark protection and copyright in the artwork. The two rights can 
belong to different parties and have different territorial and contractual 
histories. [36]
Patents, where available and valid, can protect claimed inventions 
against certain uses even when another party arrived independently. 
Copyright usually does not function that way. A reader who indepen­
dently implements a general method described in a book is not thereby 
copying the book’s expression. Baker v. Selden drew an enduring 
United States distinction between copyright in an explanation and ex­
clusive rights over the system explained. It is a historical decision, not a 
complete account of modern patent eligibility. [55, 58]
Trade-secret protection has another logic: information must satisfy 
requirements involving secrecy, commercial value linked to secrecy, 
and protective measures. The EU Trade Secrets Directive distinguishes 
unlawful acquisition from routes such as independent discovery and, 
under specified conditions, observation or testing of lawfully possessed 
or publicly available products. It does not create copyright in every use­
ful confidential fact or remove all contractual questions. [27]
For Adrian, secrecy may suit a server-side optimization that competi­
tors cannot readily observe. It is a poor strategy for a feature visible to 
every user. Publishing a detailed explanation to attract customers and 
simultaneously assuming nobody can learn the method from it is a con­
tradictory plan. The decision to disclose should therefore be made con­
sciously, with consideration of what can remain protectable after dis­
closure.
Contracts allocate obligations between parties. They can regulate ac­
cess, confidentiality, permitted processing, audit, payment, and risk. 
But a contract is not a universal statute binding everyone who later en­
counters an idea. Enforceability depends on formation, applicable law, 
mandatory rules, and the particular restriction. The practical recommen­
dation is to describe the intended transaction precisely and avoid as­
suming that broad boilerplate makes difficult legal questions disappear.

Databases illustrate why a single “copyright cleared” label is insuffi­
cient. A collection may contain individually protected works, 
protectable selection or arrangement, access restrictions, personal data, 
and—in the EU—a distinct database-right regime. The specific legal 
interest at stake must be identified. A person may be free to state a fact 
while still facing questions about systematic extraction from a protected 
database or misuse of confidential access. [3, 59]
Now place Mara’s book beside Adrian’s application. The text, art­
work, code, name, user data, confidential methods, and contractual rela­
tionships form separate layers. Some can be licensed openly; some can 
be kept private; some cannot be owned in the desired way. A realistic 
strategy protects the defensible layers and builds value in the others. It 
does not inflate a weak claim until it sounds comprehensive.
At the end of this first chapter, the reader should be able to reformu­
late an emotional complaint into an analysable proposition. Not “they 
stole our product,” but “they reproduced this protected module without 
the required permission.” Not “they stole my knowledge,” but “their 
book preserves these distinctive invented examples and their expressive 
arrangement.” Not “they used our word,” but “their presentation is 
likely to mislead relevant customers about origin or affiliation.” Preci­
sion does not make every claim succeed. It makes success, compro­
mise, and legitimate competition distinguishable.

2 — One internet, many laws
A file can cross a border without carrying the law of its 
birthplace.
2.1 — The international architecture is a network, not a 
world code
Mara publishes in English from Romania. A Swiss distributor sells her 
book. A United States service processes it on infrastructure located 
elsewhere and answers a customer in Germany. Which copyright law 
applies? There is no useful answer consisting only of the place where 
the website was registered. The acts, locations, claims, defendants, con­
tracts, and forum must first be separated.
Berne provides a framework of national treatment, automatic protec­
tion, and minimum standards. National treatment broadly means that el­
igible foreign authors receive the protection a country gives its own na­
tionals, subject to the Convention’s rules. It does not mean that the au­
thor carries one home-country copyright law everywhere. TRIPS adds 
trade-related minimum obligations and enforcement disciplines, incor­
porates much of Berne, and expressly distinguishes expression from 
ideas and methods. TRIPS does not incorporate Berne’s Article 6bis 
moral-rights obligations, which should not be mistaken for abolishing 
moral rights in national law. [2, 3]
The WIPO Copyright Treaty supplements the framework for the digi­
tal environment. Its subject matter includes computer programs and 
original compilations, and it addresses distribution, communication, 
technological protection, and rights-management information. These in­
ternational obligations are implemented and interpreted through legal 
systems. A treaty can make national rules more alike without erasing 
differences in exceptions, ownership, remedies, or procedure. [4]

For trademarks, the Paris Convention establishes important principles 
including national treatment, priority, and protection against unfair 
competition. Its priority mechanism can preserve an earlier filing date 
for subsequent filings within the applicable period—six months for 
trademarks—when the relevant conditions are met. It is not an auto­
matic worldwide trademark. [5]
The Madrid System is a procedural route for seeking trademark pro­
tection in designated participating jurisdictions through an international 
application and registration framework. Designated offices retain sub­
stantive examination powers. Refusal in one jurisdiction does not mean 
the mark has necessarily failed everywhere, and acceptance elsewhere 
does not force a country to ignore its own grounds of refusal. The sys­
tem simplifies administration; it does not turn the world into one trade­
mark territory. [6]
Think of the international system as a railway network. Agreements 
help trains move between national systems, but the stations still have 
rules, and a ticket to one destination is not a ticket to every destination. 
This analogy also identifies a common commercial mistake: paying for 
an international filing without identifying the markets in which protec­
tion is actually needed.
For Mara’s book, the analysis could begin with the disputed repro­
duction and the market where relief is sought. For Adrian’s trademark, 
it could begin with the territory in which customers encounter the sign 
and the rights registered or acquired there. For their contracts, a govern­
ing-law clause and a forum clause may matter without deciding every 
noncontractual claim. Those are questions for jurisdiction-specific ad­
vice, not details that disappear because the service is delivered online.
There is also an enforcement budget. Winning a legal argument in 
principle does not guarantee affordable evidence collection, a solvent 
defendant, or practical removal of distributed copies. An international 
strategy should therefore identify not only rights but leverage: contrac­
tual counterparties, app stores, payment relationships, commercial dis­
tributors, and identifiable hosting operators. Those channels must be

used truthfully and proportionately. Sending a platform a misleading in­
fringement allegation is not a legitimate substitute for having a right.
An instructive exercise is to draw three maps for a project: where rel­
evant content is obtained and processed, where customers are targeted, 
and where enforceable counterparties or assets exist. Differences be­
tween the maps reveal legal and operational questions. A project does 
not become lawful merely by placing one server in a favourable juris­
diction. Nor does every globally accessible website necessarily trigger 
identical consequences in every country. The point is to replace a fic­
tional universal jurisdiction with a documented analysis of real acts.
2.2 — The United States: flexible exceptions, demanding 
distinctions
The United States is important in AI disputes because many influential 
services, contractual relationships, and litigated acts connect to it. That 
importance does not make its rules global. A European publisher who 
says “fair use permits this” must still explain why United States law 
governs the relevant act and whether the asserted use actually satisfies 
the doctrine.
Several practical distinctions deserve attention. Registration is not 
what initially creates ordinary United States copyright, but registration 
or refusal is generally required before bringing an infringement action 
concerning a United States work, subject to statutory qualifications. 
Timely registration can affect eligibility for statutory damages and at­
torney’s fees. These procedural consequences are different from the un­
derlying existence of copyright. A foreign work can raise different reg­
istration prerequisites while still presenting remedial questions. [11]
Mara should therefore not assume that a copyright notice is an en­
forcement plan. She needs to understand whether and when registration 
is useful for the works and markets at issue. A notice can communicate 
a claim; it does not automatically establish ownership, human author­
ship, or compliance with registration requirements. Filing an inaccurate 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
20
description of AI-generated content could create difficulties rather than 
solve them.
Fair use is particularly relevant to analysis, search, criticism, and 
computational processing, but “transformative” is not a synonym for 
technologically sophisticated. A tool that uses a text for a different 
function may have arguments unavailable to a tool that simply delivers 
a competing readable substitute. Still, purpose alone is not the complete 
statutory inquiry. Amount, the nature of the source, and legally cogniz­
able market effects also matter. The existence of a hypothetical licence 
does not mechanically decide the analysis, and neither does an asserted 
public benefit. [57]
A further distinction separates litigation about training from litigation 
about output. The United States Copyright Office’s May 2025 prepubli­
cation report on generative-AI training discusses the issue as context-
dependent rather than universally fair or universally infringing. The Of­
fice’s AI page still identifies that document as a prepublication version 
in the material consulted for this book. An agency report is informative, 
but it is not a Supreme Court judgment or an enacted general training 
exception. [15, 16]
For a business, uncertainty should produce a decision record. Sup­
pose Adrian can choose between a model backed by well-documented 
training permissions and a cheaper model with opaque provenance. He 
should compare actual evidence, contractual protection, use restrictions, 
indemnity conditions, and the consequences of a dispute. He should not 
treat a vendor’s marketing claim that a model is “safe” as a substitute 
for examining the transaction. Conversely, incomplete public disclosure 
is not itself proof that a model infringes.
United States remedies also require careful reading. Injunctions, ac­
tual damages and attributable profits, and statutory damages operate un­
der specific requirements. Statutory ranges are not automatic invoices, 
and counting outputs is not necessarily the same as counting works for 
damages. Safe-harbour provisions for qualifying service providers have 
conditions and do not declare every hosted item lawful. An 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
21
enforcement strategy that begins with the largest conceivable number 
can mislead both the rights holder and the alleged infringer. [12]
The practical lesson is neither “litigation is useless” nor “register and 
you are protected from AI.” It is to align procedural preparation with 
the actual asset. Preserve drafts, versions, publication facts, relevant 
contracts, and evidence of the disputed use. Distinguish direct copying 
from a general competitive threat. Document the human contribution 
accurately. Keep a route for resolving small issues without escalating 
them into expensive symbolic battles.
For the reader, the United States offers a methodological warning: 
flexible legal standards require more factual discipline, not less. A four-
factor framework does not mean that any preferred outcome can be 
dressed in four paragraphs. It means that the advocate must explain 
why the particular use, of the particular material, in the particular mar­
ket, fits the doctrine.
2.3 — The European Union: harmonized layers and two 
mining exceptions
A European project does not face one undifferentiated “AI copyright 
law.” It faces copyright rules, software-specific rules, national imple­
mentation, trademark law, and AI-provider obligations that answer dif­
ferent questions. Confusing those layers produces claims such as “the 
AI Act authorizes training” or “a no-AI notice prohibits every use.” 
Neither is a reliable statement of the framework.
The Software Directive protects the expression of computer programs 
rather than their underlying ideas and principles. Other directives ad­
dress reproduction and communication, databases, duration, and en­
forcement. The EU’s general harmonized term for an author’s copy­
right is life plus seventy years, with special rules for particular cate­
gories. Member States retain important national rules within and along­
side harmonized fields. Romania, France, and Germany are therefore 
not interchangeable for every ownership, moral-rights, contract, or pro­
cedural question. [20, 26]

The DSM Directive’s Articles 3 and 4 create distinct text-and-data-
mining routes. Article 3 concerns eligible research organizations and 
cultural-heritage institutions conducting scientific research with lawful 
access. Article 4 is broader in beneficiaries and purposes but is subject 
to an appropriate express reservation of rights; online reservations are 
contemplated in machine-readable form. Both have conditions, and 
their scope must be examined for the particular acts. The Article 3 route 
is not displaced merely by an Article 4 reservation. [21]
The difference can be made concrete. An eligible university team 
with lawful access studies patterns across publications for a scientific 
project. A commercial service mines a catalogue to develop a product. 
They cannot simply exchange legal labels because both operations in­
volve algorithms. Nor can an organization claim the research route 
merely by naming its product department “the research laboratory.” Eli­
gibility, purpose, access, security, and the actual operation require anal­
ysis.
A second misconception treats mining as the entire product lifecycle. 
Suppose access and copying for a qualifying analysis are permitted. 
That does not automatically authorize publication of a copyrighted 
chapter in the final user interface. Conversely, a carefully limited out­
put does not settle whether the corpus was lawfully acquired. The per­
mission or exception must correspond to the act, not to the project’s 
most attractive description.
The AI Act adds a provider-compliance layer. Article 53 requires 
general-purpose-model providers within its scope to have a policy for 
compliance with EU copyright law, including identifying and respect­
ing Article 4(3) reservations, and to publish a sufficiently detailed sum­
mary of training content using the prescribed template. Certain open-
source-model exemptions concern specified documentation duties, not 
a blanket exemption from the copyright-policy and summary duties. 
These are not private licences from every rights holder and do not 
themselves resolve infringement claims. [22]

route from the binding legal obligations. They also identify the phased 
application of the AI framework. For procurement, the important 
question is the provider’s applicable obligations and transitional status, 
not whether a code logo appears on a website. [23, 24]
Mara’s publisher can express reservations appropriately and preserve 
evidence of when and where they applied. That is an operational step, 
not a promise that every historical training event becomes reversible. 
Adrian can ask his suppliers how they implement relevant reservations 
and document downstream information. Neither should claim that a 
crawler signal alone supplies complete global legal protection.
For a European educational platform, a more useful design is a per­

trieval, quotation, transformation, and distribution. A legal basis for one 
column should not automatically fill all the others. The platform can 
then explain a refusal precisely: “We may index this material under 
these conditions, but we have not established permission to return long 
passages.” That is better than either indiscriminate blocking or a univer­
sal “AI use permitted” flag.
2.4 — Romania and Switzerland: nearby markets, dis­

Adrian operates a Romanian development company and works with a 
Swiss business partner. Mara’s publishing project has readers in both 
countries. Geographic proximity and commercial familiarity can make 
differences look smaller than they are. Switzerland is not an EU Mem­

merely because a project is European.
Romania’s principal copyright statute is Law No. 8/1996, with 
amendments including Law No. 69/2022. The consolidated text

Switzerland offers a particularly memorable copyright distinction.

A useful joint operating model would place Romanian employment

2.5 — The United Kingdom, Japan, and the danger of ex­
porting slogans
Two further examples are enough to demonstrate why “AI copyright” 
cannot be summarized as a global yes-or-no question. The United King­
dom has a distinctive statutory tradition concerning computer-generated 
works. Japan has a distinctive analytical exception associated with non-
enjoyment uses. Neither can be reduced to an internet slogan without 
losing the qualifications that make the rule meaningful.
The United Kingdom government’s report of 18 March 2026 states 
that a broad mining exception with an opt-out is no longer its preferred 
approach and that further work is needed before reform. The report also 
discusses the existing computer-generated-works regime and possible 
change. These are policy positions, not proof that Parliament has al­
ready enacted the discussed repeal or a new general training permis­
sion. The current framework includes a narrower noncommercial-re­
search mining exception, subject to conditions. [34]
This is a lesson in reading legal news. A consultation asks what 
should happen. A government response indicates policy direction. A 
bill proposes legal text. An enacted provision may still have a future 
commencement date. A judicial interpretation may narrow or clarify its 
application. Treating these documents as identical can make an internal 
compliance policy wrong before anyone has acted under it.
For Mara, the United Kingdom’s discussion of computer-generated 
works also challenges a simplistic international claim that every juris­
diction treats unassisted AI output exactly as the United States

Japan’s Agency for Cultural Affairs published a 2024 overview ex­

The conceptual interest is considerable. Reading a novel to enjoy it

The book does not attempt equivalent summaries for every other ma­

short country list can create a misleading sense of clearance when it 
silently omits the one rule relevant to a particular deployment. The bet­

jurisdiction.

can be imitated
A name can tell you who stands behind a promise. It

3.1 — A trademark is an address for trust
Adrian’s application could be reproduced in at least two importantly 
different ways. A competitor might build a different program that per­
forms the same tasks and sell it under a clearly different name. Another 
might distribute an inferior program under a name, icon, and presenta­
tion that suggest Adrian’s company made it. The first situation calls for 
an investigation of code, patents, contracts, and the permissible scope 
of competition. The second adds a source-identification problem. Cus­
tomers may choose the wrong product because they misunderstand who 
is responsible for it.
A trademark is a sign capable of distinguishing one source’s goods or 
services from those of others. Words and logos are familiar examples, 
but the legal categories can extend beyond them. The sign is not owned 
for every imaginable purpose: protection depends on the relevant law, 
rights, goods or services, and use. A company name, a domain registra­
tion, and a trademark registration are different things. Securing one 
does not establish entitlement to all three. [25, 36]
This difference from copyright is easiest to see with a plain word. An 
invented product name may have little or no copyright significance as 
text, yet function powerfully as a mark. An intricate illustration may at­
tract copyright protection but fail to function as a trademark because 
customers see decoration rather than a source identifier. A logo can oc­
cupy both categories. Its designer’s copyright and the business’s trade­
mark interest then need to be handled separately, including through a 
proper agreement.


THE RIGHT TO COPY
30
Distinctiveness explains why some names make better candidates 
than others. The USPTO distinguishes stronger coined, arbitrary, and 
suggestive marks from descriptive expressions and generic terms. A de­
scriptive term may acquire source-identifying significance; a generic 
term is the name of the relevant category rather than one undertaking’s 
distinctive identifier. These categories depend on the goods and cus­
tomer perception, not on the word in isolation. A familiar word can be 
arbitrary for an unrelated product. [37]
Suppose Adrian calls his tool “Research Evidence Organizer.” The 
name efficiently describes its job. That marketing advantage comes 
with a legal disadvantage: other businesses also need ordinary words to 
describe the same job. A distinctive name followed by an explanatory 
subtitle may serve both purposes better. This is a strategic recommen­
dation, not a clearance opinion about any particular candidate. The at­
tractive invented word may already belong to someone in the relevant 
market.
Mara encounters the same issue when she creates a publishing im­
print. A memorable imprint can identify the editorial institution respon­
sible for a family of books. That identifier is conceptually different 
from the arguments in a book or the title of one publication. The dis­
tinction is useful because the imprint can persist while the content 
changes. It becomes an address to which readers direct trust, criticism, 
and expectations.
Trust must nevertheless be earned outside the register. A legally pro­
tected mark does not certify that a book is accurate, a program secure, 
or a model responsibly trained. It enables identification, after which or­
dinary evidence about quality still matters. Confusing registration with 
endorsement would repeat the very error that source-identifying law is 
meant to reduce.
For the AI economy, this suggests a limited but important opportu­
nity. Where a substitute is easy to generate, knowing who reviewed it 
and who will correct it may become more valuable. That is an analyti­
cal expectation, not a guarantee of commercial success. Some

customers will prefer the cheapest adequate substitute. A trustworthy 
source must therefore offer something identifiable and useful, rather 
than merely insist that its name deserves loyalty.
3.2 — Clearing, registering, and maintaining a mark
The least expensive moment to discover a naming conflict is before a 
team has printed the packaging, published the documentation, and per­
suaded customers to remember the name. Trademark planning should 
therefore begin as a search problem, not as a graphic-design problem. 
The relevant search is broader than asking whether an identical string 
appears in a register. Similar pronunciation, appearance, meaning, and 
related markets can matter. The USPTO’s guidance treats similarity be­
tween marks and relatedness of goods or services as central to confu­
sion analysis. [38]
A practical clearance process begins with the actual business plan. 
What will be sold? To whom? Where? Through which channels? A 
name acceptable for an unrelated industrial product may conflict with 
an existing educational-software brand. A company that begins with an 
ebook and later offers courses, downloadable applications, and hosted 
services should discuss that realistic expansion with a trademark profes­
sional. Filing the vaguest possible description is not a substitute for do­
ing this work.
The Nice Classification organizes goods and services for registration. 
It is an administrative framework, not a partition of commercial reality 
into unrelated legal universes. The EU trademark regulation expressly 
prevents treating goods or services as similar or dissimilar merely be­
cause they occupy the same or different Nice classes. A book, an edu­
cational application, and an online course may be commercially related 
even when their classification differs. [7, 25]
Territory is equally important. A Romanian national mark and an EU 
trademark have different geographic reach. Switzerland requires its 
own analysis. The Madrid System can simplify seeking protection in 
designated members, but it does not turn the application into a single

globally enforceable right exempt from local examination. Decisions 
about filing should follow expected markets, exposure, and budget 
rather than the ambition to “own the name worldwide.” [6, 30, 33]
An imagined small publisher might prioritize its home market and 
principal export markets, reserve appropriate domains, and monitor a 
narrower set of likely conflicts. A business preparing a major interna­
tional launch might justify a more extensive search and filing pro­
gramme. Neither plan is automatically superior. The right level of in­
vestment depends on the cost of renaming, likelihood of confusion, 
commercial importance of the identifier, and resources available for 
maintaining the portfolio.
Registration is also not a one-time purchase of immortality. Renewal 
and genuine-use requirements matter. EU registration runs in renewable 
ten-year periods, while a continuous five-year period of non-use can 
make a mark vulnerable, subject to the applicable rules and proper rea­
sons. The Swiss IPI likewise describes renewable ten-year protection 
and the importance of use. Exact evidence, deadlines, and remedies 
must be checked in the relevant system. [25, 32, 33]
Adrian should preserve examples of real use: product pages, invoices, 
release materials, and dated packaging or screenshots showing the mark 
in relation to the relevant goods or services. The records should reflect 
reality, not manufacture an appearance of activity. He should also 
maintain ownership and licence documentation. A portfolio becomes 
less useful if the registered owner is a dissolved company while the ac­
tual business assumes somebody else handled the transfer.
The practical lesson is administrative rather than glamorous. A good 
mark is a maintained relationship among a sign, a responsible undertak­
ing, a market, and evidence. The filing certificate is one important doc­
ument within that relationship. It is not the relationship itself.

3.3 — Confusion, comparison, and the right to name a 
competitor
Imagine three advertisements. One says, “Our independent application 
imports files exported by Adrian’s application.” A second says, “The 
official successor to Adrian’s application,” although there is no such re­
lationship. A third compares the two tools using a confusingly similar 
logo and an ambiguous download button. The presence of a competi­
tor’s name does not make these advertisements equivalent. Their fac­
tual accuracy, presentation, and likely interpretation differ.
Trademark law must leave room for truthful references. A repair ser­
vice may need to identify the products it repairs. A reviewer must be 
able to identify the book being reviewed. A compatibility statement 
may need to name another program. EU law includes limitations con­
cerning necessary reference to the proprietor’s goods or services, sub­
ject to honest practices. US doctrines and their application are not iden­
tical. A responsible course should not invent a universal “nominative 
fair use” checkbox that resolves every jurisdiction. [25]
For practical drafting, the relevant questions are clear even before a 
lawyer assesses the final text. Is the claim true? Is the reference neces­
sary to explain the relationship? Does the overall presentation imply au­
thorization, sponsorship, or a commercial connection that does not ex­
ist? Is the competitor’s branding more prominent than needed? Could a 
reader identify the independent supplier without searching for a dis­
claimer? These questions improve design; they do not replace the legal 
test.
Disclaimers deserve particular skepticism. A small sentence at the 
bottom of a page may not repair a misleading headline or download 
flow. Conversely, an accurate and proportionate reference should not be 
assumed unlawful merely because it names another business. The eval­
uation must concern the whole use, not an isolated token. This matters 
for AI-generated interfaces, where a model can create a polished but 
misleading combination of familiar names, logos, and confident asser­
tions.

34
Marks with reputation can receive protection extending beyond ordi­
nary confusion in circumstances defined by the applicable law. EU 
rules address certain uses taking unfair advantage of, or harming, the 
distinctive character or reputation of a protected mark. That is not a rule 
that every famous business owns every cultural reference to its name. 
The requirements and limitations remain important. [25]
Mara’s book reviewer illustrates the public-interest side. A critical 
essay should be able to say which book it criticizes. A teaching page 
should be able to compare competing interpretations. Treating every 
unfavorable reference as infringement would make commercial identi­
fiers a tool for silencing scrutiny. The creator’s legitimate interest in 
avoiding deception must coexist with the public’s legitimate interest in 
discussing recognizable objects.
An AI assistant introduces an additional design challenge. It may de­
scribe a third-party service as “official” without evidence. It may place 
a generated product beside the original brand in a way that suggests en­
dorsement. Those failures are not solved by measuring textual original­
ity. A platform should preserve the source of affiliation claims, require 
verification before applying official labels, and distinguish quotations 
from its own assertions. These are proposed safeguards against avoid­
able confusion.
The operational insight is that brand safety cannot be reduced to a 
blacklist of words. A system that blocks all competitor names prevents 
lawful comparison. A system that permits every mention can enable im­
personation. Better design represents relationships: made by, compati­
ble with, reviewed by, licensed by, inspired by, and unaffiliated with. 
Each relationship should mean something testable. A trustworthy inter­
face should not use them interchangeably.
3.4 — Book titles, author identities, and synthetic en­
dorsement
Mara initially believes that registering her book title will protect the en­
tire book from competing versions. The belief combines two mistakes. 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
35
A mark does not give copyright-like control over content. Moreover, in 
US registration practice, the title of a single creative work generally 
does not function as a registrable trademark. A genuine series title can 
be different. The USPTO explains that editions or formats of the same 
work do not simply create a series, and that some categories, including 
computer software, require different treatment. [39]
This rule should not be generalized into “book titles are never pro­
tected anywhere.” National law and other causes of action can matter. 
Nor should a publisher manufacture a nominal series and assume that 
the underlying source-identification requirement has disappeared. The 
useful strategic question is whether customers recognize the sign as 
identifying an ongoing source, rather than merely identifying one ex­
pressive work.
Suppose a seller releases a book called “Mara’s Official 
Companion,” places Mara’s photograph on the cover, and says she ap­
proved the advice. The content might contain no copied paragraph. The 
problem would not thereby vanish. False endorsement, misleading pre­
sentation, trademark issues, personality interests, and other national 
rules may need separate examination. Copyright clearance cannot es­
tablish permission to appropriate someone’s commercial identity. A 
photograph may also carry its own rights.
Now change the facts. An independent scholar publishes an unmis­
takably independent critical guide to Mara’s work. Mara dislikes it, but 
the title accurately identifies its subject and the presentation does not 
suggest endorsement. The ethical and legal analysis must change. The 
ability to discuss another person’s work is essential to scholarship and 
criticism. A creator’s dislike is not, by itself, evidence of a protectable 
monopoly over that discussion.
AI systems make the difference between these examples particularly 
easy to blur. A model may adopt a familiar author’s manner, generate 
an invented endorsement, or produce a fake bibliography entry. A pub­
lisher may then mistake fluency for authorization. A sensible editorial 
rule is to treat every claim about a real person’s participation as a 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
36
factual claim requiring evidence. “The model said so” establishes only 
that a model produced text.
The same reasoning applies to a software product that names a re­
spected engineer as an adviser or claims compatibility certification 
from a platform. These claims are about relationships in the world. 
They need documentary support independent of the model that gener­
ated the marketing copy. A signed statement can help identify the per­
son making an assertion, but the publisher must still check its meaning, 
scope, and continuing validity.
Mara can strengthen her publishing practice without trying to monop­
olize critical discussion. She can maintain an authoritative catalogue of 
her own editions and approved collaborations, make corrections pub­
licly traceable, and give retailers a clear route for reporting imperson­
ation. Readers can then distinguish the genuine source from an unaffili­
ated interpretation. This is not perfect protection: false listings can still 
appear, and readers can still ignore the catalogue. It is a practical reduc­
tion in avoidable uncertainty.
The deeper issue is not whether an AI can sound like an author. It is 
whether the surrounding institution represents authorship, approval, and 
responsibility honestly. A persuasive imitation may be lawful in one re­
spect and deceptive in another. Treating “AI-generated” as a complete 
legal category hides that distinction precisely when readers most need 
it.
3.5 — Why trademark cannot repair every copyright dis­
appointment
The temptation is understandable. If copyright does not prevent a com­
petitor from using public-domain material or unprotected ideas, perhaps 
a claim about “origin” can restore control. In Dastar v. Twentieth Cen­
tury Fox, the US Supreme Court rejected a use of the Lanham Act that 
would effectively convert a dispute about authorship of communicative 
content into a new copyright-like entitlement. Its reasoning distin­
guishes the origin of tangible goods from authorship of the ideas or 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
37
expression embodied in them. The decision is not permission to make 
every deceptive affiliation claim. [53]
The principle matters beyond its precise US doctrinal setting. Differ­
ent legal instruments have different jobs. A business should not assume 
that losing a copyright argument automatically produces a trademark 
argument with the same desired scope. Doing so risks turning a poten­
tially renewable source-identification right into indefinite control over 
cultural content.
Consider four versions of Adrian’s rival product. In the first, the 
competitor uses a clearly different identity and independently imple­
ments a public functional specification. In the second, it copies pro­
tected source code while keeping a different identity. In the third, it 
writes independent code but presents the product as Adrian’s official 
release. In the fourth, it both copies code and impersonates the source. 
These are not four degrees of the same wrong. They are different com­
binations of potential copyright and trademark issues, with other rights 
remaining possible.
Mara faces an equivalent matrix. A new explanation of the same sci­
entific topic under an independent title may be legitimate competition. 
A disguised adaptation of her distinctive expressive structure may re­
quire copyright analysis. A fake official edition adds source confusion. 
A review with clear attribution may involve permissible discussion. 
Classifying every version as “stealing my book” prevents a proportion­
ate response because it erases the reasons why one version differs from 
another.
This distinction also disciplines enforcement. A complaint should 
identify the particular protected interest, the act, the relevant evidence, 
and the remedy requested. A seller who has copied a logo should not 
automatically be ordered to abandon all lawful functional competition. 
A rival who has copied protected text should not be allowed to dismiss 
the issue merely because its own company name appears on the cover. 
Precision protects both the claimant and the person accused.


THE RIGHT TO COPY
38
There is also an ethical limit to the strategy of competing through 
brand. The author’s name should represent genuine work, not merely 
become a premium label attached to unreviewed machine output. The 
software brand should identify a real maintenance obligation, not a 
marketing shell around indistinguishable and unaudited components. 
Where the institution cannot explain what it contributes, trust becomes 
a mechanism for charging more without doing more.
The chapter’s practical conclusion is therefore qualified. Trademarks 
can help preserve distinguishable responsibility in a market full of easy 
substitutes. They cannot preserve every old margin, prevent every inde­
pendent explanation, or make an unreliable product reliable. Their 
value is strongest when attached to a verifiable promise: this edition 
was checked by these people; this release will be maintained by this or­
ganization; this error-reporting route leads to someone accountable.
Before moving to books, ask a final classroom question. If the com­
petitor changes its name and removes every misleading claim of affilia­

important. But it will have to stand on its own legal and factual founda­
tions. That is where the copyright analysis begins.

4.1 — Five meanings of “equivalent”
Mara uploads two manuscripts to a comparison tool. It reports little tex­
tual overlap. She feels that the second book nevertheless reproduces the 
first. The publisher replies that the sentences are new. Both are pointing 
to a genuine feature of the evidence. Neither has yet identified the right 
level of comparison.
We need at least five meanings of equivalence. Lexical equivalence 
concerns words and strings. Expressive similarity concerns the creative 
form in which material is presented, potentially including more than lit­
eral wording. Semantic equivalence concerns what propositions or 
meanings a passage conveys. Functional equivalence concerns what a 
reader can accomplish after using it. Commercial substitutability con­
cerns whether one product displaces demand for another. These are an­
alytical distinctions for this course, not five statutory categories.
Two passages can be semantically similar and expressively different. 
Two manuals can enable the same task while using different examples 
and structures. Two novels can compete for the same evening’s reading 
without sharing any distinctive plot. Conversely, changing every sen­
tence may leave a detailed creative sequence substantially intact. Copy­
right’s exclusion of ideas and methods, together with protection for 
qualifying expression and adaptations, is why the distinction matters. [1, 
3, 8]
Consider a short teaching passage invented here: “A town trusted one 
bell to announce a flood. When the bell stayed silent, the council con­
cluded that the river was safe. No one had checked whether the bell still 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
40
worked.” A second passage says: “The warning siren made no sound, 
so the villagers assumed there was no danger. They had forgotten to test 
its battery.” The words and objects differ, but the miniature scenario 
and instructional turn are close. That observation supports further ex­
amination; it is not an automatic judicial conclusion about a tiny hypo­
thetical text.
A third passage says: “An absent warning supports a reassuring con­
clusion only when the warning mechanism is sufficiently reliable.” It 
conveys the general reasoning lesson without reproducing the little nar­
rative. A fourth develops the lesson through an independent discussion 
of missing laboratory measurements. The progression illustrates why 
“same meaning” is too broad to define infringement and “different 
words” is too narrow to exclude it.
Now scale up from three sentences to a book. Suppose the competitor 
retains forty distinctive examples, their order, their surprising reversals, 
and the relationship between the examples and chapter endings. That 
combination deserves a different analysis from a standard progression 
through introductory definitions, basic examples, and advanced applica­
tions. Conventional teaching order may be constrained by the subject 
itself. Unusual creative organization may carry a different evidential 
and legal significance. The analysis must separate the two rather than 
call every structure proprietary.
A translation provides the simplest warning against word-count theo­
ries. A translated chapter can have almost no identical words while still 
being an adaptation requiring permission, absent an applicable excep­
tion or other legal basis. Copyright law has long addressed transforma­
tions across form and language. An LLM is a new mechanism for trans­
formation, not the inventor of nonliteral copying. [2, 8]
The practical question is therefore not “How much must I change?” It 
is “What am I using, what is protected, and what authorizes this use?” 
A percentage target invites a cosmetic exercise designed around a de­
tector rather than the law. The US Copyright Office expressly warns 
against fixed numerical rules for fair use. A short protected passage can

matter greatly; a long use of unprotected facts raises a different ques­
tion. [57]
For a publisher, the five-way distinction becomes a useful review 
form. Record literal overlap separately from narrative or structural cor­
respondence. Identify shared facts and necessary conventions sepa­
rately from discretionary expression. Assess market substitution sepa­
rately from the legal basis. The result will be more demanding than a 
single similarity score. It will also tell the decision-maker why a close 
match matters—or why an emotionally persuasive allegation may reach 
beyond the right actually held.
4.2 — Fiction, nonfiction, style, and plagiarism
A novelist and a textbook writer can use the same word, “copying,” 
while objecting to different things. The novelist may recognize a dis­
tinctive chain of events and relationships. The textbook writer may see 
a competitor repeat the central explanation of a public scientific princi­
ple. Both may have suffered competition. The legal question is whether 
protected expression, rather than merely subject matter or knowledge, 
was appropriated.
For fiction, the level of abstraction matters. “A stranger arrives in a 
divided town” is an extremely broad premise. A fully developed se­
quence of scenes, revelations, character interactions, and original dia­
logue is much more concrete. Genre conventions do not become one 
writer’s property simply because that writer used them effectively. Nor 
can a detailed adaptation necessarily be made independent by replacing 
names and moving the setting. These are applications of the distinction 
between ideas and expression, not a promise that every court draws the 
line at the same place. [1, 8]
Style is particularly slippery. Short sentences, dry irony, fragmentary 
narration, or a melancholy atmosphere describe broad expressive ten­
dencies. A general request for such qualities does not identify a specific 
protected passage. But a system can produce actual protected expres­
sion while ostensibly being asked only for a style. The legal analysis 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
42
should inspect the output and its relationship to identifiable works, not 
treat the prompt’s label as decisive. Separate identity and endorsement 
concerns may also arise when a real person’s name is used in market­
ing.
Nonfiction raises another boundary. Facts do not become owned 
merely because uncovering them was expensive. In Feist, the US 
Supreme Court rejected labour alone as the basis of copyright and dis­
tinguished unprotected facts from original selection or arrangement. In 
Baker v. Selden, copyright in a book explaining an accounting system 
did not provide exclusive control over using the system. Together, these 
decisions expose why a useful book can create value that copyright 
does not reserve to its author. [54, 55]
Mara cannot own the principle that unreliable measurements require 
caution. She may nevertheless have rights in her particular text and 
other qualifying creative choices. A competing author who investigates 
the underlying subject, consults independent evidence, and creates new 
explanations is in a different position from someone who feeds Mara’s 
chapter to a model and requests a disguised substitute. The latter 
process is evidence of dependence, although dependence alone still 
does not eliminate the need to identify protected material and assess 
any defence.
Plagiarism is a related but different concept. In academic practice, 
presenting another person’s ideas or expression as one’s own can vio­
late attribution norms even when copyright does not prohibit the use. 
Conversely, a person can clearly credit an author and still reproduce 
protected material without permission or an applicable exception. Cita­
tion answers a question about acknowledgment. It does not automati­
cally answer a question about authorization.
Consider a public-domain historical essay. Republishing it with hon­
est identification may be legally permissible, while silently presenting it 
as newly written personal scholarship would be academically deceptive. 
Consider a recent novel. Printing a substantial unauthorized adaptation 
with a prominent thank-you does not become permitted merely because 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
43
the source is acknowledged. One example separates legality from intel­
lectual honesty; the other separates intellectual honesty from legal per­
mission.
Editorial integrity therefore requires two reviews. A rights review 
asks whether uses are authorized or justified. An attribution review asks 
whether the publication accurately represents intellectual debts and 
contributions. A third factual review asks whether the claims are true. 
None can safely substitute for the others. A properly licensed text can 
be false. A truthful text can be unlawfully reproduced. A noninfringing 
summary can misrepresent who developed the ideas.
There is a final trap in nonfiction: copying mistakes. Suppose Mara 
invents a city name in a fictional exercise, and the rival book repeats it 
as historical fact. The match may support an inference of dependence, 
but it is not a magical entitlement to all surrounding facts. It is also a 
warning about epistemic quality. A production process optimized to 
preserve useful-looking meaning can preserve errors with equal effi­
ciency. The task is not merely to make the language independent; it is 
to make the reasoning and evidence genuinely accountable.
4.3 — Training, retrieval, rewriting, and publication are 
separate acts
“Was the book used by AI?” is too coarse a question for serious analy­
sis. A file may be acquired, stored, cleaned, tokenized, included in a 
training dataset, placed in a retrieval index, inserted into a model’s con­
text, summarized, adapted, or published. Different actors may perform 
different steps in different countries. The research framework devel­
oped by Lee, Cooper, and Grimmelmann usefully treats generative AI 
as a supply chain rather than a single indivisible act. [49]
Training generally changes model parameters through a learning 
process. Supplying a book in a prompt does not necessarily train the 
model in that sense. Retrieval-augmented generation, often abbreviated 
RAG, retrieves material from an external collection and supplies se­
lected content to a model when answering. Fine-tuning performs an 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
44
additional learning process on selected data. These technical distinc­
tions matter because the location, purpose, persistence, and exposure of 
copies can differ. A system’s marketing description may conceal those 
differences.
Imagine a publisher with permission to store a manuscript for ordi­
nary editing. An employee uploads it to a third-party service. The ser­
vice uses it only for that session in one configuration, but retains it for 
improvement in another. These are materially different data flows, even 
before assessing the contract and applicable law. A general statement 
that the employee “used an assistant” is not enough to decide which 
rights or confidentiality obligations were implicated.
The same separation works in reverse. A developer could have law­
fully assembled training material and nevertheless operate a system that 
reproduces protected passages in problematic outputs. Another system 
might produce an independently worded answer while relying on un­
lawfully acquired source files. A low output-similarity score does not 
retrospectively authorize acquisition. A lawful input route does not 
prospectively authorize every possible output.
Memorization is not merely a philosophical possibility. Carlini and 
colleagues demonstrated extraction of verbatim training examples from 
GPT-2. Their work establishes a concrete risk under the studied condi­
tions, not that every current model stores every training book as an eas­
ily recoverable archive. A responsible assessment must distinguish a 
demonstrated mechanism from an unsupported universal claim. [44]
The legal debate about training remains sensitive to facts and juris­
diction. The US Copyright Office’s May 2025 pre-publication report on 
training rejects simplistic treatment of all training as one kind of use. It 
discusses purpose, source acquisition, outputs, market effects, and li­
censing. At the research cutoff, the Office’s AI page still identified Part 
3 as a pre-publication version. It is an official policy analysis, not a 
statute or a binding resolution of every pending case. [15, 16]
Litigation developments require equally careful labeling. On 20 July 
2026, the district court granted final approval of the class settlement in

45
Bartz v. Anthropic, involving a $1.5 billion settlement fund and dis­
puted downloading of books from pirate libraries. The order also ad­
dresses destruction of the relevant original files and copies, subject to 
preservation obligations. This is a substantial, concrete resolution of a 
particular dispute. It is not a Supreme Court declaration that all AI 
training is lawful or that all AI training is infringement. [48]
For EU projects, the distinction between the research-oriented and 
broader text-and-data-mining exceptions described in Chapter 2 re­
mains important. Lawful access, purpose, eligible institutions, rights 
reservations, and the particular act must be examined. Separately, the 
AI Act imposes obligations on general-purpose model providers con­

tory duties do not themselves decide whether a specific generated book 
infringes copyright. [21, 22]
The recommended operational response is a data-flow map with 
rights decisions attached. For each stage, record the material, purpose, 
actor, location, retention, access conditions, and intended downstream 
use. Such a map does not guarantee legality. It creates an object that 
lawyers, engineers, editors, and auditors can actually evaluate. Without 
it, organizations tend to argue about the word “AI” while remaining un­
able to say where the manuscript went.
4.4 — Who authored the AI-assisted book?
Mara experiments with three workflows. In the first, she writes a chap­
ter and uses an assistant to flag awkward sentences. In the second, she 
creates a detailed argument, selects evidence, drafts examples, and 
rewrites machine-proposed passages into her own expression. In the 
third, she asks for an entire book and accepts the result with minor cor­
rections. The fact that all three involve a human and a model does not 
make the human contribution identical.
The US Copyright Office’s January 2025 report explains that human-
created expression can remain protected in AI-assisted works, while 
purely generated material or material without sufficient human

46
expressive control does not acquire protection on that basis. Creative 
selection, arrangement, and modification can matter. Prompts alone 
generally do not supply the required control under the systems exam­
ined. The assessment is case-specific and concerns expression, not a 
tally of prompts or hours spent. [14]
The useful question is therefore evidential: what did the human actu­
ally create that is perceptible in the result? A researcher may have con­
tributed an original conceptual framework; a writer may have com­
posed the text expressing it; an editor may have creatively reorganized 
material; a machine may have supplied most of the wording. Those 
contributions can coexist without merging into a claim that one person 
necessarily authored every protected element.
A purchase contract with an AI provider does not settle this threshold 
question. Contractual language can allocate whatever rights the con­
tracting parties possess or promise cooperation concerning them. It can­
not force a jurisdiction to recognize copyright in material that fails the 
legal requirements for protection. Nor can an output-ownership clause 
extinguish third-party rights that the provider never owned.
National differences remain relevant. The United Kingdom has a 
statutory framework addressing certain computer-generated works and 
was considering reform in its March 2026 policy report. That report 
should not be read as if proposed removal or amendment had already 
occurred. A publisher distributing internationally should avoid reducing 
all authorship questions to one US office statement, while still recog­
nizing its importance for US registration and practice. [34]
For evidence, Mara keeps substantive drafts, source notes, revision 
histories, and a record of important editorial decisions. She need not 
preserve every keystroke merely to look human. The objective is to 
show real contributions and permissions, not to manufacture an artifi­
cial performance of effort. A prompt log can help reconstruct a process, 
but quantity is not quality: a thousand instructions to “try again” do not 
necessarily establish authorship of the final expression.

The more uncomfortable question concerns editorial responsibility. 
Even where a publisher cannot claim copyright in every generated 
sentence, it can still be responsible for choosing to distribute the book. 
It can investigate factual claims, check citations, remove misleading 
attributions, obtain necessary permissions, and correct errors. Those 
obligations are not made less important by uncertainty about exclusive 
rights.
This book applies the same principle to itself. Its front matter dis­
closes AI-assisted drafting and does not invent a human author for all 
of its expression. Its educational value should be assessed through the 
arguments, examples, and sources, not through a fictional origin story. 
Substantive human review before publication remains important. A 
book explaining transparency should not conceal its own production 
conditions behind a decorative copyright notice.
The constructive lesson is to design the creative process around gen­
uine intellectual work rather than legal cosmetics. Use assistance where 
it helps. Preserve human decisions where they matter. Describe the re­
sult honestly. Rights can then be assessed against an actual creative 
record, while readers receive a more accurate account of who stands be­
hind the publication.
4.5 — When the substitute is lawful but the damage is 
real
Return to the most difficult version of Mara’s problem. The competitor 
consults lawful sources, does not reproduce protected expression, uses 
an independent brand, and creates a genuinely different explanation. Its 
book nevertheless gives many readers enough of what they wanted 
from hers. Assume, for this thought experiment, that no other legal pro­
hibition applies. Mara loses sales. What exactly should copyright do 
now?
The answer cannot be inferred from the loss alone. Markets regularly 
contain lawful substitutes: a lecture instead of a textbook, a new text­
book instead of an old one, a library loan instead of a purchase, or an 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
48
open tutorial instead of a paid course. A rule prohibiting every effective 
substitute would reserve not merely an expression but much of its sub­
ject matter and practical purpose. The legal system’s distinction be­
tween ideas and expression would become hollow.
AI may make such substitution cheaper and more individualized in 
some settings. The economic concern is not necessarily that every 
model can reproduce every valuable book. It is that a reader may accept 
a lower-cost answer, tailored explanation, or compressed guide that is 
good enough for a particular purpose. The original publisher then com­
petes with something that need not match the book’s full quality to re­
duce demand. This is a scenario analysis, not a measured prediction of 
the entire publishing market.
An invented example clarifies the asymmetry. Suppose producing 
and checking a specialist book costs €40,000. A competing explanation 
costs €1,000 to generate and package, but has not undergone compara­
ble verification. These figures are assumptions. If readers cannot distin­
guish reliability before purchase, the cheaper product may exert price 
pressure disproportionate to its quality. Alternatively, readers may 
value the original author’s care and choose the verified edition. The re­
sult depends on institutions of discovery, evaluation, and trust, not on 
generation cost alone.
The most important productive response may therefore lie outside the 
text itself. A verified edition can offer traceable references, corrections, 
exercises with carefully checked answers, access to the author’s evolv­
ing analysis, and a coherent learning path. Institutions can purchase edi­
torial reliability for students or staff. None guarantees income. Each 
makes the creator’s contribution more observable instead of pretending 
that useful knowledge can be sealed forever inside one wording.
There is also a collective-action problem. If new explanatory systems 
depend on a continuing supply of expensive reporting, research, and 
careful synthesis, a market that pays only for cheap repackaging could 
underfinance the upstream work. Whether this happens, and to what ex­
tent, is an empirical question. It should be investigated through 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
49
production incentives, entry, diversity, quality, and long-term invest­
ment—not asserted from one dramatic example.
Policy responses might include targeted licensing where rights apply, 
collective arrangements with appropriate governance, public or philan­
thropic funding for socially valuable works, institutional subscriptions, 
and support for open educational resources. These mechanisms do dif­
ferent things. Licensing compensates authorized uses of rights. Public 
funding can support work whose social value exceeds what individual 
readers will pay. Neither should be advertised as a universal cure.
Mara therefore needs two diagnoses. The first asks whether a com­
petitor unlawfully used her protected expression or misled readers. The 
second asks whether her business remains sustainable under lawful sub­
stitution. Litigation may address the first and fail to address the second. 
Commercial adaptation may address the second without excusing the 
first. Keeping the diagnoses separate is not indifference to creators. It is 
a prerequisite for choosing a remedy that could actually work.

5.1 — Software has more than one identity
Adrian compares two screens. Both applications import research pa­
pers, connect claims to sources, and export an evidence report. The re­
semblance is commercially significant. It tells him almost nothing, by 
itself, about whether the underlying source code was copied. To investi­
gate that question, he must separate the program’s function from its im­
plementation, documentation, interface assets, data, and identity.
Internationally, TRIPS treats computer programs in source or object 
code as literary works. The EU Software Directive protects the expres­
sion of a computer program while excluding ideas and principles under­
lying its elements, including interfaces. These rules do not turn every 
useful algorithm or capability into copyright property. They also do not 
reduce software protection to identical strings alone. [3, 20]
An implementation contains choices at several levels. There are indi­
vidual expressions and statements, functions and modules, internal 
structures, external interfaces, and larger organizational patterns. Some 
choices reflect creative discretion. Others follow from an algorithm, ef­
ficiency, a platform, a standard, or compatibility requirements. Before 
comparing two programs legally, an analyst must understand which is 
which.
Computer Associates v. Altai developed the influential US abstrac­
tion–filtration–comparison approach. In simplified terms, the analysis 
moves through levels of program structure, filters out elements that 
should not receive copyright protection, and compares the remaining 
protectable material. It is a judicial method developed in a particular 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
51
legal setting, not a universal algorithm that automatically decides all 
software cases. Its lasting educational value is the insistence on filtering 
before treating resemblance as wrongful appropriation. [56]
Consider a function that sorts records by date. Both programs may 
use a familiar algorithm and expose an input parameter called “date.” 
Those similarities can arise from the task rather than copying of pro­
tected expression. Now suppose both contain a long unusual comment, 
a distinctive arrangement of unnecessary helper functions, and the same 
idiosyncratic error message. The second set of correspondences raises 
different evidential questions. Neither set should be judged without 
technical context.
A modern application also contains assets that do not become unpro­
tected merely because they are packaged with code. An original illus­
tration, a substantial manual, an explanatory video, or a distinctive set 
of written tutorials requires its own analysis. A developer may be free 
to implement a function yet not free to copy the manual that explains it. 
Reimplementing the engine does not clear every component of the 
product.
Patents and trade secrets occupy another layer. Independent develop­
ment is not a general defence to patent infringement where a valid ap­
plicable patent covers the implementation. Trade-secret law can pro­
hibit improper acquisition or use of confidential material, while pre­
serving routes such as independent discovery and certain lawful obser­
vation or testing under the relevant rules. Contractual restrictions can 
add further questions. Copyright’s limits therefore do not establish a 
universal freedom-to-operate opinion. [27, 58]
Adrian’s first useful document is an asset map. It identifies source 
code, third-party libraries, user-interface artwork, documentation, 
datasets, brands, confidential know-how, and any relevant patent inter­
ests. Each asset has a source, an owner or claimant, permissions, and a 
proposed use. This map is not bureaucracy for its own sake. Without it, 
a team may spend weeks comparing source code while overlooking a 
copied tutorial or an unauthorized logo.

The conceptual lesson is that a software product is not a single legal 
object. It is a bundle of interacting objects, behaviours, and 
relationships. AI-generated code does not collapse those layers into one 
new category. It makes good separation more important because a 
model can mix conventions, remembered fragments, new expression, 
and third-party identifiers in one apparently coherent answer.
5.2 — Interoperability, APIs, and the limits of reimple­
mentation
Imagine that Adrian’s application is the only tool that can read a cus­
tomer’s archive. The customer wants a competing tool to import the 
same files. Should Adrian’s copyright necessarily entitle him to prevent 
that? The question concerns not only investment in software but also 
control over the user’s accumulated work. Interoperability can therefore 
produce a conflict between an incumbent’s interests and the public 
value of competition and portability.
In SAS Institute v. World Programming, the Court of Justice of the 
European Union held that a program’s functionality, programming lan­
guage, and data-file formats used to exploit functions were not forms of 
expression protected as computer programs under the Software Direc­
tive. The Court’s official summary also emphasizes lawful observation 
and study, while preserving the possibility of infringement through pro­
tected material from a manual. The facts included the absence of access 
to the original source code. [19]
This is not a licence to copy every file format specification verbatim, 
misuse confidential material, or ignore other rights. It illustrates a nar­
rower principle: protecting the expression of a program is not the same 
as controlling every functional means by which another program can 
work with it. The exact source of information and the implementation 
process remain important.
The US Supreme Court’s Google v. Oracle decision reached a differ­
ent legal question through fair use. Assuming without deciding that the 
relevant material was copyrightable, the Court found the particular 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
53
reuse of Java API declaring code at issue to be fair use. The decision 
concerned approximately 11,500 lines used in a new computing envi­
ronment. It did not declare all APIs everywhere uncopyrightable or au­
thorize wholesale copying of arbitrary implementation code. [18]
The distinction between these cases is educational. A result can fol­
low because something is outside the protected subject matter, because 
the allegedly copied elements are unprotected, or because an exception 
permits a use even though protected material is involved. These routes 
should not be merged into the slogan “interfaces are free.” Their condi­
tions and consequences differ.
EU software law also distinguishes observation or testing during au­
thorized acts from decompilation for interoperability. The latter excep­
tion has conditions, including indispensability and limits on the infor­
mation obtained and its use. It is not an unrestricted right to reconstruct 
a competitor’s product for any purpose. The enforceability of conflict­
ing contractual provisions also requires attention to the Directive’s 
mandatory rules. [20]
For Adrian, the practical question becomes how to document a legiti­
mate implementation process. A lawful public specification may pro­
vide an adequate starting point. Authorized testing can identify input-
output behaviour. A team can write its own implementation and docu­
mentation, record the reasons for compatibility choices, and avoid im­
porting proprietary source or confidential notes. This is a proposed evi­
dence-conscious process, not a statutory safe harbour.
A clean-room arrangement separates the people examining permitted 
reference material from those implementing an independently ex­
pressed specification. Its value lies in reducing exposure and clarifying 
provenance. It does not legalize an unlawfully obtained specification or 
neutralize patents, contractual restrictions, or protected expressive ma­
terial passed through the supposed barrier. An LLM that has seen un­
known training data adds uncertainty rather than making the barrier 
perfect.

One especially weak practice is to place proprietary source code into 
a model and ask it to rewrite the code “so that it is legally independent.” 
A request of that form demonstrates neither independence nor permis­
sion. The resulting program may preserve protected structure, may be 
an unauthorized transformation, and may expose confidential material 
to a service. Changing the tool used for copying does not answer the le­
gal question.
A stronger engineering aim is narrower and testable: implement a de­
fined lawful interface with independently developed code, preserve evi­
dence of permitted inputs, and review actual correspondences before re­
lease. That approach accepts uncertainty without surrendering legiti­
mate interoperability. The point is not to make competition impossible 
to challenge. It is to make the reasons supporting lawful competition in­
telligible.
5.3 — Open source is permission with terms, not aban­
doned ownership
A coding agent suggests a useful function. It runs. The team copies it 
into its repository and moves on. Later, someone finds a similar imple­
mentation in a public project. At this stage, two bad reactions are com­
mon: assuming that everything public is unrestricted, or assuming that 
any resemblance makes the entire company subject to an incompatible 
licence. Neither reaction begins with the facts.
Open-source licensing generally operates through permissions 
granted by rights holders, subject to the relevant licence’s conditions. 
Public availability alone does not provide those permissions. Con­
versely, a valid open-source licence can authorize substantial uses, in­
cluding commercial uses, that would otherwise require individual nego­
tiation. The actual text and the manner of use matter more than the in­
formal label “open.” [40, 41, 42, 43]
The MIT licence grants broad permissions while requiring preserva­
tion of the copyright and permission notices in copies or substantial 
portions. Its warranty disclaimer is also important. Apache License 2.0 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
55
adds more detailed provisions, including conditions concerning modifi­
cations and notices, an express patent licence with defined scope and 
termination conditions, and a limitation concerning trademarks. These 
are primary licence texts, not evidence that every project using their 
names has a clean ownership history. [40, 41]
GPL version 3 takes a different approach to certain covered distribu­
tions or conveyances: the relevant permissions are tied to copyleft re­
quirements, including provision of corresponding source in the circum­
stances defined by the licence. This is not a rule that learning a tech­
nique from GPL software makes every later implementation GPL. The 
protected material used, whether a covered or derivative work is in­
volved, and the mode of distribution need examination. [42]
AGPL version 3 adds a network-interaction provision for modified 
versions of the covered program. Describing this as “all SaaS must re­
veal its code” is inaccurate. The question is whether the licence’s actual 
conditions apply to the particular covered work and modification. A re­
mote interaction with an unrelated service does not magically expand 
the licence to every program in the organization. [43]
The lesson for LLM-generated code is neither panic nor licence laun­
dering. A model’s removal of a header does not remove obligations at­
tached to protected material that has been copied or adapted. But inde­
pendently implementing an unprotected function does not acquire an­
other implementation’s licence merely because the two solve the same 
problem. Establish the relationship before choosing the compliance re­
sponse.
A useful review begins with a suspected fragment. Locate plausible 
sources and inspect their licences at the relevant version. Determine 
whether the similarity concerns banal syntax, a standard algorithm, or a 
more distinctive implementation. Check what the project actually incor­
porated and distributed. Escalate ambiguous cases instead of either con­
cealing them or announcing that the whole repository is contaminated. 
Replacement with genuinely independent code can be appropriate, but a 
cosmetic second rewrite is not automatically independent.

Licence compatibility also matters when combining components. A 
permissive label is not a guarantee that all obligations disappear; a 
copyleft label is not an explanation of every boundary case. Organiza­
tions should maintain an approved component policy, a review route for 
unfamiliar licences, and accurate notices for the release being shipped. 
The policy should be practical enough that developers can follow it 
without inventing informal exceptions under deadline pressure.
Finally, copyright permission and trademark permission should re­
main separate. An open-source project may authorize modification of 
its code while retaining control over the source-identifying name or 
logo. A fork may need a new identity to avoid suggesting official main­
tenance. This is not a contradiction. It permits technical freedom while 
helping users identify who is responsible for a particular distribution. 
[40]
For Adrian, good open-source practice can be a competitive advan­
tage. Customers may value a company that knows what it ships, can 
produce its notices, and can explain the provenance of critical compo­
nents. That confidence comes from a maintained process, not from 
claiming that the AI provider made all licensing questions disappear.
5.4 — Testing equivalence is not testing independence
Adrian commissions two evaluations of the rival program. One asks 
whether it behaves like his application. The other asks whether it repro­
duces protected code. It would be convenient if one test answered both 
questions. It does not. Behavioural equivalence concerns what pro­
grams do; copyright analysis concerns the protected material and 
legally relevant acts. A program can behave differently because it is a 
buggy copy. Another can behave identically on a defined task because 
it is independently implemented.
Even behavioural equivalence requires a specification. Does “same” 
include error messages, timing, memory use, ordering of equal records, 
treatment of malformed input, network effects, and persistence after 
failure? Two functions can agree on normal examples and diverge at a 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
57
boundary. A useful comparison states which observations matter, over 
which input domain, and under which environmental assumptions.
Suppose both applications sort evidence records by date. The test 
suite contains one hundred well-formed records with distinct dates. 
Both pass. The suite says nothing about duplicate dates, missing dates, 
time zones, extremely large files, or corrupted encodings. This is not a 
criticism of testing; it is an explanation of what the test result estab­
lishes. A statement such as “equivalent for the tested cases” is evidence. 
“Equivalent in all circumstances” requires much more.
There cannot be a general terminating procedure that decides com­
plete behavioural equivalence for every arbitrary program in a suffi­
ciently expressive programming system. A brief reasoning argument 
shows why. Compare a program that immediately returns zero with one 
that first simulates an arbitrary computation and returns zero only if that 
computation halts. A perfect equivalence decider would tell us whether 
the simulated computation halts. This connects the unrestricted equiva­
lence problem to the halting problem. Restricted languages, bounded 
domains, and specified properties can still admit useful verification.
Legal independence is harder to mechanize in a different way. Tex­
tual matching can reveal literal fragments. Structural comparison can 
reveal correspondence after renaming or reordering. Neither tool de­
cides whether the matching material is protected, whether copying oc­
curred, whether permission exists, or whether an exception applies. A 
detection system should therefore produce evidence with explanations 
rather than an unsupported binary verdict.
A proposed review pipeline starts with exact and approximate 
matches, then groups candidates by technical context. A common gen­
erated boilerplate function should not receive the same risk score as a 
distinctive copied comment and implementation sequence. Reviewers 
record the source, licence, similarity, functional constraints, and dispo­
sition. High-risk unresolved components can be quarantined before re­
lease. The pipeline improves consistency; it does not eliminate the need 
for judgment.


THE RIGHT TO COPY
58
A software bill of materials helps answer a different question: what 
components are present? SPDX provides standardized ways to 
represent information about software components and licensing. Such 
metadata supports inventory and exchange, but it does not prove that 
every ownership assertion is true or every missing component has been 
identified. A beautifully formatted inventory can still be incomplete. 
[52]
Signed commits and release signatures can help establish integrity 
and identify the key used to approve a version. They do not, by them­
selves, prove that the signer authored the code or had authority to li­
cense it. A reliable record should connect technical artifacts with con­
tributor agreements, review decisions, and actual organizational respon­
sibilities. Cryptography can protect the record from certain alterations; 
it cannot fill an empty evidential field with truth.
The final separation is between legal and engineering quality. A fully 
licensed program can contain a severe security defect. An indepen­
dently implemented program can corrupt data. A technically excellent 
program can carry unresolved licence obligations. Adrian needs release 
gates for all three questions: rights, behaviour, and security. Allowing 
one successful test to stand in for the others is an attractive shortcut pre­
cisely because the missing failures are initially invisible.
5.5 — What remains valuable when code becomes 
cheaper?
Assume that AI assistance substantially reduces the cost of producing a 
first implementation of some feature. That assumption does not estab­
lish that the entire product has become cheap. A feature must fit re­
quirements, integrate with existing systems, survive unusual inputs, 
protect data, remain maintainable, and be corrected when conditions 
change. For some products those costs may dominate; for others a 
cheap substitute may be enough. The responsible conclusion is differ­
entiated rather than triumphalist.


THE RIGHT TO COPY
59
Adrian should therefore ask what customers actually buy. They may 
buy code access, but they may also buy operational reliability, 
migration help, support, accountable handling of incidents, 
documentation, integrations, or a continuing compatibility promise. 
Some of these contributions are protectable assets; others are services 
or organizational capabilities. They should not be collapsed into the 
phrase “our intellectual property.”
A hypothetical customer comparison makes the point. Product A is 
cheaper at launch but has no tested recovery procedure. Product B is 
more expensive but comes with maintained exports, reproducible re­
leases, and a team responsible for restoring service. A customer per­
forming a trivial personal task may rationally choose A. A laboratory 
whose evidence archive supports years of work may rationally choose 
B. Neither choice proves that copyright is obsolete. It shows that differ­
ent users value different bundles.
The maintenance argument should not become an excuse for unnec­
essary dependence. A supplier can create apparent value by making mi­
gration painful, concealing file formats, or attaching obscure restric­
tions to ordinary data access. Some such arrangements may raise sepa­
rate legal questions; even when permitted, they deserve ethical scrutiny. 
A business model based on customers’ inability to leave is different 
from one based on customers’ confidence that staying is worthwhile.
Open standards and interoperability can support another equilibrium. 
Several firms may implement the same basic capability while compet­
ing on verification, support, and specialized features. Shared compo­
nents can reduce duplicated effort. The corresponding danger is under­
funding common infrastructure if every beneficiary expects someone 
else to maintain it. Financing mechanisms—support contracts, shared 
foundations, member contributions, or institutional procurement—
should be assessed against that problem rather than assumed to arise au­
tomatically.
Adrian’s practical plan can therefore combine selective protection 
and openness. He may keep some legally protectable code proprietary,

60
publish a stable interchange format, use open-source components with 
proper compliance, and offer paid support. Another company may 
choose a fully open model. These are strategic options, not universal 
prescriptions. Their viability depends on the product, customer base, 
maintenance burden, and incentives of contributors.
When facing a suspected clone, he should pursue a proportionate in­
vestigation. Preserve the released versions and public claims. Identify 
the specific code or other assets allegedly copied. Distinguish func­
tional similarity from expressive correspondence. Check his own chain 
of title before asserting rights. Review possible permissions and inde­
pendent-development explanations. Then choose a response: technical 
clarification, a negotiated licence, correction of branding, replacement 
of a component, or formal proceedings where justified.
The important discovery is that code generation and software produc­
tion are not identical activities. Copyright can still matter greatly to 
code and documentation, but it cannot guarantee a monopoly over a 
useful result. A resilient software organization knows which part of its 
advantage rests on exclusive rights and which part rests on doing diffi­
cult work well. AI makes that accounting more urgent; it does not per­
form the accounting on the organization’s behalf.

6.1 — Start with a rights ledger, not a prohibition banner
Mara considers adding “No AI use of any kind” to every page. Adrian 
considers a policy banning all coding assistants. Either measure might 
express a preference. Neither, by itself, tells the organization what it 
owns, what others may lawfully do, or what employees actually need to 
accomplish. The first practical solution is less dramatic: inventory the 
rights and the uses before deciding where to impose controls.
A rights ledger is a proposed operational record, not a new statutory 
register. For each asset, it identifies the version, creator or source, 
claimed owner, supporting agreement, permitted uses, relevant territo­
ries, restrictions, expiry or review date, and unresolved questions. The 
ledger distinguishes verified ownership from a contributor’s unsup­
ported assertion. It also records when a work is used under an exception 
rather than a licence. A green “cleared” label without scope is not 
enough.
For Mara’s book, one entry might cover her manuscript, another a 
commissioned cover, another a licensed photograph, and another an ex­
tract used for criticism. The entries should not imply that permission for 
the photograph covers the photographer’s subject’s identity rights, or 
that an ebook licence necessarily covers a translated audiobook. Sepa­
rate assets and separate uses require separate reasoning. The ledger 
makes those distinctions visible before a new format is released.
For Adrian, the same structure can cover employee code, contractor 
contributions, dependencies, documentation, datasets, and trademarks. 
It can record the evidence supporting the company’s entitlement to

62
release each component. The implementation need not be elaborate: a 
small team can begin with a controlled document or repository file. The 
quality lies in the accuracy and maintenance of the information, not in 
the sophistication of the database.
An AI-processing agreement should describe activities rather than 
rely only on a sweeping word such as “use.” Internal editing, temporary 
retrieval, fine-tuning, general model training, creation of derivative edi­
tions, public outputs, and sublicensing are materially different. The par­
ties can negotiate retention, deletion, access controls, permitted 
providers, reporting, audit, and responsibility for claims. Whether par­
ticular terms are enforceable remains a question of applicable law and 
mandatory limits.
Risk allocation should also be readable. An indemnity is a contractual 
allocation of specified losses; it is not proof that a use is lawful. Its 
value depends on coverage, exclusions, procedure, limits, and the coun­
terparty’s ability to perform. A small publisher should not assume that a 
reassuring sentence in a service description will finance every dispute 
about a generated book. Likewise, a developer should not promise cus­
tomers more protection than its upstream contracts and actual process 
support.
The release decision can use three internal states. “Approved” means 
approved for a stated use on stated evidence. “Conditional” means 
specified obligations remain, such as notices or a restricted distribution 
route. “Unresolved” means the material cannot pass the relevant release 
gate without further review. These are proposed management cate­
gories, not legal determinations. Their advantage is that uncertainty be­
comes work to complete rather than a vague anxiety passed to the next 
team.
A final record should identify the accountable person and review trig­
ger. A change in model configuration, a new distribution territory, a 
newly discovered source match, or a new edition may require reassess­
ment. This prevents yesterday’s permission from silently becoming a 
perpetual approval for every future product. Good governance is not the

elimination of uncertainty. It is the ability to say where uncertainty re­
mains and who must act when the facts change.
6.2 — Provenance, signatures, and the limits of technical 
protection
A hash can answer a narrow question extremely well: does this file 
match the bytes represented by this digest, assuming the chosen crypto­
graphic function behaves as intended? It cannot answer who created the 
file or who owns its copyright. A person can hash someone else’s man­
uscript. A timestamp can support a claim that particular data existed by 
a certain time, within its trust assumptions; it does not prove original 
authorship. The same limits persist when the record is placed on a 
blockchain.
This is not an argument against cryptography. It is an argument for 
assigning it the right job. Integrity, authenticated assertions, and 
durable chronology are useful components of an evidence system. Le­
gal ownership requires additional facts about creation, employment, 
transfers, licences, and applicable law. A mathematically valid signa­
ture on a false ownership assertion remains a valid signature on a false 
assertion.
C2PA provides a technical architecture for cryptographically verifi­
able provenance information. Its trust model concerns signed claims 
and the identity or credentials associated with a signer; a consumer still 
needs to evaluate the assertions. This book cites version 2.2 as a con­
crete specification, not as a claim that it is the newest release or a uni­
versal copyright registry. Provenance can improve interpretation with­
out certifying that the underlying content is true or lawfully owned. [46]
For Mara, useful provenance might connect a released edition to its 
editorial history and approved publisher. For Adrian, it might connect a 
release to source revisions, component inventories, and review ap­
provals. These records should be designed for a real dispute: can the or­
ganization explain the chain, reproduce the relevant version, and show 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
64
which claims were independently verified? A decorative authenticity 
badge with no accessible evidence is much weaker.
Rights-reservation signals solve a different problem. The W3C-
hosted TDM Reservation Protocol community has developed machine-
readable mechanisms intended to communicate reservations and licens­
ing routes. Such signals can help automated actors discover an ex­
pressed position. Community specifications are not themselves legisla­
tion, and a signal’s legal effect depends on the relevant rule and circum­
stances. It is not a universal ban on every analytical activity. [47]
The Robots Exclusion Protocol makes another boundary explicit: 
crawler instructions are not access authorization or a security mecha­
nism. Publishing a robots rule can communicate a preference to cooper­
ative crawlers, but it does not prevent a determined person from re­
questing a public resource. Conversely, the absence of a robots prohibi­
tion does not establish that all copyright uses are licensed. Security con­
trols, legal permissions, and machine-readable preferences are separate 
layers. [45]
Watermarking and fingerprinting should be assessed against a stated 
threat model. Exact fingerprints are useful for exact copies. They fail by 
design when different bytes are compared. More tolerant matching can 
detect some transformations but may introduce false positives. A water­
mark intended to survive paraphrase needs evidence that it does so un­
der the relevant transformations; its mere presence in a product descrip­
tion proves little. No particular watermark is endorsed here as a general 
solution to semantic rewriting.
Monitoring also has costs. Scanning private manuscripts or propri­
etary repositories through an external service can expose sensitive ma­
terial. Keeping every prompt indefinitely can increase confidentiality 
and privacy risk. A sensible design minimizes unnecessary collection, 
restricts access, separates public provenance from confidential evi­
dence, and records retention decisions. The aim is proportionate ac­
countability, not universal surveillance of creative work.

The practical package is therefore layered: clear notices, appropriate 
reservation signals, controlled access where warranted, versioned 
evidence, limited matching tools, reviewable claims, and an 
accountable response process. No layer turns an idea into property. 
Together, they can make unauthorized copying harder to hide and 
authorized collaboration easier to document without pretending that a 
cryptographic primitive has solved a legal question.
6.3 — Licensing and compensation: useful mechanisms, 
hard allocation problems
A licensing market is attractive because it can replace uncertain con­
frontation with permission and payment. A publisher grants specified 
uses; a model developer obtains a defined corpus; both record the 
scope. The basic mechanism is familiar. The difficult questions concern 
who can license which rights, what uses require permission, how prices 
are negotiated, and whether small creators can participate on workable 
terms.
Direct licensing may suit a distinctive catalogue with clear ownership 
and substantial demand. It can also impose transaction costs that make 
very small deals impractical. Collective arrangements may reduce those 
costs by representing many rights holders. Yet aggregation creates gov­
ernance questions: membership, representation, repertoire accuracy, 
withdrawal, distribution rules, and accountability. These mechanisms 
should be evaluated as institutions, not applauded merely because they 
contain the word “compensation.”
A creator may value different permissions differently. Training a 
general model, operating a controlled educational search service, creat­
ing an authorized adaptation, and selling excerpts can affect the cre­
ator’s plans in different ways. A single undifferentiated price may con­
ceal these differences. A well-designed negotiation separates purposes 
and material, while respecting uses that do not require a licence under 
applicable law.


THE RIGHT TO COPY
66
Compensation for training inputs also faces an attribution problem. 
Suppose a model produces an explanation of a familiar scientific 
principle after learning from thousands of overlapping sources. Which 
source contributed how much? Similarity to an output is not necessarily 
causal contribution to learning. Causal contribution is not necessarily 
legal entitlement. Legal entitlement is not necessarily a complete 
account of what society ought to reward. These are three different 
allocation questions.
Wang and colleagues propose a contribution-based compensation 
framework using the probabilistic structure of generative models and 
cooperative-game-theoretic ideas. This is a research proposal, not an 
enacted royalty system or a demonstrated universal solution for allocat­
ing payments across the world’s books and software. Its importance lies 
in making the allocation question explicit and amenable to technical in­
vestigation. [50]
Several conceptual difficulties remain. A foundational source may in­
fluence many later works while being rarely reflected in literal output. 
Two sources may provide nearly interchangeable information. A low-
quality source may increase a model’s tendency to reproduce an error. 
A contributor may inflate a corpus with duplicates to seek additional 
payment. Any mechanism should be tested for such incentives rather 
than assume that a plausible formula will produce fairness by itself.
There is also a bargaining-power issue. A market in which a few ma­
jor catalogues can negotiate and everyone else faces opaque take-it-or-
leave-it terms may improve compensation unevenly. Conversely, a li­
censing rule so expensive or administratively complex that only the 
largest developers can comply may strengthen incumbents. A balanced 
design should measure transaction costs, access for smaller actors, and 
the concentration effects of its compliance requirements.
Open licensing offers another route when creators deliberately 
choose broad reuse. Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 permits uses 
under its conditions, including attribution and indication of changes 
where required. It does not license every separate right associated with

a work or permit a licensor to grant rights it does not hold. Its permis­
sions cannot simply be withdrawn from compliant recipients because a 
later use becomes commercially inconvenient. [51]
A creator can therefore choose different models for different assets: 
open foundational material, paid verified editions, controlled licences 
for selected adaptations, or institutional funding for public access. That 
portfolio may serve some projects well and others poorly. The impor­
tant point is informed choice. Compensation policy should not assume 
that every creator wants maximum exclusivity, while open-culture pol­
icy should not assume that every creator can finance substantial work 
without income.
6.4 — Enforcement that protects creators without au­
tomating censorship
Mara finds a suspicious book. The first impulse is to demand immedi­
ate removal from every platform. Sometimes a rapid response is justi­
fied, especially where the evidence is clear and harm is ongoing. But a 
reliable process must distinguish an exact unauthorized copy from a 
lawful review, an independent explanation, or a misidentified public-
domain edition. A system designed only for speed can amplify the 
claimant’s mistake.
A practical evidence packet should identify the claimant and basis of 
authority, the original work and relevant version, the allegedly infring­
ing material, specific correspondences, and the requested remedy. It 
should state why the correspondences concern protected expression 
rather than merely a shared topic. For a trademark complaint, it should 
explain the protected sign and the misleading presentation. These are 
proposed evidential standards for organizational practice, not a substi­
tute for the formal requirements of a particular procedure.
US law includes the section 512 notice-and-counter-notice frame­
work and related limitations on liability for qualifying service 
providers. The statute also addresses knowing material misrepresenta­
tions in the relevant notices. This is not a worldwide automatic-removal

68
rule, nor does a platform’s takedown decision conclusively determine 
infringement. Users and claimants must consider the actual procedure 
and consequences before acting. [12]
EU enforcement rules likewise require attention to effectiveness, fair­
ness, and proportionality. A remedy should address the established 
problem without casually eliminating legitimate trade or imposing un­
necessary barriers. The Enforcement Directive supplies a framework 
that operates through national implementation and procedure; it is not a 
universal litigation script. [28]
Automation can assist by organizing evidence, locating copies, clus­
tering notices, and checking whether required fields are present. It 
should not be entrusted with an unexplained final determination that all 
semantically similar text is unlawful. A summary of a scientific princi­
ple may resemble many sources because they describe the same princi­
ple. A model trained to find “equivalents” may be excellent at detecting 
the wrong kind of similarity for a legal claim.
False positives have a distributional cost. A large publisher may be 
able to challenge a mistaken removal. A small educator may abandon 
the work because contesting the decision takes too much time. False 
negatives have an equally real cost for creators whose work is copied at 
scale. The solution is not to pretend that one error type is harmless. It is 
to design review, explanation, appeal, and escalation around both.
The remedy can also be narrower than deletion. An incorrect affilia­
tion claim may require correction. A missing licence notice may require 
compliance and legal review of any other consequences. A copied mod­
ule may be replaced or licensed. A disputed excerpt may justify negoti­
ation over its scope. Some cases will still require injunctions or dam­
ages. Precision increases the chance that the response solves the actual 
problem instead of merely punishing the opposing party.
For Adrian, a sensible escalation ladder begins with preservation and 
technical assessment, then moves to a clear private inquiry where ap­
propriate, qualified legal review, and formal action if justified. He 
should avoid public accusations unsupported by evidence. He should

also check his own rights before demanding exclusivity over a third-
party component or a public functional convention.
The institutional goal is contestable enforcement: a person affected 
by a decision should be able to understand its basis and present relevant 
contrary evidence. This is a recommendation for responsible systems, 
not a statement that every platform presently offers such a process. It 
protects creators by improving the quality of their claims and protects 
users by preventing intellectual property from becoming an opaque in­
strument of removal.
6.5 — What policy should protect, and what it should 
leave free
The strongest case for intervention is not “a creator lost revenue,” but a 
more specific account of the behaviour, the right, the incentive, and the 
public consequence. Unauthorized copying of protected expression, 
misuse of confidential material, and deceptive branding can justify re­
sponses under existing legal frameworks. Lawful learning and indepen­
dent implementation require different treatment. A policy that refuses 
this distinction risks punishing the very activity that copyright leaves 
available.
This book’s normative position is against a general monopoly over 
semantic equivalence. Such a monopoly would be difficult to define 
and potentially broad enough to control explanations, translations of 
ideas into practice, independent tutorials, and compatible software. It 
could allow the first persuasive account of a subject to constrain later 
accounts of the subject itself. A rule intended to protect creators could 
become a tax on understanding.
That position does not imply that all training or all rewriting must be 
free. Where protected acts require permission and no exception applies, 
authorization remains necessary. Where an adaptation preserves pro­
tected expression, changing words is not a complete answer. Where 
output impersonates a source, trademark and other rules may matter. 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
70
Defending the boundary around rights requires taking the rights inside 
that boundary seriously.
The more promising reforms are narrower. Improve access to rele­
vant evidence without requiring public disclosure of every trade secret 
or private manuscript. Support interoperable rights information without 
converting optional metadata into a new formality for copyright exis­
tence. Facilitate licensing where rights apply while preserving research 
and lawful competition. Make remedies usable for smaller creators 
without depriving smaller defendants of a meaningful chance to re­
spond.
Public financing belongs in the conversation because some valuable 
production may not be well funded by exclusive sales alone. Libraries, 
universities, professional institutions, public grants, and philanthropic 
organizations can support research, translation, preservation, and edu­
cational work. The design questions concern independence, selection 
quality, accountability, diversity, and capture. Public support is not au­
tomatically wise; private exclusivity is not automatically sufficient.
Any proposed compulsory or extended licensing regime would re­
quire explicit legal design, not a technical platform declaring itself au­
thorized. It would need to address scope, eligibility, rates, governance, 
distribution, exceptions, oversight, and international obligations. A con­
tribution-scoring system cannot enact those choices. At most, it can in­
form one part of the administration after legitimate rules exist.
Evaluation should focus on outcomes rather than the number of docu­
ments generated or licences signed. Are high-quality new works still 
being produced? Can new entrants compete? Are small creators receiv­
ing usable compensation? Can researchers access necessary material? 
Are readers better informed about source and reliability? Are disputes 
resolved more accurately and proportionately? These are proposed 
evaluation questions that make competing policy claims testable.
The difficult tradeoff is between preserving incentives and preserving 
the freedom to build on knowledge. Excessively weak protection can 
reward appropriation and undercut costly work. Excessively broad

71
protection can entrench incumbents and make ordinary learning legally 
hazardous. The objective should be a system in which creating, sharing, 
criticizing, adapting with permission, and independently improving all 
remain viable activities—not a system that maximizes one side’s con­
trol at any cost.
6.6 — A ninety-day programme for a small publisher or 
software team
An organization does not need to solve international copyright policy 
before improving its own practice. It needs a sequence of concrete deci­
sions. The following programme is a proposed operating plan for a 
small team, not a compliance certification. Its timescale is illustrative 
and should be adapted to the complexity and risk of the work.
During the first month, establish the asset and use inventory. Mara’s 
team identifies manuscript versions, illustrations, quotations, publishing 
contracts, and AI-processing routes. Adrian’s team identifies contribu­
tors, dependencies, code-generation practices, documentation, datasets, 
and marks. Both select an accountable coordinator. They distinguish 
existing evidence from assumptions and stop obviously risky practices 
such as uploading confidential third-party material without a reviewed 
basis.
The first month’s output is not a perfectly clean archive. It is a priori­
tized map of gaps. Missing contractor assignments, uncertain image li­
cences, unexplained code fragments, and unverified branding claims 
should become specific tasks. High-impact issues receive attention be­
fore low-impact administrative refinements. A team that discovers a 
problem should preserve evidence and obtain advice rather than quietly 
erase the history.
During the second month, build a workable release process. Establish 
which inputs may enter which AI services, under what configurations, 
and for which purposes. Add a rights review for new book editions and 
a component review for software releases. Create a record of required 
attributions and notices. Review the chosen marks in the intended 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
72
markets. Make the editorial or engineering contribution visible through 
sources, tests, corrections, and named responsibilities.
The process should be tested on actual examples. Give the reviewers 
a public-domain text in a newly protected translation, a short attributed 
but unauthorized extract, a clearly independent explanation, a copied 
code comment, and a truthful compatibility statement. Ask them to ex­
plain why the cases differ. A policy that only works when the answer is 
obvious will fail when teams most need guidance.
During the third month, test the dispute and correction routes. Simu­
late a complaint from an author, a licence concern from a customer, and 
a misleading official-product claim on a marketplace. Can the team re­
trieve the relevant version and permissions? Can it distinguish a factual 
allegation from a legal conclusion? Can it correct a genuine problem 
without making unsupported admissions about unrelated issues? A re­
hearsal exposes missing evidence more cheaply than a real dispute.
Measure practical indicators: the proportion of critical assets with re­
viewed provenance, unresolved issues at release, time needed to recon­
struct a version’s rights basis, accuracy of required notices, and time 
taken to correct verified errors. These are internal management mea­
sures, not proof of noninfringement. Avoid a target such as “zero simi­
larity,” which can reward pointless rewriting rather than lawful and use­
ful production.
At the end of the programme, revisit the business model. Which cus­
tomer value would survive a lawful independently produced substitute? 
Which value depends on verifiable quality, support, original investiga­
tion, or continuing maintenance? Which restrictions merely compensate 
for weak product design? These questions are commercially uncomfort­
able, but avoiding them can lead a company to overestimate what litiga­
tion will achieve.
The best achievable outcome is not invulnerability. It is a more de­
fensible institution: aware of its rights, honest about uncertainty, careful 
with other people’s work, capable of proving its own contributions, and 
prepared to compete where competition is lawful. That is a realistic

solution to many avoidable problems. It is not a promise to restore a 
world in which useful expression was expensive to reproduce.

Seminar — Four cases that resist 
easy answers
The purpose of this seminar is to practice separating questions before 
answering them. Each situation is fictional. The analyses are teaching 
approaches, not predictions of a particular court’s decision. The rele­
vant doctrinal sources are identified in the corresponding chapters. A 
good answer states the missing facts that could change the result; it 
does not simply repeat that “it depends.”
Case A: the private book tutor. A university wants students to ask 
questions about a recent textbook. It owns printed copies. A contractor 
proposes scanning the book, placing it in a retrieval system, and allow­
ing the assistant to answer from selected passages. The system is re­
stricted to enrolled students. The university assumes that an educational 
purpose and the purchase of books settle the matter.
Begin with the acts, not the institution’s good intentions. Scanning, 
storing, indexing, supplying passages to a model, and distributing an­
swers require separate analysis. Identify the country, licence terms, rel­
evant exceptions, authorized users, retention, and output behaviour. The 
fact that access is limited may matter without resolving every issue. 
The fact that the project is educational does not automatically import a 
US fair-use conclusion into another jurisdiction.
A safer proposed design might begin with licensed material, a clearly 
defined educational permission, or a reviewed exception applicable to 
the actual acts. It would limit access and retention consistently with that 
basis, preserve attribution, avoid systematic delivery of unauthorized 
replacement chapters, and test whether adversarial questions elicit long 
protected passages. These controls support a legal basis; they do not 
manufacture one where none exists.
Now alter the facts. The material is an openly licensed textbook, but 
some photographs are excluded from that licence. The answer changes 
at the component level. The institution can use the permissions actually 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
75
granted while handling the photographs separately. This is why “the 
textbook is open” should be verified against the licence and exceptions 
listed in the publication, rather than treated as a statement about every 
embedded asset.
Case B: the rival textbook with new words. A startup prompts a 
model to rewrite Mara’s entire book for younger readers. It changes ev­
ery name, simplifies the language, and rearranges some sections. The 
resulting work retains the sequence of distinctive invented cases and 
their resolutions. It carries a different brand and credits Mara as an in­
spiration. The startup argues that the work is original because the word­
ing is new.
The analysis should distinguish a general subject from expressive 
transformation. A derivative-work or adaptation question may remain 
despite low literal overlap. The credit does not supply permission. The 
new brand reduces one potential source-confusion concern but does not 
answer the copyright question. Review the particular expressive corre­
spondences, access, permissions, and any claimed exception under the 
applicable law.
A genuinely independent educational treatment would start from the 
subject, primary evidence, and its own pedagogical choices, rather than 
from a command to preserve one book’s journey under different words. 
That process would provide stronger evidence of independence, but the 
actual output would still require review. A procedure is not a legal talis­
man. It should produce substantively different work, not just a cleaner 
story about how the work was made.
Now alter the facts again. The rival shares only conventional defini­
tions and publicly established facts, with independent examples and 
structure. Mara’s sales fall. The remaining problem may be lawful com­
petition rather than infringement. The appropriate response could in­
volve improving discoverability, verified quality, or institutional distri­
bution. A copyright complaint should not be used to hide that change in 
diagnosis.

Case C: the AI-written replacement library. Adrian wants to replace 
a third-party library whose licence no longer suits a planned product. 
An engineer supplies its complete source to a coding agent and asks for 
an equivalent implementation under the company’s preferred licence. 
The resulting files contain new variable names and no copied licence 
header. All existing tests pass.
The tests establish a bounded behavioural result, not legal indepen­
dence. The team must investigate whether protected expression was 
transformed, whether the initial processing was authorized, and what 
obligations follow from the material actually used. Removing a licence 
notice cannot be treated as a compliance step. Nor should an assumed 
need to change licences justify concealing the provenance of the re­
placement.
A more defensible route could use a legally available functional spec­
ification, qualified review of interoperability and other relevant rights, 
separately developed implementation choices, and an independent test 
design. The organization should examine whether the specification it­
self carries protected expression or confidential restrictions. It should 
also review the model’s role without pretending to know its entire pre­
training history. The goal is a documented, legally assessed implemen­
tation—not a claim of metaphysical freedom from all influence.
Now suppose the original library contains only an elementary imple­
mentation of a standard operation, and the new function follows from 
independent requirements. The assessment can be different. Open-
source obligations do not arise from mere functional resemblance to ev­
ery program under an open-source licence. This variation tests whether 
the reviewer can protect legitimate permissions and legitimate indepen­
dence at the same time.
Case D: the authentic-looking fake edition. A marketplace seller pub­
lishes a machine-generated guide under a title and cover presentation 
suggesting that Mara approved it. The text is independently generated 
and contains no identifiable copied expression. The seller points to that 
absence and says the complaint must fail.


THE RIGHT TO COPY
77
Copyright may not be the decisive instrument. Investigate the actual 
claims of authorship, endorsement, source, and affiliation, together with 
relevant trademark and other national protections. Preserve the listing 
and the context in which buyers encounter it. A response should target 
the misleading relationship rather than assert ownership over every in­
dependently written guide to Mara’s subject.
Now replace the misleading presentation with an unmistakably inde­
pendent critical guide. The author names Mara because her work is the 
subject of criticism. A claim based solely on that reference would re­
quire a different analysis. This variation tests whether a brand-protec­
tion process distinguishes deception from discussion. A creator should 
be able to defend a genuine identity without acquiring a veto over criti­
cism.
Across all four cases, the same method survives: identify the asset, 
separate the acts, locate the applicable law, inspect permissions and ex­
ceptions, assess the actual correspondence, and choose a proportionate 
response. The method is deliberately more stable than the answer. New 
models and new cases will change facts and interpretations. They need 
not force every organization to restart from a slogan.

Epilogue — The freedom to learn 
from a book
Mara begins this story believing that a rival has taken something be­
cause the rival’s product seems to do what hers does. Adrian begins 
with the same belief about software. By the end, neither has received a 
promise that the law will preserve every advantage created by hard 
work. They have something more useful: a sharper account of what can 
be protected, what must remain available to others, and where institu­
tions need to do work that copyright cannot do alone.
That account can be uncomfortable for creators. A brilliant explana­
tion can teach a reader enough to write another explanation. A useful 
program can reveal that a function is worth implementing. An effective 
method can spread beyond the book in which it was described. These 
are not merely defects in an otherwise perfect system of control. They 
are among the reasons people create, publish, and learn in the first 
place.
The discomfort also runs in the other direction. A system that trans­
forms language can make appropriation harder to recognize. A model 
can conceal dependence behind fluent new sentences. A copied module 
can lose its licence notice while retaining protected structure. A fake 
edition can exploit a trusted name without copying a paragraph. De­
fending openness requires confronting these failures, not pretending 
that every transformation is independent creation.
The central distinction is therefore not between humans and ma­
chines. It is between justified and unjustified uses, truthful and mislead­
ing relationships, independent contributions and disguised dependence, 
effective competition and unlawful appropriation. Machines change 
scale, cost, and evidence. They do not make these distinctions unneces­
sary.
A mature response combines law, engineering, and institutional de­
sign. Law defines rights and limits. Engineering preserves versions, 


THE RIGHT TO COPY
79
permissions, and relevant evidence. Institutions support quality, attribu­
tion, maintenance, correction, and fair participation. None can substi­
tute entirely for the others. A signature cannot decide originality; a 
judgment cannot ensure a product’s reliability; a business model cannot 
excuse copying that the law prohibits.
There is no credible universal solution that makes every equivalent 
rewrite compensable, every output traceable to one source, or every 
creator economically secure. There are credible partial solutions to 
identifiable problems. The task is to combine them honestly, test their 
effects, and reject mechanisms whose apparent certainty depends on 
hiding the rights or interests of someone else.
The right to create should include meaningful protection against ap­
propriation. The freedom to learn should include the possibility of un­
derstanding, criticizing, and doing something new with what one has 
learned. A culture that loses either side becomes poorer. The difficult 
work is to preserve both when imitation becomes cheap—and to re­
member that the purpose of a good book is not only to be bought, but 
also to make its reader capable of something the author does not en­
tirely control.

Glossary — A working vocabulary
These definitions are study aids. Legal terms must be read in their statu­
tory and jurisdictional context. Chapter discussions and the source reg­
ister provide the relevant qualifications.
Adaptation or derivative work. A transformation of an existing work 
that may engage rights in its protected expression. Different language 
or format does not necessarily make the transformation independent.
Assignment. A transfer of specified rights. It differs from permission to 
use them and from ownership of a physical copy. Formalities and scope 
depend on the relevant law.
Attribution. Identification or acknowledgment of a source or creator. It 
can be required by law, licence, or professional norms, but does not it­
self supply permission for every use.
Chain of title. The documented basis connecting initial ownership to 
the party claiming the relevant rights, including employment rules, 
transfers, licences, and any intervening contributions.
Clean room. A process designed to separate examination of legally 
available reference material from implementation. It can improve evi­
dence of independence but is not a universal legal safe harbour.
Commercial substitutability. The extent to which customers may 
choose one product instead of another. It is an economic relationship, 
not a synonym for copying protected expression.
Copyleft. A licensing approach attaching specified reciprocal condi­
tions to covered uses, commonly including distribution of modified or 
combined covered software under defined terms.
Copyright. A legally delimited set of rights in qualifying expression. It 
is not general ownership of ideas, facts, methods, functions, or every 
useful result of reading a work.


THE RIGHT TO COPY
81
Database right. In relevant systems, a right distinct from ordinary 
copyright that can protect qualifying investment in obtaining, verifying, 
or presenting database contents against specified acts.
Distinctiveness. A sign’s capacity to identify a commercial source 
rather than merely describe or name the relevant category of goods or 
services.
Economic rights. Rights concerning specified exploitations of a work, 
such as reproduction, distribution, adaptation, or communication, as de­
fined by the applicable law.
Exception or limitation. A legal rule allowing or excluding particular 
uses from an exclusive right under defined conditions. Its existence 
does not depend on a private licence for that use.
Expressive similarity. Correspondence in the protected form of a 
work, potentially beyond literal wording. Identifying it requires separat­
ing expression from unprotected or constrained elements.
Fair use. A contextual US doctrine evaluated through statutory factors. 
It is not a universal international permission or a fixed numerical al­
lowance.
Functional equivalence. Agreement in relevant behaviour or practical 
capability. For software, the input domain and observable effects must 
be specified before the claim becomes testable.
General-purpose AI model. A regulatory category addressed by the 
EU AI Act. Its provider’s regulatory obligations do not automatically 
settle the copyright status of every input or output.
Human authorship. The human creative contribution relevant to pro­
tection under applicable law. The use of an AI tool is not by itself dis­
qualifying; accepting output is not by itself proof of authorship of all its 
expression.
Interoperability. The ability of systems or components to exchange in­
formation and use it meaningfully. Legal routes supporting interoper­
ability have jurisdiction-specific limits.


THE RIGHT TO COPY
82
Licence. Permission concerning specified rights or uses, under stated 
terms. A licensor cannot grant rights it does not hold, and a licence does 
not eliminate unrelated legal requirements.
Likelihood of confusion. A central trademark concept concerning 
whether relevant consumers may misunderstand commercial source or 
connection, assessed through the applicable legal framework and facts.
Moral rights. Author-related interests such as attribution and integrity. 
Their scope, transferability, waiver, and enforcement vary across legal 
systems.
Originality. The legal threshold concerning qualifying creative expres­
sion. It is not identical to novelty, scientific importance, effort, or com­
mercial value.
Plagiarism. Misrepresentation of intellectual contribution or failure of 
required acknowledgment under relevant ethical or institutional norms. 
It can exist without copyright infringement, and vice versa.
Provenance. Information about origins, processing, and relationships 
among artifacts. A record can be useful evidence without conclusively 
establishing authorship, truth, or ownership.
Public domain. Material not subject to applicable exclusive copyright, 
including where protection does not arise or has expired. New compo­
nents or versions may require separate analysis.
Retrieval-augmented generation. A system design that retrieves ex­
ternal material and provides it to a generative model while answering. It 
is not identical to changing model parameters through training.
Rights reservation. An expression retaining specified rights or exclud­
ing specified permissions, with effect determined by the relevant law 
and circumstances. Machine readability can matter in particular 
regimes.
Semantic equivalence. Agreement in conveyed meaning at a specified 
level. Meaning is not generally coextensive with the expression pro­
tected by copyright.


THE RIGHT TO COPY
83
Software bill of materials. An inventory describing components in 
software. Standardized formats can support licensing and security work 
without proving the completeness or truth of every entry.
Text and data mining. Automated analytical processing of text or 
data. Legal exceptions addressing it differ in beneficiaries, purposes, 
access conditions, reservations, and permitted acts.
Trade secret. Information meeting the relevant legal requirements con­
cerning secrecy, value, and protective measures. It is not a right over all 
useful information once publicly disclosed.
Trademark. A source-identifying sign protected under the relevant le­
gal regime. Its function is different from ownership of the content, 
function, or idea associated with the product.
