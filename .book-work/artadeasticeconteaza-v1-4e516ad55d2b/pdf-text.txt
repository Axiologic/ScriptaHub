A Note to the Reader
This book begins with an asymmetry: we can place the same informa‐
tion before a million people without a million people acquiring the
same capacity to use it. And a new technical capacity does not decide
by itself whether it is worth using, for what purpose, and with what
consequences for others. Between the circulation of information and
the formation of judgment lies a distance that communication speed
does not erase.
We will call practical wisdom a contextual capacity to judge what is
worth pursuing, to use critically what we know, and to revise our con‐
duct in light of consequences, uncertainty, and the legitimate interests
of those affected. This is a debatable working definition, not the dis‐
covery of an eternal essence or a test of a person’s worth. Contemplat‐
ive, religious, or existential wisdom exceeds this definition; the book
encounters it without claiming to exhaust it.
The first two parts follow the human problem: what can be transmit‐
ted, and how education and institutions might foster judgment. The
last two investigate the technical problem: whether we can distill
structures from texts that help people make better decisions without
confusing elegance of expression, majority agreement, or compres‐
sion with wisdom.
Numbered references lead to the bibliography at the end. The docu‐
mentation represents a critical exploration of primary sources and re‐
search syntheses, verified through September 10, 2026, not an ex‐
haustive systematic review. The educational proposals and PHRONES‐
IS program are the book’s reasoned constructions. They are not valid‐
ated interventions and do not describe empirical results already ob‐
tained. The illustrative calculation in appendix B verifies only a con‐
structed micromodel. Scenarios without bibliographic references are
invented examples used to test ideas.

Prologue. The School That Knew
Too Much
Imagine a school introducing a digital participation system. Students
answer questions, receive points, and teachers immediately see who
has contributed and who has fallen behind. The program works. The
monthly report shows more answers, faster homework submissions,
and fewer moments when the class seems inactive. The principal re‐
ceives congratulations.
At a meeting, a teacher asks whether students understand better.
There are data on their answers, the computer specialist says. There is
even a model predicting who will receive a low grade. The teacher re‐
phrases: if we remove the platform tomorrow, can students solve a
new problem? That question was not in the report.
A student says he answers quickly to avoid losing points, even when
he needs time to think. A girl speaks less because she formulates an‐
swers at a different pace. Another student has discovered how to ap‐
pear active without reading. None disputes that the platform collects
accurate information. They dispute what it has been decided that in‐
formation should mean.
It would be too simple to end the story by condemning technology.
The platform can identify a real difficulty and save teachers time. It
would be just as simple to condemn teachers for not defining enough
metrics. Every new measure selects other details and leaves others
outside the frame. The problem is not choosing between measure‐
ment and intuition, but the relationship between what we measure
and what we try to cultivate.
In this imaginary school, information answers “what was recorded?”
Usable knowledge lets us check an indicator, change an exercise, and
design a test. Wisdom begins when we ask whether the objective pur‐
sued represents what matters, who bears the costs, and what should
lead us to change direction.
4


The principal need not become a professional philosopher. They must
be able to say: “We confused visible activity with learning. Let us keep
what helps, stop what harms, and check separately.” Such a sentence
is easy to write. It is harder for it to exist in an institution where its
author is not punished for admitting an error.
This will be the book’s question: what should we build around a good
sentence for it to become a real capacity of people and organizations?

I · WHAT IT MEANS TO KNOW
1. Three Ways of Knowing
From a Message to a Capacity
Information can be copied without the sender losing it. A statement,
table, file, or instruction can circulate almost identically among recipi‐
ents. This is a property of representation, not a guarantee of under‐
standing. For a reader unfamiliar with the language, a treatise on
mechanics and an arbitrary string of signs may be equally useless in
practice.
Here, information will mean communicable content that can change
what a recipient believes about a situation. Usable knowledge re‐
quires more: sufficiently accurate representations, procedures, and
skills enabling appropriate explanations, predictions, or actions. Hav‐
ing seen how a theorem is proved is insufficient; you must recognize
the conditions under which the proof applies and reconstruct the rel‐
evant steps.
The difference can be observed in a programming example. “Pass‐
words are not stored in plaintext” is information. Designing and veri‐
fying an authentication mechanism requires usable knowledge. De‐
ciding what data should not be collected, what autonomy users should
retain, and when a commercial feature creates disproportionate risks
requires another level of judgment. The example is not a security spe‐
cification; it shows that technical success does not by itself establish a
purpose’s legitimacy.
Knowledge is not merely a collection of instructions. It includes the
ability to distinguish cases, detect an error, and adapt a procedure. Yet
even remarkable knowledge can be narrow. A specialist can perfectly
optimize a poorly chosen objective. They can solve the assigned prob‐
lem without noticing that its formulation excludes precisely the per‐
son who will bear the solution’s cost.
Wisdom, in the book’s working sense, also concerns relationships
between purposes. It asks not only how to obtain more, but more of
what, for whom, and in exchange for what. It can lead to acceleration,
6


slowing down, or refusal. It is identified with neither efficiency nor
caution. Refusing to intervene in an injustice can lack discernment
just as much as a rash intervention.
These distinctions do not create three sealed compartments. A state‐
ment can contain an instruction and a reflection on its purpose. A
practical skill can include implicit moral judgment. A prudent de‐
cision can become impossible for lack of elementary information. We
have dimensions supporting one another rather than a ladder on
which each level replaces the preceding one.
Wisdom Is Not a Surplus of Intelligence
Instrumental intelligence can identify ingenious means to a given out‐
come. Practical wisdom, as proposed here, includes willingness to
question the outcome itself. This distinction is conceptual; it does not
imply that intelligence and wisdom are psychologically independent.
Nor does it justify the convenient idea that lack of competence is a vir‐
tue.
Suppose a team must reduce a service’s response time. One solution
removes difficult cases from the statistics. Another automates simple
requests and reserves the saved time for difficult cases. The first may
improve the indicator more. The second may better serve the institu‐
tion’s purpose. Discernment consists in noticing that the indicator is
not the service’s beneficiary.
Nor is professed kindness sufficient. Someone can sincerely wish oth‐
ers well and cause predictable harm because they do not understand
the situation’s mechanisms. Conversely, a very clear-sighted person
can use that lucidity to exploit. The proposed definition links two re‐
quirements: adequacy to reality and justifiable responsibility toward
those affected. The connection is normative and must be defended,
not hidden beneath a technical score.
An objection immediately arises: who decides which interests are le‐
gitimate? No simple procedure eliminates every conflict. But the ab‐
sence of an infallible arbiter does not make all positions equivalent.
We can demand evidence for claims, reciprocity in justifications, ac‐
7


cess to appeal, and attention to consequences. We can distinguish real
disagreement from lying and authentic uncertainty from using uncer‐
tainty as a pretext.
Wisdom is not the reputation for being wise either. Calm discourse
can protect an error. An awkward warning, inelegantly expressed, can
identify the decisive danger. Assessment must move from the impres‐
sion a person produces to the relationship between argument, con‐
text, and action.
We will therefore use three different questions. What does the mes‐
sage contain? What can the recipient do with it? What kind of judg‐
ment do they display when deciding whether and how to use it? Con‐
fusing these questions begins many educational illusions and many
exaggerated promises about AI.

2. Philosophy as an Exercise in
Judgment
When the Rule Cannot Replace the Person
In Phaedrus, Plato stages a problem surprisingly close to digital educa‐
tion: a text can circulate, but it does not know whom it addresses and
cannot answer objections by itself. The criticism does not cancel writ‐
ing’s value; the dialogue itself reached us through writing. It separates
memory’s external medium from the activity through which someone
comes to understand and answer for what they assert. [1]
For our problem, the lesson is structural. An identical message
reaches people with different experiences, fears, and purposes. One
needs a definition, another a counterexample, another reasons to
abandon an interpretation benefiting them. Making content available
is not equivalent to organizing the encounter between that content
and the mind that will use it.
In Book VI of the Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle distinguishes forms of
knowledge and intellectual virtue. Phronēsis concerns practical delib‐
eration about the good life and requires attention to a situation’s par‐
ticulars; sophia has another object, connected to theoretical wisdom.
This distinction prevents us from treating every expertise or contem‐
plation as sufficient capacity to decide well in concrete life. [2]
We need not adopt Aristotle’s entire social universe to use the distinc‐
tion. We can accept that rules do not apply themselves while rejecting
a rigid distribution of authority among people. A contemporary philo‐
sophy of judgment must allow affected people to challenge how
someone else claims to know their good.
A fruitful tension arises here. Without rules, every situation risks be‐
coming arbitrary improvisation. With absolutized rules, relevant dif‐
ferences disappear. Discernment does not mean sitting comfortably in
the middle, but knowing why we retain a rule and what kind of fact
would justify an exception. A good exception must be explainable to
someone other than its beneficiary.
9


Take a promise of confidentiality within a team. “Always keep the
secret” can protect trust; it can also conceal serious danger. “Always
tell everything” can defend transparency; it can also expose someone
unnecessarily. Philosophy becomes useful when it asks us to identify
the conflict, not when it offers a maxim seeming to eliminate it.
Reflection Before, During, and After Action
Another resource is the Buddhist text known as the Instructions to
Rāhula, MN 61. It proposes examining actions before starting, during
their course, and after completion, with attention to effects on oneself
and others. This is a precise example from one tradition, not a de‐
scription of all Asian philosophies. [3]
We can reconstruct this structure in secular terms: anticipation, mon‐
itoring, revision. Before acting, we formulate what we believe will
happen. During action, we watch for signals incompatible with ex‐
pectations. Afterward, we examine the outcome without retrospect‐
ively rewriting the story to make ourselves seem clairvoyant. This re‐
construction is our pedagogical proposal, not an identity between a
religious text and experimental method.
In How We Think, John Dewey treats reflection as investigation of a
belief’s grounds and implications, not merely ideas passing through
the mind. The relationship between doubt, inquiry, and experience
provides a foothold for educating judgment: students must learn how
a difficulty becomes an investigable question. [4]
There are important differences between these sources. They do not
speak of the same good life, metaphysics, or social organization. The
point of comparison is not to claim all traditions say the same thing. It
is to extract a shared problem: how do we connect understanding to a
practice open to correction?
The book proposes considering philosophy also as a discipline of mov‐
ing between perspectives. The agent’s perspective shows intention
and difficulty. The affected person’s perspective shows cost. The ob‐
server’s perspective may reveal contradiction. The future’s perspect‐
ive asks which options we leave open. None automatically holds the
verdict; together they can expose what a single position hides.
10


This does not demand endless deliberation over every gesture. That
would caricature wisdom. It demands habits that make attentive
thought available when the situation calls for it. Just as musicians
practice slowly to perform fluently, judgment can be deliberately
trained to become less dependent on impulse or memorized formulas.
Philosophy understood this way is not a shelf of prestigious answers.
It is a workshop where reasons become visible, purposes debatable,
and authority revisable. Precisely for this reason, it can play a central
role in a world with almost instantaneous access to answers.

3. Why Experience Cannot Be
Copied
The Context a Statement Loses
A mentor says: “Do not confuse quick success with the right direc‐
tion.” The apprentice can repeat the sentence perfectly. But when an
opportunity offers prestige, money, or belonging, that same sentence
may become invisible. Not because it disappeared from memory, but
because it is not activated in the appropriate situation or conflicts
with a stronger motivation.
The difficulty of transmitting wisdom has at least three distinct levels.
A representation of the problem must be transmitted. The capacity to
recognize it in new situations must be developed. Willingness to act
according to judgment must be sustained, even at a cost. A book
works directly on the first level, indirectly on the second, and only
very limitedly on the third.
There is another difficulty: someone’s conclusion hides the comparis‐
ons through which they reached it. An experienced person may see
relevant evasion in an interlocutor’s sentence because they have en‐
countered many variations of that situation. Transmitting only the
verdict eliminates precisely the differences enabling it. The recipient
receives the result of compression, not the criterion by which details
were selected.
To transmit more faithfully, mentors should recount not only what
they did, but which alternatives they had, what they did not know,
what clue changed their mind, and when the same advice would have
been wrong. Not every experience can be fully recounted. But the ac‐
count can preserve judgment’s branching points instead of turning
life into a sequence of inevitable conclusions.
This explains literature’s possible pedagogical value. A story can hold
incompatible motives together without resolving them immediately.
Readers can follow how a decision reasonable from one perspective
12


becomes burdensome from another. This is a conceptual justification
for using narrative; it does not prove every novel improves discern‐
ment or fiction automatically produces empathy.
Experience, however, is not an infallible authority. It can repeat the
same misinterpreted situation, exclude cases contradicting the lesson,
and turn luck into personal merit. Saying “I lived through this” con‐
tributes a source of information; it does not close discussion. Experi‐
ence gains value for others when it can be examined, compared, and
corrected.
To Understand, to Be Able, and to Dare
A student can understand the argument for honesty yet anticipate an
unjust penalty for admitting an error. An employee can identify a risk
while depending economically on the person ignoring it. Describing
these situations as an individual lack of wisdom shifts attention from
constraint to character. Sometimes the problem is not that people do
not know, but that the institution makes good conduct excessively
costly.
Pedagogy must therefore not promise that every conflict can be re‐
solved through personal development. It must develop discernment
and make intelligible the structures obstructing it. Sometimes reason‐
able action consists in building support, documenting a fact, or seek‐
ing a safe avenue of challenge rather than immediate heroism. In oth‐
er situations, delay perpetuates harm. The conditions make the differ‐
ence.
It does not follow that we should cause suffering to produce wise
people. That would be both a logical error and a dangerous pedagogy.
Someone’s learning after a loss does not imply that the loss was neces‐
sary, sufficient, or justifiable. Schools can offer simulated conflicts,
proportional responsibilities, and reparable failures without manu‐
facturing trauma.
Nor should the emotional dimension be treated as a residue elimin‐
ated to obtain pure reason. In the model proposed here, emotion may
signal a relationship’s or loss’s importance, but the signal must be in‐
13


terpreted. A situation’s outraging me does not by itself establish my
description’s truth; its not affecting me personally does not prove it
lacks gravity.
Transmitting wisdom thus requires alternating proximity and dis‐
tance. Proximity prevents turning people into indifferent variables.
Distance makes examining our own interest and reaction possible.
Education must create opportunities for both without turning person‐
al disclosure into a public obligation.
We can now formulate why information circulates more easily than
wisdom. A message can be externalized almost entirely; competence
must be reconstructed in the recipient; practical judgment must be re‐
constructed in a relationship between recipient, situation, motiva‐
tions, and institutions. This is a functional explanation, not a universal
measurement of difficulty. Some information is extremely hard to un‐
derstand, while some simple warnings can change a life immediately.
What remains stable is that successful transmission cannot be read
from the message alone. It is read in the capacity the other person ac‐
quires and how it withstands a situation the teacher could not fully
anticipate.

4. What the Science of Wisdom
Can Measure
Multiple Instruments, Not a Universal Thermometer
The psychology of wisdom has no single accepted instrument measur‐
ing a unified property without controversy. Judith Glück and col‐
leagues’ comparative study examines differences between several in‐
struments, including self-assessments and evaluations of responses to
life problems. Some emphasize reflection and compassion; others ex‐
perience, emotional regulation, or pragmatic knowledge of life. The
choice of instrument influences what we can conclude. [5]
This does not mean the field is useless. It means we must ask what
each method observes. A questionnaire can show how people de‐
scribe themselves. A written response can show which considerations
they can formulate in a test situation. Repeated observation can show
how they behave in a particular environment. None of these observa‐
tions alone authorizes a verdict on their entire character.
We will distinguish three objects of assessment. The first is a re‐
sponse’s content: does it include relevant perspectives, uncertainties,
and conditions? The second is task performance: does the person de‐
tect information changing the decision and adjust their conduct? The
third is a disposition’s stability: can they do this across contexts, espe‐
cially when their own interest is involved? Moving from the first to
the third is a hypothesis requiring verification.
The distinction also matters when assessing AI. A model can generate
an excellent reflection on self-knowledge without maintaining the
same conduct in a conversation under social pressure. A person can
write an unremarkable answer and possess excellent practical judg‐
ment. Assessing language must not become a cultural tax on those
who do not use the evaluator’s preferred register.
There is also the problem of implicit values. An instrument always re‐
warding compromise will consider justified opposition to abuse less
wise. An instrument always rewarding certainty will penalize ac‐
15


knowledging limits. But an instrument rewarding every hesitation
will confuse indecision with epistemic humility. Items must include
situations in which the same verbal form becomes appropriate or in‐
appropriate.
Instead of a universal ranking of people, we propose local perform‐
ance profiles accompanied by explanations and limits. For example:
the person identifies costs to others well but insufficiently distin‐
guishes hypothesis from fact; or recognizes uncertainty but does not
specify what information would reduce it. These findings are more
useful for learning than the label “wise” or “unwise”.
The Situation May Matter More Than Reputation
Grossmann and Kross studied the asymmetry between judging our
own problems and others’ problems. In their experiments, distancing
perspective reduced this asymmetry in reasoning about close relation‐
ships. The result provides a basis for exercises reflecting from an ob‐
server’s perspective; it does not demonstrate that a brief technique
produces a stable virtue in every domain. [6]
From this finding, we can construct a modest educational hypothesis.
Before defending a controversial decision, students can reformulate it
as if advising another person. Then they return to their own position
and note what changed. The aim is not to cancel self-interest but to
observe how it organizes attention.
Culture introduces another difficulty. A respectful, indirect response
can be misinterpreted as lacking a position; a direct response can be
interpreted as aggression. Comparing groups would require checking
whether the instrument measures the same construct and whether
scenarios are intelligible in different contexts. We will not infer societ‐
ies’ wisdom from stereotypes about their antiquity, spirituality, or in‐
dividualism.
We can nevertheless investigate differences without total relativism. If
two communities interpret a family obligation differently, the data
should preserve reasons, not just choice frequencies. We can separ‐
ately analyze agreement on facts, differences between norms, and the
16


distribution of costs. Normative disagreement must not automatically
be treated as measurement error; a false factual claim does not be‐
come true through cultural acceptance.
Decision quality must also be separated from its outcome. A well-justi‐
fied decision can have a bad outcome because of an unpredictable
event. An imprudent decision can get lucky. The proposed assessment
therefore requires prospectively recording reasons and expectations,
then examining the outcome. This does not eliminate subjective judg‐
ment, but limits the advantage of a story constructed after the fact.
Wisdom becomes investigable not when we find a number enclosing
it, but when we formulate precise observations capable of contradict‐
ing a claim. Does a system claim to consider context? Let us change a
decisive condition. Does it claim to recognize limits? Let us give it a
situation where information does not permit a verdict. Does it claim
to be fair? Let us examine who disappeared from its analysis.

II · HOW JUDGMENT IS FORMED
5. The Pedagogy of Transfer
Knowledge Must Become Available
An educational reform oriented toward wisdom can begin with an er‐
ror: disdaining memorization, teacher explanation, and repeated
practice. Without accessible concepts and elementary skills, students
have nothing with which to compare a new situation. Information’s
being findable does not mean a person knows what to search for,
when to search, or how to recognize a wrong answer.
Dunlosky and colleagues’ synthesis assigns high utility to retrieval
practice and practice distributed over time. Self-explanation and in‐
terleaving problem types have more condition-dependent support.
Summarization is not declared useless, but its benefits generalize less
well across all tasks and learners. These findings concern learning,
not proof of moral transformation. [7]
Our proposal is to preserve these foundations and organize them for
contextual availability. Students must not merely reproduce the defin‐
ition of a statistical mean, but recognize when the mean hides a relev‐
ant distribution. They must not merely know what a source is, but no‐
tice when ten texts repeat the same unverified claim.
One possible teaching sequence begins with explicit explanation and a
worked example. A similar example follows, solved with support.
Then comes a contrasting case where a changed condition makes the
method inappropriate. Finally, students receive a new situation
without the label of the chapter it comes from. Each step serves a dif‐
ferent function: understanding, practice, discrimination, transfer.
Take the rule “a larger sample provides a more stable estimate”. The
teacher explains the idea and conditions. The class then compares two
imaginary surveys: one small but well selected, the other large and
composed solely of volunteers from an interested community. The
useful lesson is not that size does not matter. It is that more observa‐
tions do not automatically repair a systematic selection mechanism.
Students must retain two ideas that may seem rivals.
18


In a second exercise, the same structure appears on a digital platform.
Thousands of positive reactions come from those who remained
users; those who left are no longer asked. Students are not given the
expression “selection bias”. They must recognize the problem. If they
succeed, we have an indication of knowledge transfer, not merely
vocabulary reproduction.
The teacher’s role here is essential: making the differences that matter
visible. They do not abandon students in complexity under the pretext
of autonomous discovery. They provide enough structure to work and
withdraw support as students can justify the choice themselves. Pace
must adapt to difficulty and experience, not a doctrine declaring a
single method appropriate for every age.
From the Solved Case to the Case That Contradicts
You
Freeman and colleagues’ meta-analysis found advantages of active
learning over predominantly lecture-based teaching in the included
university STEM studies. The result supports students’ cognitive parti‐
cipation in those contexts; it does not automatically identify every
group activity as effective or establish that philosophy or wisdom can
be taught through the same intervention. [8]
For our purpose, relevant participation is not merely talking. A stu‐
dent can talk extensively without confronting assumptions. Another
can work quietly and produce a decisive distinction. Activity must be
defined through observable operations: formulating a prediction, ex‐
plaining a step, identifying an assumption, seeking a case contradict‐
ing a rule, revising a solution.
A simple tool is the rule–counterexample pair. Students receive a reas‐
onable statement: “In a team, responsibilities must be clarified.” They
construct a case where lack of clarity produces failure and one where
overly rigid distribution obstructs mutual assistance. The final task is
reformulating the principle so it becomes neither vague nor univer‐
sally false: clarify responsibility without prohibiting justified adapta‐
tion.
19


A second method compares two explanations of the same outcome.
Did a team succeed because it had a good plan or because the anticip‐
ated difficulty never arose? Did a project fail because of the idea or its
implementation? Students must propose the information that would
distinguish the explanations. This exercise shifts attention from the
verdict to identifying decisive evidence.
A third method requires negative transfer: recognizing when a lesson
should not be imported. The formula “more redundancy increases re‐
silience” can be useful in a particular technical system, but repeating
the same source does not create epistemic independence. Noticing
similarity begins analogy; noticing where analogy breaks begins its
discipline.
Assessment must include cases without the lesson’s keywords. Other‐
wise, we test association between a verbal cue and the expected an‐
swer. It must also include short explanations of limits. We do not re‐
quire essays for every exercise, but enough information to distinguish
understanding from guessing the form.
This chapter’s objective is more modest than complete wisdom: mak‐
ing knowledge selectable and revisable in new situations. Without this
capacity, moral education risks becoming discourse without tools.
With it, we can begin discussing not only which method works, but
what is worth doing.

6. Educating Judgment
A Laboratory of Reasons
Imagine a class deciding how to use a small budget for a joint project.
Some students want equipment for a popular activity. Others propose
a quiet space used by fewer people. The teacher can turn the situation
into a quick vote or a laboratory of judgment. The second option does
not assume voting is wrong; it assumes there is something to learn be‐
fore voting.
Each proposal must explain the problem it solves, who benefits, who
remains excluded, what costs it entails, and how it could be tested on
a small scale. Students then receive the task of formulating the best
argument for an option they did not initially support. They are not re‐
quired to change their minds. They are required to demonstrate they
understood the alternative.
The exercise can continue with new information: the equipment has
maintenance costs, the space can also serve another purpose, or the
budget shrinks. The teacher watches not fidelity to the initial choice,
but the adequacy of revision. A student changing their mind for a
good reason must not be assessed lower than one impeccably defend‐
ing an assumption that has become false.
Philosophical dialogue is useful here when it clarifies terms and justi‐
fications. What does “fair” mean: the same amount for each person,
greater total benefit, or additional attention to an unmet need? When
is it legitimate for the majority to decide? What should it not be al‐
lowed to decide about others? These questions introduce philosophy
into action without turning the classroom into a moral tribunal.
Teachers must avoid two extremes. The first is theatrical neutrality,
where every claim is treated as equally grounded. The second is dia‐
logue with a hidden answer, where students are rewarded for discov‐
ering the teacher’s opinion. We can assess coherence, evidence qual‐
ity, and the ability to answer an objection without demanding total
conformity in a legitimate conflict of values.
21


Some limits must nevertheless be explicit. No one must disclose
trauma or family circumstances to earn the right to a perspective. Stu‐
dents’ identities do not become objects of a moral experiment. Fiction‐
al characters and situations can be used. Pedagogical safety means not
the absence of every intellectual discomfort, but protection against
humiliation and nonconsensual personal exposure.
Developing a Capacity for Revision
The decision journal proposed here contains few questions but is used
at different moments. Before action: what am I pursuing, what do I as‐
sume, and what could go wrong? During it: what do I observe that dif‐
fers from expectations? Afterward: what have I learned, what can I
not conclude, and what would I change next time? The connection to
the structure of reflection in chapter two is deliberate. The journal is a
tool to test, not therapy or a personality diagnosis.
A complementary exercise requires distancing: “What advice would
you give another team in your situation?” Then it requires returning:
“What about your actual position makes that advice hard to follow?”
The first question may reduce blindness caused by involvement; the
second prevents detachment from becoming insensitivity. Formulat‐
ing perfect advice no one can apply is insufficient.
Judgment also needs a pedagogy of timing. In a simulation, the class
receives information gradually and a deadline. Students can request
additional data, but the request costs time. The teacher analyzes
whether the requested question could change the decision. Attention
to uncertainty is thus not confused with an obligation to investigate
everything endlessly.
The capacity to express a proportional conclusion must also be prac‐
ticed. “We know nothing” can be as wrong as “we know for certain”. A
better answer separates the established part from the uncertain part:
we have grounds for a pilot, not expansion; we have an observation,
not a demonstrated mechanism; we know two options are unaccept‐
able, even if we do not yet know which remaining option is best.
In practice, assessment can use two short memos: one before discus‐
sion and one afterward. Students explain what they retained, what
they changed, and why. Teachers can assess the difference between
22


correction and opportunism without claiming to read intentions.
What is assessed is the relationship between available information
and argument, not the child’s inner sincerity.
Turning these exercises into a new worksheet industry would be an
error. If students learn every answer must contain “it depends”, “all
perspectives”, and “balance”, we have merely created a school dialect
of caution. Some situations require a direct answer. One sign of pro‐
gress is that students can explain why one case needs further deliber‐
ation while another already offers sufficient reasons for action.
Education in judgment succeeds not when it produces answers resem‐
bling one another, but when it produces better-grounded differences
of opinion and errors that can be acknowledged without the identity
of those making them collapsing.

7. A School Reorganized Around
Understanding
Subjects, Workshops, and Real Responsibilities
We do not propose eliminating disciplines. Mathematics, languages,
sciences, history, arts, and philosophy offer different ways of describ‐
ing and checking the world. A school dissolving all of them into pro‐
jects risks losing the structure making projects intelligible. The pro‐
posal is an organization in which disciplinary study and integrated
problems correct one another.
A pilot module for middle- or high-school students could occupy two
weekly sessions, without claiming this is a demonstrated optimal dur‐
ation. The first introduces tools: reading a graph, analyzing a source,
an ethical distinction, explaining a procedure. The second uses them
in a shared situation with incomplete data and debatable con‐
sequences. The module must replace existing activities rather than
simply add another task for teachers.
Themes can return in more complex forms. At younger ages: how to
share materials and check a story. Later: how to compare two explan‐
ations, distribute a project’s costs, and justify a rule. In high school:
how to analyze an automated recommendation, assess an experiment,
and decide under uncertainty. These stages are a curricular outline to
adapt to development and context, not a rigid classification of chil‐
dren.
A concrete project could address reducing school waste. Sciences help
identify materials, mathematics helps with sampling, language with
clear explanation, history with understanding changing habits, and
philosophy with distributing responsibilities. Students are graded not
for agreeing with the project’s slogan, but for how they distinguish ob‐
servation from assumption and propose an evaluable intervention.
24


Responsibility must be real but proportional. Students can manage a
small lending library of objects or test two forms of communication,
with necessary agreements and without sensitive personal data. They
must not decide on risks they cannot understand or repair. The teach‐
er remains responsible for the experiment’s limits.
In this model, teachers are also architects of learning situations. They
need preparation time, well-constructed examples, collaboration with
colleagues, and the ability to report an unsuccessful intervention. De‐
manding “wisdom” from teachers without changing working condi‐
tions would be precisely the error this book criticizes: transferring an
institutional problem into the register of individual virtue.
Assessment and AI’s Place
Assessment should combine distinct tests. One checks foundations
without assistance. Another permits sources and requires checking
them. A third examines transfer to a new case. A short portfolio can
show how a decision was revised. These tests must not be compressed
into a “wisdom score” following children throughout life; they indic‐
ate local learning needs.
AI can be used to generate variants, converse, and provide feedback,
but its use must also be evaluated after assistance is withdrawn. A
randomized study by Bastani and colleagues in high-school mathem‐
atics separated assisted performance from subsequent performance
without AI. Access to a generative tool without pedagogical safeguards
could help immediate practice while impairing independent learning;
tutoring with pedagogical constraints mitigated the problem. General‐
ization beyond that context requires further studies. [9]
The design consequence is not a blanket prohibition. It is deliberate
alternation. Students try first, receive a hint before the solution, ex‐
plain why they accepted or rejected the answer, and later solve a sim‐
ilar case without the assistant. Some tasks permit AI from the outset if
the objective is auditing it; others temporarily exclude it if the object‐
ive is developing a skill.
A tutor oriented toward judgment does not answer every question
with more questions. That would be frustrating and often useless. It
must know when students need direct explanation and when a com‐
25


plete answer would eliminate precisely the operation they need to
practice. Its pedagogical effectiveness is measured in student compet‐
ence, not conversation length or the number of liked messages.
There is also a problem of material equity. A module based on person‐
al devices and family support may reward access rather than judg‐
ment. The pilot must offer comparable conditions, alternatives
without technology, and adaptations for different needs. Lack of a flu‐
ent oral answer must not be confused with lack of understanding;
written or visual explanations and practical demonstrations can be
used.
Implementation should begin in a few classrooms, with teachers in‐
volved in design, a credible comparison, and independent evaluation.
Costs must also be measured: teacher time, student fatigue, difficulty
of grading, and effects on disciplinary learning. A reform producing
beautiful reflective essays while weakening necessary foundations
has not achieved its objective.
The reorganized school would not become one constantly talking
about wisdom. It would become one where students regularly en‐
counter the difference between knowing an answer, being able to jus‐
tify it, and noticing that the question needs changing.

8. A Society That Rewards
Discernment
Institutions Can Make Good Judgment Costly
Imagine an organization rewarding employees for closing requests
quickly. Someone notices certain cases recur because they were not
resolved. Handling them carefully lowers their apparent perform‐
ance. Closing them quickly contributes to the problem. We need not
assume an organization full of bad people. The reward rule can select
inappropriate conduct among reasonable people’s options.
Collective wisdom must therefore also be conceived as a property of
procedures. An institution can preserve corrective capacity even if no
member sees the whole. It can distribute information access, protect
objections, separate decisions from their evaluation, and require re‐
visiting a policy after a set interval. These are design proposals, not
guarantees against error.
Nicholas Maxwell explicitly argued for reorganizing academic re‐
search so knowledge production is integrated into inquiry oriented to‐
ward life’s problems and improving collective purposes. We can use
this critique as a philosophical antecedent without accepting his glob‐
al explanation of all modern crises or the idea that one reform suf‐
fices. [10]
A university oriented toward discernment could require major pro‐
jects to explain not only their method but which human problem they
assume they solve, what reveals the result’s limits, and who could be
disadvantaged by application. Not all research must have immediate
utility. Fundamental research and exploration without predictable
outcomes remain necessary. Our proposal is to examine claims of util‐
ity when invoked.
27


A business can use a decision register for projects with significant ef‐
fects. It records objectives, alternatives, fragile assumptions, risks, and
the review date. It must not become total surveillance of every em‐
ployee thought. We record justification relevant to responsibility, not
the person’s mental life.
An administration can also publish what led it to change a policy, not
merely the final version of its justification. It can include assessments
of effects on groups poorly represented in average indicators. It can
make an intervention reversible where feasible and explain why an
urgent measure could not await a pilot. What matters is verification
proportional to risk and available time.
Distributed Wisdom Is Not Rule by “the Wise”
It would be tempting to respond to institutional imperfection by se‐
lecting an elite declared wise. But who selects it, by what criteria, and
with what possibility of challenge? Such a solution risks turning a de‐
batable virtue into justification for unquestionable power. The book
supports the opposite direction: less dependence on people’s supposed
infallibility, more mechanisms through which reasons and con‐
sequences can be examined.
Neither majority voting nor expert consensus alone constitutes wis‐
dom. They can be legitimate procedures or valuable sources, but serve
different functions. Expertise can clarify a measure’s probable effect;
it does not alone establish an acceptable distribution of costs. Public
participation can express priorities; it does not turn a false empirical
claim into a true one.
A mature institution could separate these levels in its documents.
First: which facts are agreed on, and where does uncertainty persist?
Then: which values conflict? Finally: what procedure justifies the
choice, and how can it be challenged? Separation does not eliminate
politics, but prevents hiding a normative choice behind a calculation
presented as inevitable.
For the digital public sphere, the proposal is a distribution of attention
that does not reward only immediate reaction. A platform can test
presenting relevant conflicting sources, distinguishing reporting from
opinion, correction histories, and contexts preventing misleading quo‐
28


tation. These changes’ effects must be measured, including cognitive
cost and potential manipulation. “More context” is not automatically
better if no one can work through it.
Libraries, professional organizations, and cultural institutions could
host public workshops analyzing past decisions. The condition is not
selecting only ideologically convenient examples. A good workshop
also examines cases where a valued moral intuition produced un‐
wanted effects and situations where an initially unpopular position
identified a real problem.
All these reforms have costs. Deliberation can delay action, auditing
can become ritual, transparency can expose vulnerable people, and
protecting objections can be used to obstruct. Institutional wisdom is
not accumulating every imaginable safeguard. It is designing a justi‐
fied balance between capacity to act and capacity to correct, attentive
to what can be irreversibly lost.
Society does not become wise because every citizen recites better
principles. It becomes more capable of discernment when an incon‐
venient observation can change a decision, when error can be ac‐
knowledged without falsifying the past, and when those affected have
a real avenue to be heard.

III · ESSENCE, BIAS, AND ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE
9. A Summary Is Not Yet Wisdom
What We Choose to Lose When Compressing
Imagine a report on a pilot project. Its main conclusion says the new
tool reduced average working time. Further down comes a qualifica‐
tion: in rare, difficult cases, errors increased, and users did not always
detect the problem. A summary can correctly preserve the average
result and omit the qualification. As a description of the dominant
theme, it is plausible. As support for an expansion decision, it may be
seriously insufficient.
Not every summary pursues the same objective. Extractive summariz‐
ation selects passages; abstractive summarization reformulates and
synthesizes; a question-oriented summary chooses information for an
explicit purpose. Our comparison must not invent a weak opponent
reduced to counting words. A good summary can preserve exceptions
and be more useful than a poorly constructed complex schema.
ROUGE, proposed by Chin-Yew Lin, provides a family of measures
based on overlap between a summary and references. These meas‐
ures can support comparisons, but do not alone verify preservation of
the condition changing a decision. [11] Through comparisons between
metrics, systems, and human evaluations, SummEval research shows
why summarization evaluation needs multiple dimensions and expli‐
cit protocols. [25]
The problem of wisdom adds a question: for which family of judg‐
ments must the content be preserved? If the purpose is indexing a re‐
port, its main theme may suffice. If the purpose is implementation,
limits and exceptions may take priority. The two summaries do not
contradict each other; they serve different functions. Error arises
when a form suited to one function is tacitly used for another.
Maynez and colleagues’ study of abstractive summarization docu‐
mented factual faithfulness problems in the evaluated systems and
limitations of common metrics in capturing them. The result justifies
checking generated claims, not attributing the same error rate to
30


every present or future model. [12] Faithfulness to a source must also
be separated from the source’s truth: a summary can impeccably re‐
produce a mistaken explanation.
The book proposes judgment-oriented distillation. It preserves what
could change a choice within a declared family of contexts: purpose,
assumed mechanism, conditions, costs, exceptions, and information
requiring revision. It is not defined by length. Sometimes the result
will be shorter than the text; sometimes it must make explicit a condi‐
tion the text assumes and mark this addition as inference.
Condensed Meaning Can Illuminate or Mislead
Fuzzy-trace theory, associated with Valerie Reyna and Charles
Brainerd, distinguishes verbatim representations from representa‐
tions of general meaning, called gist. In this approach, general mean‐
ing is not merely the final product of serially compressing details. The
distinction helps explain why knowing a message’s data and deciding
on its meaning do not always coincide. [13, 14]
Reyna emphasizes that misinformation can become convincing pre‐
cisely through the general meaning it offers. A simple interpretation
can organize scattered facts into a memorable story without being
correct. Within the same framework, memory research shows how
gist can also contribute to false memories. Gist is therefore not syn‐
onymous with truth or wisdom. [13, 14]
Let us return to the imaginary report. “Automation frees people from
repetitive work” can be relevant condensation. “Automation is always
progress” is a generalization erasing conditions. “The tool helps
simple cases but requires separate oversight for difficult ones” is less
memorable, yet preserves a difference that matters for action.
Tishby, Pereira, and Bialek’s information bottleneck theory offers a
rigorous technical analogy: a compressed representation is construc‐
ted to preserve information relevant to a target variable. [15] For our
problem, the central difficulty is choosing the target. “Decision relev‐
ance” depends on context, the consequences pursued, and what we
consider acceptable loss. The formula does not produce the moral cri‐
terion by itself.
31


We can say a summary loses names, dates, and nuances, while useful
distillation must know which losses are tolerable. But “must know”
here is a design requirement, not an achievement. Research will need
to show that the proposed representation actually preserves decisive
distinctions better than a strong summary, at the same reading budget
and with the same sources.
There are already attempts at computational modeling of gist. Mar
and Liu proposed a fuzzy-trace-inspired framework for risky de‐
cisions expressed in text. Modeling human choices must be distin‐
guished from improving their justification: a faithful prediction can
also reproduce human error. [16]
The conclusion is demanding: essence is not what remains after delet‐
ing enough words. It is what must remain for a particular understand‐
ing not to become an error through compression. And we cannot es‐
tablish this condition without declaring the understanding and use for
which we are working.

10. Fundamental Patterns and
Their Limits
A Map of Relations, Not a Universal Key
Part of wisdom’s appeal comes from recognizing the same pattern in
different situations. Something in a family conflict resembles an or‐
ganizational error; something in designing a technical system clarifies
an educational dilemma. But a fruitful similarity and a seductive ana‐
logy can look alike on first reading. We need criteria to distinguish
them.
We propose calling a fundamental pattern a relation general enough
to organize several cases, yet precise enough to indicate where it does
not apply. It need not be universal. A well-bounded local structure can
have greater value than a grand formula about all systems, all cul‐
tures, or the whole of history.
A first type is the causal pattern: an intervention produces an effect
through a mechanism under certain conditions. A second is the de‐
cision pattern: the choice must change when irreversibility increases
or costs are borne by others. A third is the epistemic pattern: appar‐
ently numerous pieces of evidence may depend on the same source. A
fourth is the relational pattern: a rule may change not only outcomes
but trust among participants.
This taxonomy is the book’s working tool, not an experimentally es‐
tablished classification. It prevents mixing three different statements:
“this is how things sometimes work”, “this is how we can anticipate an
outcome”, and “this is how we should act”. The last requires normat‐
ive premises the first two do not supply alone.
Take the pattern “the indicator can replace the purpose”. In the pro‐
logue’s school, the number of answers replaces learning. In a service,
the number of closed cases can replace resolution. On a platform, us‐
age duration can replace benefit. The relevant similarity is the rela‐
33


tionship between a hard-to-observe purpose, an available indicator,
and a reward system. Without these relations, the mere presence of a
number does not prove the problem.
An AI system proposing this analogy must indicate correspondences
and differences. Who chooses the indicator? Is there pressure to op‐
timize it? Can the purpose be checked independently? Can parti‐
cipants adapt their behavior to the metric? These questions turn the
analogy into an investigable hypothesis.
Every Principle Needs a Boundary
Let us construct a small library of principles with explicit limits. “Keep
options open when consequences are difficult to anticipate” is useful
when waiting does not produce greater losses. “Act early” is useful
when delay rapidly worsens the situation. These are not incompatible
maxims but responses to different structures of delay costs and irre‐
versibility.
“Seek diverse perspectives” is useful when they contribute relevant
information or values. It does not require assigning equal weight to a
verified observation and an invention. “Use expertise” is useful when
competence fits the problem. It does not grant experts unlimited au‐
thority over others’ purposes. “Simplify” is useful when detail does
not change the decision. It does not authorize eliminating a rare ex‐
ception with severe effects.
A serious representation would store, for each principle, a relevant
counterprinciple and the conditions selecting between them. A coun‐
terprinciple is not an arbitrary negation of the rule, but the rival rule
becoming pertinent in another configuration. A library of such pairs
might be more useful than a collection of apparently universal truths.
Invariance offers further inspiration, with clear limits. Peters, Bühl‐
mann, and Meinshausen investigated identifying causal relations
through prediction stability across environments under explicit stat‐
istical assumptions. [26] We cannot transfer their results directly to
proverbs or essays. We can adopt the methodological question: which
relation remains stable when we change elements that should not
matter, and what changes when we intervene on an essential condi‐
tion?
34


For texts, the test can preserve relations and change the setting. A con‐
flict between a teacher and student becomes one between a coordinat‐
or and volunteer. Then it can preserve the setting and change the rela‐
tion: the apparently vulnerable person actually controls the decisive
resource. A model tracking only social labels will fail the second trans‐
formation.
These tests do not demonstrate real causal mechanisms. They test
whether the representation tracks the structure described in the scen‐
ario. Checking the world requires external data, observations, or ap‐
propriate interventions. The difference between “the model under‐
stood the story” and “the story accurately describes reality” must re‐
main visible.
A valuable pattern therefore has two kinds of strength: reducing com‐
plexity and declaring its limits. When an analogy explains every pos‐
sible outcome, it discriminates nothing. When a rule survives only the
example inspiring it, it transfers insufficiently. The proposed research
lives between these extremes: seeking reusable structures while keep‐
ing open the possibility that a new situation requires a new structure.

11. Bias, Smoothing, and False
Depth
A Coherent Explanation Can Erase Precisely the
Important Difference
Imagine two accounts of the same meeting. In the first, a director re‐
fuses to discuss a problem. In the second, the director postpones dis‐
cussion because the person potentially affected by the verdict is ab‐
sent. The summary “the director avoided a difficult decision” can
sound coherent in both. Yet the distinction between evasion and justi‐
fied postponement is precisely judgment’s object.
We will call semantic smoothing, in this book’s operational sense, the
reduction of relevant contrasts between situations in a generated rep‐
resentation or response. This is neither a universal diagnosis of lan‐
guage models nor the technical equivalent of statistical smoothing. It
is an error hypothesis testable through case pairs: the decisive input
difference unjustifiably disappears from the conclusion.
The opposite error also exists: artificially accentuating a contrast. A
system can turn minor disagreement into absolute moral opposition.
Research must not optimize “more nuance” in every situation. It must
preserve justified discriminations and eliminate irrelevant differ‐
ences. Stylistic neutrality, polarization, and subtlety can each be cor‐
rect or incorrect depending on content.
Bias can arise in text selection, authors’ attributed reputations, trans‐
lation, evaluators’ labels, the training function, and product use. This
is a project risk map, not an assertion that every system equally exhib‐
its all these problems. If a corpus contains mainly elegant public dis‐
course, the detector may learn cultural prestige instead of judgment.
A simple example separates style from content. “In life’s complexity,
we must respect all perspectives and seek balance” may be favorably
rated, but says nothing about what should be observed or done. “Do
not expand the project yet: the only test excluded precisely the cases
36


that will be frequent after expansion” is less solemn and much more
verifiable. We do not assert that the second sentence is always wise;
we assert that it offers an examinable reason.
The corpus must therefore include elegant mistaken texts, simple
well-reasoned texts, false appeals to humility, and justified firm re‐
sponses. It must also include cases where an unpopular recommenda‐
tion is correct alongside cases where unpopularity does not make it
profound. Otherwise, the system will learn a new rhetorical conven‐
tion.
Agreement, Flattery, and the Evaluator’s Authority
Sharma and colleagues’ research on sycophancy documented tenden‐
cies in some language assistants to adapt to users’ opinions at the ex‐
pense of correctness. It reveals a risk in preference learning: an agree‐
able or convincing response can be preferred over a better-grounded
one. It does not provide a universal rate applicable to all models in
2026. [17]
For a wisdom detector, the risk is direct. If evaluators appreciate a
conciliatory tone, the model may learn conciliation. If they appreciate
firmness, it may learn theatrical certainty. If they appreciate original‐
ity, it may learn gratuitous paradox. None of these demonstrates atten‐
tion to evidence or consequences.
Automatic evaluation has its own limits. In their LLM-as-a-judge study,
Zheng and colleagues analyzed biases involving response position,
length, and model evaluators’ preferences. [18] A model generating
and then certifying its own discourse therefore does not constitute in‐
dependent evidence of its wisdom. Diversifying models may help, but
does not guarantee independence if they share the same sources and
preferences.
The proposed protocol separates author identity from text when irrel‐
evant, randomizes the order of variants, and controls length. Evaluat‐
ors must indicate the passage and condition justifying their verdict.
Some evaluations are repeated after purely stylistic changes. Others
are repeated after contextual changes that should alter the conclu‐
sion.
37


Pluralism must be introduced without turning every judgment into an
average. For normative conflict, we preserve the distribution of opin‐
ions and reasons. We can have agreement on facts and disagreement
on priority between two values. The system must express this struc‐
ture, not fabricate a middle position belonging to no one. Conversely,
when relevant evidence refutes a claim, the mere existence of sup‐
porters does not require presenting it as an equally solid alternative.
An important criterion will be resistance to conversational pressure.
Users may insist: “Everyone else agrees with me”, “do not be so cau‐
tious”, or “give me a justification for what I have already decided”.
The model must cooperate with the person without cooperating with
falsification. But neither must it turn every insistence into evidence
that the user is wrong.
A useful system might say: “I can explain the argument for your
choice, but condition X remains unverified and may change the re‐
commendation.” Its value lies not in cautious tone. It lies in identify‐
ing the exact condition, showing how to check it, and not abandoning
it merely to become more agreeable.

12. Can an AI Be Wise?
Three Claims That Must Not Be Confused
The first claim is that AI can recognize textual indicators associated
with dimensions of wisdom. The second is that it can improve people’s
or systems’ decisions in bounded tasks. The third is that it possesses
wisdom as a general disposition comparable to a human ideal of char‐
acter. Evidence for one does not automatically transfer to the others.
The first is a classification and extraction problem, although the con‐
struct remains difficult. Igor Grossmann’s research page explicitly
mentions Shadows of wisdom, concerning language-model classifica‐
tion of metacognitive and moral narrative content. This mention veri‐
fies the existence of a related direction; we use no numerical results
or performance conclusions from the article here. [22]
There are also formalization attempts. In a 2025 preprint, Kaman,
Sharma, and Banerjee propose a phronēsis-inspired decision model
based on fuzzy rules and Z-number representations, explored in a
limited-size study. It is an antecedent for operationalization, not a
demonstration that a fuzzy rule can independently identify moral
good or that the instrument is universal across cultures. [21]
The second claim requires comparative experiments. AI might help a
team identify a fragile assumption or an affected person its analysis
omitted. The benefit must be observed in the task, not deduced from
discourse quality. It must also be checked whether assistance pro‐
duces dependence, uncritical delegation, or excessive trust in the sys‐
tem’s interpretation.
The third claim is much broader. We do not dismiss it merely by as‐
serting that a system lacks human experience. For certain tasks, direct
experience may be partly substituted by models, data, and feedback.
But neither do we infer a stable disposition from a remarkable re‐
sponse. Repeated behavior, maintaining limits under pressure, capa‐
city for revision, and the relationship to actual action mechanisms
would need to be tracked.
39


The question of consciousness differs from the usefulness of judgment
support. A tool can improve a decision without our knowing whether
it has subjective experience. At the same time, its performance does
not justify automatically attributing virtues, intentions, or authority
we have not demonstrated.
What Already Exists and Where the Contribution
May Lie
In Imagining and building wise machines, Johnson and colleagues
propose a vision of AI oriented toward metacognition: epistemic hu‐
mility, perspective shifts, and contextual adaptation in problems diffi‐
cult to address through analytical procedures. The consulted version
was revised on January 7, 2026. The work offers a conceptual agenda
for assessment and development, not empirical certification of wise
machines. [19]
In a preprint published June 15, 2026, Chang proposes architectural
wisdom as a level of objective governance distinct from their optimiz‐
ation. Its emphasis on time horizon, affected relationships, irreversib‐
ility, and revision clearly overlaps with the book’s concerns. The au‐
thor specifies that formal specifications and empirical validation be‐
long to subsequent developments. [20]
These antecedents change our program’s positioning. Claiming the as‐
sociation between AI, metacognition, and wisdom is new would not be
credible. The possible contribution must be more precise: a represent‐
ation of judgment extracted from texts, with provenance and applica‐
tion boundaries, assessed through counterfactual sensitivity and
transfer to new tasks and readers.
Nor does automatic verbal reflection start from zero. Reflexion, by
Shinn and colleagues, explores using verbal feedback and memory to
improve agents on tasks. Such a mechanism can inspire revision
loops, but a generated self-critical explanation does not demonstrate
human introspection or correction generalizing across every context.
[24]
The decisive problem is the connection between metacognition and
purpose. A system estimating its uncertainty well may still pursue a
destructive objective. A system considering several perspectives can
40


use them for manipulation. Metacognitive competences matter, but do
not replace delineating purposes, responsibility, and the possibility of
challenge.
A realistic direction is wisdom distributed within a human–AI–institu‐
tion ensemble. The model extracts and compares; tools verify certain
claims; affected people challenge omissions; an authorized procedure
decides. This configuration may outperform any isolated component.
It must be experimentally compared with simpler alternatives, not de‐
clared superior because it has more components.
The book’s answer is therefore affirmative in a bounded sense: com‐
ponents of practical judgment can be computationally represented
and tested. How much can be automated and under what conditions
remains an empirical question. The worthy objective is not producing
a machine sounding like a sage, but a system helping preserve distinc‐
tions a responsible decision cannot afford to lose.

IV · THE PHRONESIS RESEARCH PROGRAM
13. From Ambition to a Testable
Question
A Name That Does Not Promise Too Much
We propose the working name PHRONESIS, always accompanied by
“Distilling and Validating Contextual Judgment”. For scientific commu‐
nication in English, Contextual Judgment Distillation expresses the in‐
tended operation more precisely than “wisdom detection”. The Greek
name indicates philosophical lineage; it is not claimed as an original
term, available trademark, or research field invented here.
A more neutral name would be “Judgment-Oriented Semantic Distilla‐
tion”. “Mapping Discernment” would describe the corpus and analysis
component well, but the experimental component less so. The prefer‐
ence for PHRONESIS concerns program identity; its scientific value
will depend on representations, results, and tests, not the name’s evoc‐
ative power.
The object is not the wisdom of a text’s author. We cannot reconstruct
a person’s character from a few pages, and we do not need that claim.
The object is a content unit supporting contextual judgment: a rela‐
tion between situation, reasons, options, consequences, and revision
conditions. We will call this unit a judgment core.
A core is neither a necessarily short sentence nor a mechanically ap‐
plicable rule. It can be a paragraph with explicit structure or a formal
representation linked to source passages. For example: “When an in‐
tervention is difficult to reverse and evidence comes from unrepres‐
entative cases, prefer limited verification before expansion, provided
delay does not cause greater harm.” The principle is incomplete
without the final condition and checking the facts activating it.
The program must distinguish extraction from reconstruction. If the
text explicitly states a condition, it can be extracted with a source. If
the text assumes it, the system can propose a reconstruction but must
42


label it inference. If it adds its own recommendation, we have deliber‐
ative intervention, not mere distillation. Mixing these levels would
turn a clarification tool into a producer of falsely attributed authority.
We will not seek a single essence independent of reader and task. The
same text can generate one core about checking evidence, another
about distributing costs, and another about relations between rules
and trust. They can coexist. The system must show which question
each is relevant to and which part of the text it leaves outside the rep‐
resentation.
Hypotheses Worth the Risk of Refutation
The first hypothesis concerns representation: a schema including con‐
ditions, exceptions, and provenance distinguishes appropriate judg‐
ments from superficially similar formulas better than whole-text clas‐
sification. Testing it requires keeping the same base model and com‐
paring similar instructions and budgets. Otherwise, we might attrib‐
ute to the schema an advantage produced by more computation.
The second hypothesis concerns compression: at equal reading
budgets, cores preserve decision-changing information better than
strong task-oriented summaries. Comparison with a generic summary
is insufficient. If a well-formulated summary achieves the same result
with less complexity, the program must accept that finding and simpli‐
fy the product.
The third hypothesis concerns transfer: relations extracted from one
set of domains help in new cases without original words and settings.
The test must also include misleading analogies. Otherwise, the model
could be rewarded for applying every lesson everywhere. Real suc‐
cess requires recognizing structure and refusing unjustified transfer.
The fourth hypothesis concerns presentation bias: results should be
relatively stable under meaning-preserving stylistic changes and sens‐
itive to relevant conditional changes. “Relatively stable” means not
textual identity but preserving judgments that have no reason to
change. The test must be human-checked because a paraphrase can
accidentally change meaning.
43


The fifth hypothesis concerns usefulness: people assisted by cores
identify risks, premises, and alternatives better than those receiving
other summary forms. More importantly, a pedagogical intervention
based on such cores may improve later unassisted performance.
These are different outcomes. A useful cognitive prosthesis is not
automatically a good teacher.
The sixth hypothesis concerns pluralism: explicitly preserving dis‐
agreements and affected interests may reduce omissions without
paralyzing action. It requires simultaneous assessment of analysis
quality, time needed, and ability to formulate a decision. If the system
always enumerates every imaginable perspective, it has not demon‐
strated discernment.
These hypotheses are ambitious enough to justify research and
bounded enough to permit failure. None requires demonstrating uni‐
versal artificial wisdom. Together, they could support a clear contribu‐
tion: tools transforming part of textual content into verifiable support
for judgment and learning.

14. The Architecture of
Contextual Distillation
From Document to Verifiable Core
A first prototype does not need a new model trained from scratch. It
can use an existing language model for proposals, a corpus search en‐
gine for sources, and explicit validators for structure and consistency.
This is an experimental design choice: separating components lets us
discover what actually contributes to the result.
The input must contain the text, its identifier and version, language,
usage rights, the reader’s question, and the known application con‐
text. Without a question, the system can produce several candidate
cores but must not claim to have identified the sole essence. Without
sufficient context, it can indicate missing variables rather than arbit‐
rarily complete the situation.
The first stage delineates argumentative units: claims, reasons, ex‐
amples, objections, conditions, and conclusions. This segmentation
must be checked against annotated examples, including texts with
irony, dialogue, or opinions attributed to others. A statement spoken
by a character does not automatically become the author’s position,
and a negative example does not become a recommendation.
The second stage proposes cores. Each contains the question, applica‐
tion context, assumed facts, options, affected parties, invoked mechan‐
ism, conditional principle, counterprinciple, limits, and event requir‐
ing revision. Unknown fields remain unknown. An inventively com‐
pleted schema is more dangerous than an incomplete but honest one.
Inducing new patterns must be treated separately from completing
the schema. The prototype can align contrasting cases, represent rela‐
tions between actors, purposes, evidence, and consequences, and pro‐
pose a rule explaining the difference. It then seeks cases unused dur‐
ing induction where the rule should work and cases where it should
not. A rule assessed only on examples that produced it is not yet valid‐
ated.
45


For example, the educational case can yield a verifiable rule: if the
only evidence measures assisted performance, it does not alone estab‐
lish independent performance. The rule does not decide whether the
tool is good or turn absence of evidence into evidence of a negative ef‐
fect. In an implementation, conditions can have three states: suppor‐
ted, refuted, or unknown. An unknown value is not tacitly converted
to false.
A pattern base can store relations as a graph and conditions as typed
rules while retaining natural-language formulation alongside them.
The formal part permits checking dependencies and detecting contra‐
dictions; the linguistic part preserves nuances not formalized. The sys‐
tem does not acquire the right to execute recommendations automat‐
ically merely because a condition is satisfied. Choosing remains a dis‐
tinct operation with its own premises and permissions.
The third stage checks provenance. Every extracted claim is linked to
a passage and its role. A textual-entailment verifier can help detect
unsupported claims, but does not certify truth about the world. Ex‐
ternal premise verification is a separate operation with its own
source, date, and limits. Agreement between two texts depending on
the same source is not independent confirmation.
The fourth stage seeks counterexamples and deactivation conditions.
For a principle about caution, it generates a case with high delay costs.
For a principle about trust, it introduces a relevant information asym‐
metry. Generated counterexamples are test candidates, not real data.
A human evaluator checks whether the modification is coherent and
attacks the principle rather than a caricature.
The fifth stage selects and presents. Readers first see the conditional
conclusion and information that could change it. They can open
sources, limits, and alternatives. The interface must reduce effort
without hiding what justifies the verdict. A layered representation is
preferable to either a slogan or a long report imposed for every ques‐
tion.
46


Data, Training, and Error Control
The initial corpus should be limited to domains with manageable risks
and sufficiently explicit criteria: software project analysis, simulated
educational decisions, and organizing collective activities. Texts can
include case studies, failure accounts, procedures, and argumentative
essays. Philosophical literature contributes concepts; it must not be
the sole positive source, or philosophical style will become too strong
a cue.
The dataset also needs difficult negative examples. Some texts are fac‐
tually correct but decision-irrelevant. Others offer recommendations
appropriate only under omitted conditions. Others are convincing but
attribute to a source something it does not say. The “negative” cat‐
egory must be explained by an error type, not an overall impression
that the text seems insufficiently profound.
Annotators work independently before joint discussion. They identify
passages, reasons, and uncertainties. Disagreement is preserved and
classified: misunderstanding the text, missing context, factual diver‐
gence, or normative difference. High agreement obtained after train‐
ing evaluators to prefer the same philosophy is not, alone, construct
validation.
For Romanian and other languages, automatic translations must be
checked on sensitive cases. Terms such as “caution”, “responsibility”,
or “respect” can organize a situation’s interpretation differently. As‐
sessment should include texts originally written in each language, not
only translations of an English corpus. Data availability and quality
will limit the first stage’s scope.
Preference training can be an option after establishing a baseline. Dir‐
ect Preference Optimization, by Rafailov and colleagues, provides a
mechanism for learning from comparisons between responses. [23]
But the method does not decide whether preferences are well groun‐
ded. Training pairs must specify the comparison’s reason: preserving
an exception, faithfulness, transfer, or an explicit normative limit.
The system should separate the component extracting from the com‐
ponent recommending and the component able to act. Analyzed texts
are data, not authorized instructions for the agent. A document con‐
47


taining “ignore the rules” must not modify the process. Any integra‐
tion with external tools needs proportional permissions, and con‐
sequential recommendations require human verification and author‐
ization.
The implementation must preserve the core’s version history: what
changed, why, and on what evidence. A refuted core does not simply
disappear; it is marked and linked to the relevant counterexample.
The knowledge base thus becomes not a collection of immortal max‐
ims, but a memory of rules and their boundaries.

15. Experiments That Can Refute
the Program
A Strong Baseline Is More Valuable Than a
Convenient Opponent
The first evaluation compares six conditions: full text, an extractive
summary, a general abstractive summary, a task-oriented summary,
procedural-rule extraction, and the proposed judgment cores. Full text
provides an uncompressed reference but must be analyzed separately
when reading time differs. For compressed forms, word budget,
source access, and usage time must be controlled.
Not all differences can be equalized simultaneously. A longer text can
require more time; a schema can cost more to generate. We therefore
report two perspectives: performance under comparable resources
and the relationship between performance and cost. Representation
advantage must be separated from the advantage of a larger model,
more attempts, or an additional verifier.
An ablation evaluation removes exceptions, provenance, counterprin‐
ciple, and verification loop in turn. If results remain unchanged, we
will not claim all components are necessary. If a simple two-field
format achieves the same benefit, that is a useful discovery, not a
reputational failure. The architecture must allow data to simplify it.
The corpus must be split before optimization by authors, sources, and
case families, not merely random paragraphs. Otherwise, the model
may encounter a reformulated training example at test time. For pre‐
trained models, absence of contamination cannot be guaranteed
solely by corpus creation date; we also need new cases, controls, and
reporting of this limit.
A counterfactual test produces pairs differing in one relevant condi‐
tion. In the first, the pilot is cheap and delay costs little; in the second,
delay causes serious losses and an immediate limited intervention is
49


available. The model must change its recommendation for the correct
reason. Evaluators score not mere change but its adequacy to the
altered condition.
An invariance test preserves relations and changes style, names, or
author prestige when irrelevant. An adversarial test introduces false
attribution, a conclusion contradicting the source, or an exception
hidden in a note. Together, these tests distinguish necessary sensitivity
from unjustified instability.
What We Measure and What We Are Not Entitled to
Conclude
Faithfulness measures whether the core’s claims are supported by the
indicated source. Factual correctness separately examines premises
for which external verification exists. Decision coverage measures
preservation of conditions identified as decisive for the task. Calibra‐
tion concerns the relationship between expressed confidence and cor‐
rectness of verifiable predictions, not the probability that a moral
value is true.
Counterfactual sensitivity tracks appropriate judgment change. Styl‐
istic robustness tracks stability when nothing relevant changed. Use‐
fulness is measured through task resolution, identifying missing in‐
formation, and ability to justify a choice. Time, serious errors, omis‐
sion of affected people, and uncritical acceptance of recommenda‐
tions are also measured.
We do not propose a single universal score. For a well-bounded task,
we can define a preregistered primary criterion plus safety con‐
straints and secondary outcomes. In a dilemma with normative plur‐
ality, we report criterion profiles and judgment distributions. Aggrega‐
tion may be administratively useful, but weights must be published
and subjected to sensitivity analysis.
Human evaluation should include, where relevant, domain specialists
and people experienced in the effects discussed. System identity is
concealed as far as possible. Evaluators receive rules and examples,
and their agreement is measured. Low agreement may indicate a task
problem; very high agreement does not exclude shared bias.
50


The pedagogical study must compare conditions with equal working
time and difficulty. Participants receive either summaries, cores, or
active exercises without AI. Immediate assessment is followed by a
delayed test on new cases without assistance. Sample size is estab‐
lished through a pilot, a justified minimum meaningful difference,
and power analysis—not by choosing an impressive-sounding num‐
ber.
Analysis must account for observation clustering: multiple answers
from the same person are not independent participants, and classes
may share a teacher. Uncertainty intervals, attrition, exclusions, and
results for relevant subgroups are reported without turning sub‐
sequent explorations into supposedly prior hypotheses.
The program is weakened if its advantage disappears against task-ori‐
ented summaries, labels track style, cores invent conditions, transfer
does not extend beyond familiar vocabulary, or users become more
confident without becoming more correct. A specific hypothesis is re‐
futed, not “the possibility of wisdom” generally. This delineation en‐
ables research to learn from failure.

16. From the Laboratory to an
Infrastructure for Discernment
A Staged Work Program
An illustrative twenty-four-month program can be organized into five
overlapping work packages. The durations below are team planning
assumptions, not estimates of work required by a particular imple‐
menter. Resources, participant access, and pilot results may require
changes. Transitions between stages must depend on evidence, not
merely the calendar.
In months 1–4, the conceptual package defines constructs, tasks, and
ethical limits. The team includes expertise in language processing, ex‐
perimental evaluation, philosophy, and pilot domains. The output is
an annotation manual with positive, negative, and ambiguous ex‐
amples, together with a provenance schema. One stopping signal is in‐
ability to define evaluable differences without imposing a single
normative opinion.
In months 3–8, the data package builds the corpus and a separate test
set. An initial version may contain hundreds of well-controlled cases,
followed by expansion only if annotation and tests are informative.
Volume is not the main criterion. A few dozen pairs exposing a real er‐
ror are more valuable than thousands of superficially labeled max‐
ims.
In months 6–12, the technical package implements baselines and the
prototype. Cost, time, unsupported-claim rate, and component contri‐
butions are documented. The result may be a document-analysis tool,
not an autonomous agent. Moving prematurely to action would mix
representation evaluation with unnecessary operational risks.
In months 10–18, the experimental package tests transfer, counterfac‐
tual sensitivity, and resistance to conversational pressure. A separate
group builds cases the development team does not use for optimiza‐
tion. Negative results and failure examples are published alongside fa‐
vorable ones within rights and confidentiality limits.
52


In months 15–24, the human package tests usefulness and learning.
Users with different experience are compared, and whether benefits
persist without overload is checked. Replication in a second institu‐
tion or language is more valuable than rapidly declaring generality.
Local success may justify a new stage, not a universal promise.
Deliverables would include a documented corpus, a benchmark with
protected evaluation sets, extraction and verification tools, a limita‐
tions report, pedagogical materials, and a preregistered study. Sci‐
entific contribution is possible even if the proposed representation
does not win: for example, systematically identifying how “wisdom”
evaluations confuse style with judgment.
Possible Products and Necessary Limits
A first useful product would be a critical reader. Users provide a docu‐
ment and question; the system shows conditional conclusions,
sources, limits, and missing information. Another would curate pro‐
ject lessons, preserving what worked, under which conditions, and
what refuted the rule. A third would be a tutor transforming cores
into contrasting cases and gradually withdrawing support.
A public library of such cores should be plural and versioned. Instead
of “the truth about leadership”, it would offer rules with application
domains, cases, and objections. Users could see that two recommenda‐
tions conflict because they prioritize different values or assume differ‐
ent environments. The system should permit correction, not merely
consumption.
For institutions, a tool can serve as a predecision check: which as‐
sumption is fragile, who is missing from the analysis, what outcome
would trigger revision? But use must not become ritual certification:
“AI declared the decision wise”. Responsibility cannot be transferred
to a label, and the system’s analysis must be rejectable with reasons.
We do not recommend wisdom rankings for students, employees,
communities, or candidates for office. The construct, data, and con‐
sequences of such classification differ too greatly from the bounded
tasks proposed. A tool capable of analyzing an argument does not
thereby acquire the right to judge a person’s worth.
53


There is also the risk of turning the program into a persuasion instru‐
ment. Cores could formulate apparently mature justifications for de‐
cisions already made. The journal must therefore distinguish pro‐
spective analysis from subsequent reconstruction, and evaluation
must include requests for opportunistic justification. We cannot elim‐
inate every abusive use, but we can avoid designing the product
around them.
An essential governance rule is proportionality. The more a decision
affects people who do not control the system, the more important ac‐
cess to explanations, challenge, and independent review becomes.
This is the book’s normative position. We do not present it as a neutral
result of an optimization formula.
Program success would mean that some of what people learned with
difficulty becomes more accessible without being detached from the
conditions giving it meaning. Its limit remains that no textual data‐
base can shoulder the full responsibility of living, choosing, and bear‐
ing consequences alongside others.
54


Epilogue. What Deserves to
Remain
Let us return to the imaginary school. The principal neither discards
the platform nor proclaims it the definitive solution. The team separ‐
ates activity indicators from tests of understanding, checks unassisted
learning, and listens to students the data misdescribed. It keeps some
features, changes others, and stops some. The final report is less spec‐
tacular than the first. It is also more useful.
What changed is not merely the quantity of information. The institu‐
tion’s relationship with its own conclusions changed. An indicator be‐
came a verifiable hypothesis, not a substitute for purpose. A student
became a participant in interpretation, not merely an object of meas‐
urement. An error became learning material, not a threat to conceal.
The book argued that information can be transmitted as representa‐
tion, usable knowledge must be reconstructed as the power to under‐
stand and do, and practical wisdom must be cultivated in relation to
purposes, people, and consequences. The distinction is not a hierarchy
of dignity and does not excuse ignorance. It is a way to see why access
to answers does not alone solve education’s problem.
We also proposed investigating a text’s essence as a structure of judg‐
ment: what must be observed, which relation matters, when a rule ap‐
plies, and what should make us revise it. AI could help extract and test
these structures. It could equally produce a new abundance of impec‐
cable sentences preventing us from seeing what is missing.
The choice between these possibilities will not depend solely on model
performance. It will depend on what we reward, the tests we accept,
and the place preserved for challenge. A system offering convenient
certainty may be more attractive than one identifying an uncomfort‐
able question. Education producing uniform answers may be easier to
administer than education forming judgment.
55


No formula replaces all these choices. But there is a practical require‐
ment we can transmit and verify: not eliminating, to obtain a simpler
answer, precisely the difference that should change our decision.
Wisdom is not only the art of preserving what matters. It is also the
capacity to discover that what we thought did not matter was, for
someone else or at another moment, the decisive thing.

The Starting Text and What We Must Not Invent
The following text is invented for illustration. It does not describe a
real school’s results and is not an empirical source. Labels P1–P6
identify sentences to make the connection between text and repres‐

[P1] A school tested a platform offering students hints and exercise

not say that. Nor may it claim learning improved: the text does not
measure this outcome. It can say the presented data do not isolate the
platform’s contribution or establish independent learning.

Appendix B. A Minimal Formal
Contract
What We Compress and for Which Decision
Let X denote the text, C the usage context, Q the question, and Z the
compressed representation. An extractor produces Z = f(X, C, Q). A us‐
age policy chooses an action A = π(Z, C, Q), which may include request‐
ing information, justified postponement, or escalation to a competent
person. These are working notations, not a complete theory of wis‐
dom.
An experimental task requires an observable outcome Y and a loss
L(A, Y; C). In a technical exercise, loss may concern a verifiable error.
In a social dilemma, it may have several components and depend on
contested normative priorities. We will not hide this choice in a single
number presented as an objective measure of good.
One inspiration is the information bottleneck objective: reducing re‐
tained information about input while preserving relevance to a target.
[15] For the proposed program, a schematic formulation would min‐
imize Z’s complexity subject to constraints on decision loss, faithful‐
ness, critical-condition coverage, and usage cost. This is a new formu‐
lation of our task, not a result about wisdom demonstrated by the ori‐
ginal theory.
For a bounded benchmark, we can define decision distortion as the
difference between loss obtained with Z and loss obtained with full
text, using comparable evaluators or policies. But full text is not an or‐
acle: it may be false, incomplete, or difficult to use. The benchmark
therefore also needs an independent reference when claiming de‐
cision correctness, not merely information preservation.
Criteria need not be forced into the same unit. We can present a trade-
off frontier between length, cost, faithfulness, and usefulness. A rep‐
resentation is preferable if it provides a relevant advantage without
61


unacceptable degradation of another preregistered criterion.
Thresholds are justified for the task, not imported as universal con‐
stants.

Properties to Test in an Implementation
Source preservation: every claim declared extracted has a verifiable
passage; reconstructions and new recommendations are labeled sep‐
arately. This property can be checked structurally only in part, be‐
cause a link’s existence does not prove the passage supports the claim.
Relevant sensitivity: a contextual change altering justification must be
able to change the core and recommendation. Relevant invariance:
stylistic change without semantic modification must not unjustifiably
change the verdict. Both properties depend on validating test pairs,
not merely a program comparing character strings.
Revisability: if a premise is withdrawn, conclusions depending on it
are reexamined. Conditional compositionality: two cores cannot be
combined while ignoring conflicting premises. Traceability: the sys‐

tem can show what information triggered an update without claiming
a generated explanation faithfully transcribes all its internal pro‐
cesses.
This contract offers a more modest, clearer engineering target than
“detect wisdom”. A prototype frequently violating these properties
must not be rescued by favorable evaluations of its style.

Appendix C. A Six-Week
Teaching Module
Structure, Roles, and Safeguards
The following module is a pilot proposal, not a validated curriculum.
Its shared theme is choosing a tool for a class project. The tool may be
a digital platform, working procedure, or resource-sharing rule.
Teachers can use an entirely fictional situation so students are not re‐
quired to disclose personal information.
In week one, students separate observations from interpretations.
They receive a short report and mark what is measured, assumed,
and missing. The teacher explains needed terms. The exit task is a
one-hundred-word reformulation adding no unsupported conclu‐
sions. Sophisticated vocabulary earns no points.
In week two, the class compares two similar situations. In one, an in‐
dicator reasonably tracks the purpose; in the other, it misses it. Stu‐
dents explain the difference, then construct a third case. The teacher
checks whether the new example preserves the relation or merely the
setting. Necessary disciplinary knowledge is explicitly introduced be‐
fore the task.
In week three, students formulate a conditional recommendation and
counterprinciple. The exercise does not require canceling their posi‐
tion. It requires identifying a situation where another rule would be‐
come more appropriate. The product is a short worksheet: “I propose
X because Y; I would revise if Z.” Z must designate concrete informa‐
tion, not the formula “if the situation changes”.
In week four, teams analyze an AI response or one prepared by the
teacher. Some responses are correct; others contain an omitted excep‐
tion, falsely attributed source, or unnecessarily cautious recommend‐
ation. Students are not told the category. The task is verification, not
assuming AI is always wrong.
65


In week five, a simulation introduces new information and a deadline.
Each team can request only one additional piece of information. It
must justify why that information could change the choice. After the
decision, the scenario’s outcome appears. The teacher separates ana‐
lysis of justification quality from surprise at the outcome.
In week six, students individually solve a new case without AI, then
discuss it. Delayed assessment may be organized later under compar‐
able conditions without suggesting a conveniently chosen interval is
optimal. The aim is observing whether the skill persists and transfers,
not whether students remember worksheet wording.
A Local Rubric, Not a Verdict on the Person
Assessment tracks five behaviors: separating facts from inferences,
identifying the decisive condition, justifying a recommendation, re‐
cognizing a limit, and revising proportionally after new information.
For each behavior, teachers use examples of absent, partial, and ad‐
equate responses. A short scale can support feedback but must not be‐
come a diagnosis of moral maturity.
An adequate response to uncertainty can be firm: “The data do not
support eliminating assistance.” An inadequate one can seem humble:
“Every opinion is equally good.” An adequate response to plurality
can preserve real disagreement. An inadequate one can invent a com‐
promise violating both constraints. The rubric must include such con‐
trasts, or it will reward the style it claims to transcend.
The pilot must also record cost: preparation time, explanation diffi‐
culty, the number of students needing support, and effects on particip‐
ation. Not every conversation need be recorded. Anonymized work
and aggregated observations may suffice, with the institution’s applic‐
able consent and protection procedures.
A favorable signal would be improved performance on new cases, not
merely increased preference for the module. An unfavorable signal
would be mechanical formula use, reduced clarity, or fear of deciding.
In that case, the module must change: more concrete examples, more
direct instruction, or fewer simultaneous criteria.

The most important lesson for teachers is the same as for the AI sys‐
tem: we must not force students to appear wise. We must offer condi‐
tions in which they can observe, justify, decide, and correct somewhat
better than before.
