The first dimension is capacity. What could the person understand and control at that moment? We are not asking only what an ordinary person can do under ideal conditions. Pressure, access to information, available support, and the nature of the decision matter. Nor do we turn every difficulty into an exemption: the difficulty must be relevant to the choice under examination.
The second is relevant knowledge. What did the person know, and what did they have reasonable grounds to find out? We do not demand that they predict every imaginable consequence. But neither do we accept deliberately maintained ignorance as innocence. An official who refuses to receive risk reports is not in the same position as someone from whom information was concealed in a way they could not possibly detect.
The third is the connection to the harm. Did we cause something, facilitate it, authorize it, benefit from it, or simply encounter the result? These relationships differ. Benefit can matter when allocating reparative obligations without proving harmful intent. Causal contribution can exist without blame. And the absence of a contribution does not always eliminate the obligation to provide simple, urgent help.
The fourth is role. Some tasks fall to us because we accepted them or because others justifiably depend on us. But roles have limits and can be unfairly constructed. A position cannot demand every sacrifice, and a social expectation does not become legitimate merely because it has been repeated for a long time. We must ask whether the obligation has a justification that can also be offered to the person bearing its burden.
The fifth is the power to bring about change. A director can amend a rule; an employee can only flag it; a beneficiary may at most lodge an appeal. Asking the same of them would be false equality. Yet power is not measured solely in titles. Whoever controls information, access, or the agenda may wield influence that the organizational chart does not show.
The sixth is cost, considered alongside the severity and urgency of the need. Losing a few minutes is not the same as losing your basic security. Preventing an inconvenience is not the same as preventing serious injury. Neither need nor cost should be described strategically: we do not minimize someone else's sacrifice merely because it would suit us for them to make it.
Finally, those affected must have a voice. They can correct the description of the problem and reveal costs the decision-maker does not see. Listening to them does not mean granting them infallibility or an automatic veto over every collective action. It means not constructing the judgment exclusively from the perspective of the person with the power to impose it.
The map produces questions, not automatic verdicts. In the case of the child who broke a cup, capacity and the educational purpose matter greatly. In the case of the company that concealed a defect, knowledge, contribution, and power carry a different weight. In the case of the passerby who can prevent serious harm at little cost, an obligation may exist without any prior role. No single ingredient explains everything.
Shared responsibility does not always behave like a pie, either. Discovering that an organization has a serious obligation does not imply that every individual automatically has a smaller one. The institution may need to repair the system, while the person may need to tell the truth about what they did. These are different obligations, not competing percentages of the same unit of blame.
Reparation Is Not a Ceremony of Humiliation
After harm has occurred, the public may demand a culprit, the victim may demand safety, the organization may demand an end to the scandal, and the accused may demand to be heard. These demands are not identical. Some are compatible; others conflict. A responsible process must distinguish them before announcing that “justice has been done.”
First comes protection. If a risk continues, it may be necessary to restrict an activity before establishing all retrospective responsibilities. But a preventive measure should not automatically be presented as a verdict of guilt. Its justification concerns risk and proportionality, and its conditions must be reviewed when new information emerges.
Next comes the truth about the act. Who did what, under what conditions, with what knowledge? A compelling moral story can emerge before the evidence. An accusation does not become truer because it fits our beliefs about a group. Nor is a person's good reputation sufficient to refute a specific accusation.
Then comes reparation. Sometimes this may mean returning property, correcting a decision, funding support, or publicly acknowledging an injustice. At other times, nothing equivalent can undo the loss. Acknowledging this limit is more honest than turning a sum of money or a statement into a compulsory end to suffering.
Apologies have value when they identify the act, acknowledge its effect, and connect to verifiable changes. “I regret that you felt hurt” can avoid acknowledging the conduct itself. But even perfect wording is not proof of a transformation. One person may speak awkwardly and make serious amends; another may speak impeccably and repeat the same act.
The victim does not owe forgiveness to make the community feel at peace. Forgiveness may be meaningful to them, but it must not be imposed as the final task of someone already harmed. At the same time, the absence of personal forgiveness does not by itself determine all the future rights of the wrongdoer. Shared life needs criteria that do not reduce every decision to a single emotion, however understandable it may be.
Punishment raises a further problem. It may be defended through desert, prevention, protection, or the reaffirmation of limits. These justifications are not equivalent. If a measure does not protect, deter, or repair, and merely humiliates, its defense must explain why additional suffering would be a good in itself. This book does not resolve the entire philosophy of punishment, but it refuses to confuse severity with moral seriousness.
There is also a legitimate interest in the possibility of returning. A person who has done wrong need not inevitably be reduced to their worst act. This does not require restoring every position or relationship. Some roles demand a level of trust that can be lost. But we must be able to distinguish between a justified restriction on a role and the person's indefinite exclusion from every possible future.
A culture in which every admission of error brings social destruction creates an incentive to conceal. This is an observation about the logic of incentives, not a claim that we can eliminate wrongdoing through leniency. The response to mistakes must make truth and correction possible without turning confession into a license to repeat them.
The same logic applies to institutions. Firing the most visible person is not enough if the rules that contributed to the incident remain intact. Nor is announcing a “culture change” enough without examining individual acts. Serious reparation combines a personal response with changes to the conditions that made the harm possible.
A School and a Society That Do Not Demand Omniscience
A school of responsibility would not teach children only to identify who broke the rule. It would teach them to ask what the rule protects, what information is missing, what can be repaired, and when the rule itself must change. These are philosophically motivated educational proposals, not a presentation of a program whose effectiveness we claim has been demonstrated in a controlled study.
One exercise could begin with the same event described from several positions: student, teacher, parent, affected classmate. Students would have to explain what changes when new information appears. The aim would not be to find an excuse every time, but to learn the difference between consistency and stubbornly maintaining a verdict after its grounds have disappeared.
Another exercise would separate intention, means, and outcome. Well-intentioned help can cause harm; a good outcome can arise from an imprudent choice. The student would be invited to assess what could have been anticipated, rather than merely rewarding the winner in retrospect. Luck must not become the only teacher of morality.
Collective responsibility should also be practiced without confusing it with shared guilt. If a space is damaged and the perpetrator is unknown, maintaining it may be a shared task, but this does not justify declaring everyone responsible for causing the damage. If a class has an unfair rule, students can help change it without being forced to confess to fictitious guilt.
At the social level, the same education should be supported by institutions capable of learning. An appeal should not be treated solely as an administrative inconvenience. An incident report should allow causes to be examined without predetermining the conclusion. A service must be able to discover when the people it exists to serve cannot use it. These are criteria for moral design, not guarantees that every specific mechanism will work.
Let us bring the book's cases together in a hypothetical situation. A city introduces an automated procedure for accessing support. A homeless person cannot complete the application. An official notices that the problem keeps recurring. The researcher who evaluated the system receives a complaint. A citizen sees only the queue at the counter and concludes that the beneficiaries are irresponsible.
The applicant may have the task of providing information they can obtain and understand. The official may have the task of recording the problem and using an existing support channel. Management may have an obligation to change the procedure and provide resources. The researcher must examine whether the evaluation overlooked a relevant type of failure. We can ask the citizen not to turn an incomplete picture into a confident verdict. None of these tasks is identical to the others.
If the system was reasonably designed and the problem is new, there may be less blame for its initial occurrence. Once the effect becomes known and can be remedied, the justification for inaction changes. Responsibility is not only an archive of the past. It is also how we respond to information that has just become available.
There remains one's own life. A responsible person is allowed to sleep, to enjoy themselves, to be unavailable, not to read every news story, and not to respond to every request. These freedoms need not be justified solely by making them more productive at helping others. Their life has value to them as well. Including them among the people who matter is not a departure from morality.
But limits must not be used as a formula for disappearing. Between “I cannot do everything” and “nothing falls to me” lies a large space for action. Within that space are promises kept, errors corrected, risks reported, regular contributions, and improved institutions. They are not spectacular every day. That is precisely why a society should not recognize responsibility only when someone makes a dramatic sacrifice.
Taking responsibility without believing yourself to be God means accepting two limits simultaneously. You cannot control the entire world. Nor are you completely outside it. Between the illusion of omnipotence and the comfort of powerlessness lie finite, contestable, yet real obligations. Seeking them honestly is harder than declaring yourself innocent or a savior.
EPILOGUE. The Door Remains Ours
The man outside the door has not, in the meantime, become a simple problem. We do not know all the causes of his situation. We do not know whether he will accept help, what kind of help he needs, or what can be achieved that evening. We have not discovered the biography that would give us the comforting feeling that he unquestionably deserves not to be abandoned.
