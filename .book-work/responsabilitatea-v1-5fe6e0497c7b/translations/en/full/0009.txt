If the answer is that any act attributed to the deity must by definition be called good, the concept risks losing the substance that made it important. “God is good” no longer provides moral reassurance but merely repeats a verbal identity. If goodness retains a meaning we can understand at least partially, then questions about suffering cannot be dismissed merely because they are unpleasant.
Another reply invokes the status of creator. God would have rights over what he made. But creation does not obviously confer an unlimited right to dispose of a being with interests of its own. A parent contributes to a child’s existence without turning the child into property. The analogy does not establish that the relationship between God and the world is identical to parenthood. It shows that moving from “created it” to “may justly do anything” requires an additional argument.
A defender of theism may object that all analogies are inadequate: God is not a more powerful inhabitant of the universe but the ground of its existence. The objection is serious. Yet the further we remove the deity from every intelligible category of agent, the harder it becomes to attribute categories such as care, love, justice, or promise without explanation. We cannot use analogy when it offers consolation and automatically forbid it when it produces a critical question.
Calling a deity to account does not necessarily mean claiming that we possess a cosmic tribunal. We can examine whether a concept’s properties are compatible and what reasons we have to believe a particular description of the world. Lacking the power to sanction an agent does not make every judgment about them meaningless. Otherwise, even a perfectly protected tyrant would become morally impeccable merely through the absence of an effective judge.
Suffering as Evidence Against a Promise
The philosophical problem of evil takes several forms. One asks whether the existence of evil is logically incompatible with an omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good God. Another asks whether the scale and distribution of suffering make such a hypothesis less plausible. A defense may show that a contradiction has not been established without offering a convincing explanation for every instance of actual suffering. [
The difference matters. Showing that a scenario is possible does not mean showing that it describes the world. A lawyer may show that an innocent explanation for a piece of evidence exists without having proved that explanation. Conversely, not knowing a justification for suffering is not identical to proving that no justification can exist. Serious discussion must avoid both shortcuts.
Let us test the freedom hypothesis. A world in which people can choose would also permit bad choices. If every harmful intention were immediately stopped by divine intervention, some forms of autonomy and real consequence would change. This is an intelligible reason not to identify goodness with eliminating every negative outcome. But that reason alone does not establish how much evil freedom requires.
An aggressor can retain his intention and choice even if the door he wants to force holds firm. Saving a victim does not establish that the aggressor’s freedom has been canceled in every relevant sense. The free-will defense must therefore explain more than the existence of choice: it must discuss why certain consequences are permitted and why different limits on the power to harm could not exist. This is a conceptual test, not proof that we know how to construct a complete alternative universe.
Then there is suffering that does not appear to result from a moral choice by either the victim or an aggressor. A child’s illness and the pain of a being incapable of deliberation cannot simply be explained through the affected person’s freedom. Broader connections may be proposed, but each additional connection requires justification. Moving the explanation somewhere we cannot examine is not enough.
Let us test the hypothesis of a world with stable laws. Responsible action requires some regularity: we must be able to anticipate effects. A universe in which every accident is miraculously suspended would differ from one in which we learn to protect one another. Yet it does not immediately follow that this precise distribution of risks is necessary. Stable rules explain why consequences cannot be arbitrarily eliminated without changes; they do not establish that every alternative set of rules would be worse.
Let us then test the idea of moral development through difficulty. Courage, patience, and solidarity require obstacles. But not every form of suffering produces development, and the person bearing the cost must not automatically be treated as educational material for someone else. If an adult deliberately hurt a child to make a neighbor wiser, we would demand an extremely strong justification. Invoking a higher plan does not eliminate the question of using the victim as an instrument.
The defender may reply that our judgment is too limited to assess the whole. This argument from the limits of knowledge blocks some categorical conclusions. But it has a cost: if divine goodness can look indistinguishable from cruelty to us, what practical substance remains in trusting it? And if our ignorance prevents us from condemning an evil apparently permitted by God, why would it not also prevent us from helping, since that evil might serve an unknown purpose?
A religious position can coherently reply that human duties remain valid even when the meaning of the whole is unknown. This separates the problem of providence from our conduct. Yet the separation must be maintained consistently: cosmic uncertainty must not be invoked to justify passivity before a need we understand sufficiently.
In Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion, David Hume stages a conflict between explanations of the world’s order and the experience of suffering. The text is a dialogue, not a set of statements that can be attributed to the author without qualification. One difficulty it explores is the leap from the observed world to firm conclusions about its creator’s unlimited goodness. [
Omnipotence must not be defined carelessly either. Thomas Aquinas distinguishes divine power from the capacity to realize logical contradictions: an incoherent description does not become a possibility merely because we ask it of an omnipotent being. This clarification excludes some facile objections. But it does not, by itself, establish that every imaginable reduction in suffering would be contradictory. [
The conclusion of this examination is not that God’s existence has been established or disproved in a few pages. It is narrower: attributing total knowledge, total power, and perfect goodness simultaneously creates a real explanatory burden in the face of suffering. Answers must be assessed by what they explain and what they ask us to accept without explanation.
Job and the Refusal to Call the Victim Guilty
The Book of Job matters precisely because it offers more than the image of a world in which every instance of suffering proves a fault. The friends try to bring his experience back into an intelligible moral order. The ending rebukes them for how they spoke about God. The religious text thus opens a space for challenging convenient explanations of merit, suffering, and authority. We use it not as empirical evidence but as a work staging a moral problem. [
The problem remains recognizable. Someone suffers, and those nearby feel a need to explain quickly why. Perhaps the person chose badly. Perhaps they lacked the right attitude. Perhaps there is a lesson. Some explanations may be true in particular cases. But offered without evidence, they chiefly protect the speaker’s peace of mind: if the world punishes only identifiable mistakes, the speaker can imagine themselves safe.
Refusing such an explanation does not mean claiming choices have no consequences. It means refusing the move from consequence to a total verdict on a person. Someone may be responsible for part of their situation and still suffer more than that part could justify. They may need help even if their biography is not innocent.
Job also raises the question of the right to protest. Faith can include lament, questioning, and refusing false consolation. Not every moral protest is a simple denial of the deity. It may be a demand that the goodness invoked by faith not be emptied of meaning. Conversely, unbelief does not establish the absence of obligations toward others; it changes how they are grounded.
Restoration at the end of a story does not logically cancel every loss within it. If someone later receives other goods, it does not follow that the lost people and experiences were interchangeable. This argument also applies to promises of cosmic compensation. Compensation can matter profoundly without retrospectively turning every evil into something good.
We can imagine conceptions of the deity that relinquish one of the properties discussed: limited power, limited intervention, or a relationship with the world that does not resemble an agent’s governance. These variants alter the problem of evil, but they also alter the religious promise. A God unable to prevent a tragedy is not judged in the same way as one who can prevent it at no cost and chooses not to.
If God exists and we have reasons to believe in a divine obligation, what “holding him accountable” would mean remains difficult to say. It may be a conceptual assessment, a lament, or a questioning religious relationship, not an enforceable procedure. If God does not exist, the discussion still serves a purpose: it forces us to see what connection we assume between power and obligation, without the usual excuses of human powerlessness.
In shared life, people need not resolve this dispute before cooperating. A believer may see helping as a duty toward God; a nonbeliever, as a duty toward the vulnerable being. Ultimate reasons may differ while justified action coincides. Cooperation requires no metaphysical identity, only enough shared reasons not to abandon the person before us.
Perhaps the most uncomfortable question is not whether we can construct a tribunal for God. It is whether we demand that the deity justify suffering while using that same suffering’s existence as an inevitable backdrop for our own inaction. Criticism of a cosmic plan does not repair the world by itself. Nor does belief in such a plan absolve us of what we could do within it.
9. Taking Responsibility Without Believing Yourself to Be God
How Much Can an Entire World Ask of One Person?
At the beginning of the book, we seemed to have a question about blame. By the end, we have a problem of distribution: who must notice, explain, prevent, bear costs, and repair? Sometimes the same person must do all of these. At other times, the distribution must differ. A society goes wrong when it seeks a single name for every task merely because it is easier to display.
Let us imagine a map, not a score. A score would promise that we can add power, intention, and need into a single number, then read off the verdict. We have no such validated instrument here, nor reasons to believe every relevant value is perfectly interchangeable. A map helps us see a situation’s dimensions without pretending it can resolve them mechanically.
