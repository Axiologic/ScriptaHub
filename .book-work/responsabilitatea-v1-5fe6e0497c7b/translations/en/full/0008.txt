One institutional example is the Ethics and Society Review process, described by Michael Bernstein and colleagues in a 2021 preprint. Artificial intelligence projects underwent a review of their social implications before funding. The authors analyzed the first year, involving 41 proposals. Reports of changes to projects are informative, but they do not establish through a controlled experiment that the mechanism reduced long-term social harms. [
Such an example also has value in the question it makes practical: what research decision changes after the ethical discussion? If nothing can change, the review risks becoming ceremonial. If it can block any project merely by invoking vague fears, it risks becoming a form of censorship without criteria. A good mechanism must allow arguments, revisions, proportionality, and examination of its own intervention’s costs.
Responsibility cannot be demanded only of the individual researcher. A funder who rewards exaggerated promises, a university that tolerates unverifiable results for prestige, and a company that imposes release before evaluation all help shape choices. In a real case, such statements would require evidence. As a moral structure, they show why demanding integrity without examining incentives is insufficient.
Incentives are not a complete excuse either. A system’s favoring exaggeration explains the pressure but does not automatically make exaggeration justified. We simultaneously need people who resist misguided demands and institutions that do not turn that resistance into a continuous test of professional heroism.
Does the Ability to Think Bring an Additional Duty?
Imagine a philosopher who can formulate ten elegant justifications for a practice but never asks those who bear its effects. His intelligence is real. His responsibility may be deficient. The ability to construct arguments does not guarantee that you have chosen an honest question or are willing to abandon a convenient answer.
Being able to philosophize may mean education, time, security, and access to conversations in which ideas are taken seriously. Not everyone with this opportunity acquired it exclusively through their own merit. But it does not follow that their entire life belongs to society. It provides a reason to examine how they use an advantage that can influence how others understand their condition.
There is a specific duty not to manufacture respectability for what we cannot defend directly. If an argument serves to present people as dispensable, we must also test it from the perspective of those declared dispensable. If a theory justifies a hierarchy, we must ask whether the same justification would remain acceptable if its author did not know their own place within it. These perspective tests do not decide truth on their own, but they can expose privileges hidden in the formulation.
The philosopher has no obligation to become a universal commentator. Competence in analyzing a concept does not automatically create competence in economics, medicine, or foreign policy. Intellectual responsibility may require precisely a refusal to answer authoritatively where our knowledge is insufficient. Sometimes the most useful thing an expert can say is where their expertise ends.
On the other hand, continually invoking complexity can become a refuge. If an institution demonstrably conceals harm, it is not enough to observe that the meaning of harm is philosophically disputed. Distinctions are useful when they make judgment more precise. They become irresponsible when used to postpone every practical consequence indefinitely, regardless of how clear the evidence is.
Philosophizing responsibly also means exposing the cost of one’s own theory. If I defend an almost absolute freedom to retain resources, I must say what happens to people whom voluntary help misses. If I defend a very extensive distribution of obligations, I must say which personal freedoms it limits. A theory presenting only its admirable intentions and leaving the costs to its opponents is not yet a complete justification.
But what about the person unable to engage in this abstract discussion? They need not demonstrate a theory of justice to have the right to just treatment. Intelligence and education can change the speaker’s epistemic obligations, not the fundamental worth of the person being discussed. Someone may be unable to explain in sophisticated terms why they are being humiliated and still be humiliated.
Simple speech does not guarantee truth either. Testimony should be heard, not declared infallible merely because it comes from a vulnerable person. Respect means including it in the examination, not turning the person into a symbol others use without checking the facts. Taking someone seriously includes the possibility of dialogue and disagreement.
Finally, there is responsibility for making ideas usable. A text can be precise yet useless to its intended audience. Clarity does not require eliminating difficulties but presenting them without unnecessary obstacles. Anyone who writes about responsibility and hides uncertainties in solemn language risks reproducing precisely the kind of authority they claim to examine.
The Machine Decided. Who Allowed It?
An institution uses an automated system to prioritize requests. Someone is rejected. The official says the score was calculated by the program. The supplier says the institution chose the objective. The institution says experts recommended the method. Each statement may be partly true, and between them the obligation to offer the person an explanation and a route to correction may disappear.
The philosophy of responsibility in computing discusses this “many hands” problem: outcomes arise from contributions by multiple people, organizations, and artifacts. A system’s technical autonomy must not be confused with moral autonomy. Complexity can make attributing harm difficult without that difficulty establishing the absence of any human obligation to design, oversee, or remedy. [
In our scenario, the solution is not to choose a single culprit immediately. First, we must know what kind of rejection occurred, what data were used, whether the score suited that purpose, which uncertainties were known, and who could change the process. Otherwise, “the algorithm is biased” can be as vague an explanation as “the user made a mistake.”
Formally retaining a human in the loop does not solve the problem if that person has a few seconds, no additional information, and no power to challenge the recommendation. Their signature can become a piece of moral scenery. Genuine oversight requires the ability to notice relevant errors, stop or change a decision, and receive support when doing so is justified.
Responsibility must be designed before the incident. Who authorizes using the system? Who checks whether the data are appropriate? Who monitors the effects on people? Who can withdraw it from use? Who responds to an appeal? These questions are not a complete technical specification. They are conditions for ensuring that the division of labor does not become a division of evasion.
There are also situations in which nobody could foresee a particular error. Retrospective accusation may then be unjustified, but the obligation to correct the system remains. Discovering a new risk changes what must be done next. Yesterday’s lack of knowledge does not justify tomorrow’s passivity.
How long are creators accountable for uses they did not intend? Not indefinitely and not for every imagined connection. What matters is how foreseeable the use was, how much they facilitated it, what control they retain, and what proportionate measures were available. Neither “just a tool” nor “the author is responsible for everything” resolves these differences.
Dual-use knowledge poses a similar problem. Publication can enable verification, innovation, and the sharing of benefits. It can also enable abuse. Restriction can reduce a risk but concentrate power and block legitimate research. A responsible choice must compare concrete options, including graduated access and independent scrutiny, rather than retreat into a slogan of absolute openness or absolute secrecy.
Could a future artificial intelligence itself become morally responsible? The question depends on capacities such as understanding reasons, controlling conduct, and being able to answer in a deeper sense than generating an apology. We do not establish here whether or when such conditions will be met. If we built an entity with interests of its own and the capacity to suffer, the question of our responsibility toward it would also arise, not just its responsibility toward us.
Until such hypotheses are clarified, attributing moral words to a machine must not function as an automatic transfer of accountability away from institutions. An “I’m sorry” message does not repair an unjust decision. Repair requires access to facts, authority to make changes, and acceptance of costs. These are things organizations must construct, not emotions interfaces must merely imitate.
8. Can Gods Be Held Accountable?
The Creator and Ownership of Creation
Someone sees a child in danger, can intervene at no cost, and does not. We demand an explanation. Now replace that person with a being we say knows everything, can do everything, and is perfectly good. Why should the question disappear? Perhaps we have reasons to change the standard, but that change must be argued for. Otherwise, we would construct a concept of power in which obligations grow up to a point and then disappear precisely when power reaches its maximum.
Before any accusation, we must clarify which deity we mean. A very powerful but limited god does not raise exactly the same problem as an omnipotent creator. An intelligence that initiated the universe without overseeing it is not identical to a providential parent. An impersonal reality called God is not automatically an agent who deliberates and decides. We cannot attribute all these properties to the same concept merely because we use the same word.
Let us first imagine limited gods, with intentions and interests. If they can understand suffering, choose, and have the power to intervene, they seem to enter the space of moral questions. Being more powerful than humans does not, by itself, grant an exemption. A king does not become just because nobody can defeat him. Nor does a god become good merely because contradicting him is dangerous.
Perhaps our obligation toward such gods would be one of prudence: not to provoke them. But prudence and legitimacy are different things. Submitting to avoid punishment does not establish that the order deserves obedience. This distinction also helps us understand human authorities that demand fear be called respect.
In the Euthyphro, Plato examines the relationship between what is pious and the gods’ approval. Adapted to morality, the question becomes: is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good? The first direction risks making goodness dependent on will; the second seems to recognize a standard not reducible to command. The modern adaptation is broader than the dialogue’s exact problem. [
One possible theistic reply says the alternative is incomplete: goodness would be neither arbitrary nor external to God, but connected with his nature. This reply deserves examination in its strongest form. It does not say that every divine whim would become good. It says a perfect being has no such whims. But a new question arises: by what means do we identify that nature as good, and how do we distinguish an authentic command from a mistaken human attribution?
