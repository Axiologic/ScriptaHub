The child did not ask to be brought into the world. It does not follow that all their subsequent relationships are free of obligations. It does follow, however, that basic care cannot be treated as a loan taken out by an absent debtor. A relationship can generate affection, gratitude, and, later, mutual help. It does not automatically justify unlimited submission.
Responsibility for providing care must not be confused with responsibility for guaranteeing the happiness of the person receiving it. A parent can do serious and reasonable things without being able to control all the child’s suffering. An adult child can support a parent without being able to cure every dissatisfaction. The distinction matters because an impossible obligation can become an instrument of domination: whatever the other person does, there is always another pain for which to blame them.
A responsible school should teach this limit too. Sometimes a pupil needs to learn that they may ask for help, that they need not mediate every family conflict, and that a more powerful person’s mistake does not become their fault. This is a proposal for the moral organization of education, not a diagnosis of any particular family. Applying it requires attention to context and competent support when there is a real risk to the child.
At the same time, protection must not become the elimination of every contribution. Helping care for the home or showing consideration for a tired parent can be a natural form of cooperation. The criterion is not whether a task exists, but whether it suits the child’s abilities, whether it avoids sacrificing their fundamental needs, and whether the adult retains responsibility for adult life. A child can help at the table without becoming the family’s emotional administrator.
In school assessment, responsibility can also get lost in numbers. A pupil has a poor grade; the conclusion is that they did not try hard enough. That may be true. But the grade alone does not establish the explanation. The teacher must distinguish the outcome, effort, understanding, access to support, and how the task was designed. Nor should difficulty automatically become a general excuse. A proper investigation avoids both reflexes.
Suppose a pupil submits a text produced by an AI system and presents it as her own work. Calling her lazy is not enough, just as saying technology makes the situation inevitable is not enough. What requirement was communicated to her? Which part was she supposed to do herself? What forms of help were permitted? Did she understand the difference between assistance and false attribution? If she understood and deliberately concealed it, there is an issue of honesty. If the rules were unclear, the institution also has something to put right.
A better assessment can require explaining decisions, discussing sources, and revising an error. It does not eliminate every form of cheating. But it shifts the emphasis from the appearance of a flawless product to the ability to answer for it. That would be a deeper educational objective than producing pupils who know how to give the expected answer but cannot say why it should be believed.
Moral maturity does not begin when a child no longer needs anyone. Such a moment may not exist even for adults. It begins when their participation becomes more clear-sighted: they recognize effects, can offer reasons, accept correction, and help put things right without believing that every mistake excludes them from the community.
What Do We Owe Those Who Cannot Be Held Accountable?
The expression “cannot think” gathers together profoundly different situations. One person may be unconscious. Another may have significant cognitive difficulties but be able to express preferences. Someone may not understand a complex decision yet understand a close relationship perfectly. Another has the capacity to reflect but does not use it at a particular moment. Placing all these situations in the same category is already a moral error before it becomes a practical one.
We must separate a person’s worth, their decision-making capacity, and their accountability for an action. Worth is not measured by intellectual performance. Capacity must be discussed in relation to a task and context, not automatically inferred from someone’s prestige, diagnosis, or vocabulary. Accountability for an act requires examining the relevant ability to understand and control it. Someone may need support in one area and participate autonomously in another.
The 1979 Belmont Report sets out a twofold requirement for research involving human participants: respecting autonomy and protecting people whose autonomy is diminished. The document connects consent with information, comprehension, and voluntariness. It is not a complete theory of all human relationships, but it shows why respect is reducible neither to abandoning someone before an impossible choice nor to having someone else take over all their choices. [
Imagine a woman who cannot compare the terms of a complicated contract on her own but knows where she wants to live and with whom she feels safe. Needing help with the contract does not erase her preferences about her life. Justifiable support tries to make the choice intelligible and possible. Abusive control uses the difficulty as a pretext for no longer asking her anything.
There are situations in which deliberation or expressing a choice is impossible. The question then shifts toward protection, interests, known preferences, and limits on the power of those deciding. We cannot derive approval from silence for whatever suits us. Nor can we pretend that procedure disappears because the person cannot request it. On the contrary, an inability to contest makes safeguards and the possibility of independent oversight more important.
But why should someone have rights if they cannot have reciprocal obligations? If we base all morality on exchange between equal partners, the question becomes uncomfortable. A newborn cannot pay for care. An unconscious person cannot promise gratitude. Someone with profound limitations may never be able to participate in negotiating the rules. A morality that excludes them for this reason would make bargaining power the condition of worth.
The position defended here is that the need for protection should not depend on the ability to provide something in return. Vulnerability does not, by itself, establish the entire architecture of rights, but it constitutes a serious reason for care. If a being can be affected, what we do to it must be justified. Its lack of accountability is not our lack of responsibility toward it.
This does not mean that every dangerous behavior must be allowed to continue. Protecting others and protecting the vulnerable person may require intervention. But the justification must be connected with necessity, proportionality, and competent assessment, not a wish to punish guilt the person could not have incurred. A diagnosis establishes neither the absence of responsibility nor dangerousness. A particular attribution requires a particular assessment.
Finally, refusing to think is not the same as being unable to. Someone who deliberately avoids relevant information to preserve an advantage may be accountable precisely for that avoidance. Nevertheless, the possibility of knowing and the avoidability of ignorance must be established, not inferred from our dislike. “You should have known” is a serious accusation. It must be accompanied by an explanation of what the person could have learned, how, and at what cost. [
The test of a society is not only how severely it judges those who can be held accountable. It is also how it protects those unable to construct a convincing defense. Listening only to eloquent people would be a very efficient way to confuse voice with worth.
4. The Homeless Man and the Night-Shift Worker
How Free Is a Choice Without Good Alternatives?
Someone is late for an appointment and loses an important opportunity. We can say they should have organized themselves. Perhaps so. But before issuing a verdict, we should find out whether they had somewhere to sleep, could charge their phone, knew the route, and had to choose between the appointment and an urgent need. This information does not automatically turn lateness into a virtue. But it changes what “should have” means.
When discussing the responsibility of a homeless person, two simplifications are common. The first reduces the person to a succession of bad choices. The second reduces them to a victim without any initiative. The first hides the constraints. The second hides the person. Both spare us the effort of understanding a particular life.
The terms are not interchangeable either. Asking for money on the street and lacking a stable home do not necessarily describe the same situation. Neither condition alone provides information about honesty, intelligence, or intentions. In this chapter’s scenarios, we will discuss people whose circumstances must be understood, not a human type to whom we could assign an entire psychology at a glance.
Research by Anandi Mani and colleagues, published in 2013, investigated the relationship between material deprivation and performance on cognitive tasks. The studies included prompting thoughts about financial problems and comparing the same farmers before and after harvest. The authors interpreted the findings as indications that financial pressure can consume cognitive resources. They did not establish any inherent intellectual inferiority among poor people. The designs do not permit generalization to every person and situation; seasonal comparisons are not equivalent to randomly assigning poverty. The article also received a methodological criticism published in the same journal, followed by a response from the authors. [
The moral relevance is more modest and more useful than the slogan “poverty makes you incapable.” An apparently simple requirement can demand resources an observer considers free only because they possess them. Keeping a document requires a safe place. Answering a call requires a working phone. Thinking long-term requires that tomorrow not be entirely consumed by the problem of survival. These are conditions for certain actions, not universal excuses for every deed.
Let us compare declining an appointment for convenience with missing it after a night without shelter. The administrative outcome may be identical: the person did not show up. But a system that reacts identically may confuse uniformity with fairness. Not every difference in treatment is favoritism. Sometimes it is the necessary adjustment that makes it possible to follow the rule in reality.
There is an opposite danger. If we assume that someone in difficulty cannot decide anything, we take away precisely the opportunity to rebuild their life. Consulting them does not mean attributing control over the whole context to them. It means not using that context to remove them from their own story. Support can expand the alternatives and leave the choice in their hands where that is possible.
Responsibility for one’s own life makes sense as participation in what can still be changed. It becomes cruel when used as a complete explanation of what happened. Someone can be encouraged to keep their documents, honor an agreement, and avoid hurting others. These requirements do not imply that homelessness is deserved punishment. Nor does the success of someone who overcame a difficult situation establish that everyone who failed is to blame.
