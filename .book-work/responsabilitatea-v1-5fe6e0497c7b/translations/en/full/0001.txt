Reading Note
Responsibility is discussed here as a problem of living together: what we can ask of one another and how we justify those demands. The book combines the history of ideas, psychological and sociological research, thought experiments, and moral arguments. Its history is selective; it does not assume a linear evolution from “primitive” cultures to a definitive morality.
Scenes without an identified historical source are hypothetical. They do not describe real people and should not be read as evidence of how frequently a behavior occurs. Their purpose is to reveal distinctions that an overly general concept can conceal.
Empirical findings are accompanied by numbered references to the bibliography at the end. Observation, statistical association, experimental intervention, and the authors’ interpretations are distinguished. A study can show how people behave under certain conditions; it cannot, by itself, establish what they are obliged to do. The proposals concerning education, institutions, and the distribution of obligations are arguments advanced by the book, not interventions whose effectiveness has already been demonstrated.
The sources were consulted for this text in September 2026. The bibliography includes original works, research ethics documents, and scholarly overviews offering guidance through philosophical disputes. The discussion of God examines concepts and arguments without presenting belief or unbelief as an experimental finding. Legal distinctions and examples of vulnerability are no substitute for a competent assessment of a particular case.
PROLOGUE. The Man Outside the Door
A man sits on the step of a building. It is cold. Beside him are a bag and an empty cup. We do not know whether he is asking for money, waiting for someone, or unable to continue on his way. One resident says it is not his problem. Another says it is the city council’s problem. Someone remarks that the man must have made bad choices. A woman asks whether anyone has called an assistance service. A child asks why nobody lets him inside.
The scene is imaginary. Precisely for that reason, we can afford not to invent a biography for the man that determines our reaction in advance. We will not turn him into a professor who happened to end up on the street so that he deserves compassion. We will not assign him an addiction in order to withdraw his right to it. We do not know whether he is generous, irritable, intelligent, or unbearable. We know only that he may need help.
Who is responsible for his situation? The question seems simple until we notice that it conceals other questions. Who helped bring it about? Who failed to meet an obligation? Who can intervene now? Who should bear the cost of intervening? Who ought to prevent the scene from recurring? The answers may point to different people. Someone may not be to blame for another person’s exposure to the cold and still have a serious reason to open a door. An institution may have a duty to intervene even though none of its employees caused the misfortune.
We do not ask the child to solve homelessness. But perhaps we ask the child not to throw stones. We do not ask the man on the step to reorganize the economy. But if he can understand and control himself, we can ask him not to strike the person approaching him. We do not ask the resident to turn his home into a permanent shelter. But perhaps we can ask him not to confuse uncertainty with condemnation. We do not ask the official to be omnipotent. We ask him to use the power he has honestly.
Then the question rises to another level. If there is a God who sees everything and can do everything, why should intervention fall to hurried, fragile, poorly informed human beings? If there is no God, where does their obligation come from? Fear? Empathy? A contract? The possibility that tomorrow they might be the ones on the step? And if one of them knows for certain that he will never end up there, does his responsibility disappear?
This book does not begin by assuming that every instance of suffering has someone to blame. Some misfortunes cannot be neatly apportioned between perpetrators and victims. Nor does it begin with the idea that we can live without answering to one another. A society in which every action is merely the effect of causes, and every obligation is someone else’s problem, does not automatically become more clear-sighted. It may simply become harder to live in.
I will defend a position: responsibility should be demanded in proportion to a genuine ability to understand and influence a situation, but it is not reducible to blame for the past. Power, knowledge, promises, and participation in institutions can create obligations for the future. These obligations must have limits; otherwise, conscientious people end up paying endlessly for the indifferent. Those limits must, in turn, be justified; otherwise, any privilege can declare itself powerless.
We will not find an instrument that measures blame as a thermometer measures temperature. But we can become more precise in the questions we ask. We can distinguish an explanation from an excuse, a limit from a pretext, an obligation from manipulation. And we can notice something unsettling: sometimes the question “who is to blame?” helps us achieve justice; at other times, it keeps us occupied until there is nothing left to save.
1. When We Say Someone Is Responsible
One Word, Several Processes
An engineer inspects a bridge. A worker installs a component. A director cuts the maintenance budget. A storm strikes the structure. The bridge collapses. The next morning, the public asks who bears responsibility. The question may mean who caused the accident, who should be investigated, who must compensate the victims, or who must ensure that the other bridges do not collapse. A single sentence is trying to do the work of several investigations.
The philosophy of responsibility distinguishes being a cause from being morally accountable. An object can cause a death without being a moral agent. When assessing a person, control and what that person could understand matter, among other things. Nor does an obligation to perform a task coincide with credit or blame for its outcome. Contemporary scholarship separates these questions precisely because they overlap only in part. [
In this book, causal responsibility will mean contributing to an outcome. Retrospective moral responsibility will concern the grounds for praising, criticizing, or blaming someone for what they did or failed to do. Role responsibility will concern the tasks attached to a position: parent, researcher, administrator, employee. Prospective responsibility will concern what must be done from now on. Reparative responsibility will concern restoring, compensating for, or acknowledging harm. These are working distinctions, not sealed compartments of life.
There is also legal liability. It depends on rules, procedures, evidence, and institutional authority. We will not deduce it directly from the book’s moral judgments. An act can be legal and still reprehensible; a moral obligation may not be appropriate for legal enforcement. Asking a friend to be attentive is different from asking the state to punish him for not being a good friend. Conversely, some compensation mechanisms aim to distribute a risk without declaring the person required to pay a bad human being.
We can now take the bridge case apart. The worker installed the component, but perhaps he was given defective material that the inspection required of him could not detect. The financial director never touched the structure, but perhaps she eliminated an inspection she knew was necessary. The engineer may have done everything that could reasonably be asked and still be the person best placed to organize urgent inspections. Her obligation for the next day does not establish her blame for the previous one.
The distinction protects us from two symmetrical injustices. The first is scapegoating: we seek out the person closest to the outcome and place the entire chain of causes in their arms. The second is the disappearance of obligations into complexity: because nobody controlled everything, we conclude that nobody owes anything. Between them lies the difficult work of identifying each person’s contributions, warnings, duties, alternatives, and actual power.
There is also responsibility as a character trait. We call someone responsible when we can rely on them. But trust needs an object. An accountant can be exceptionally consistent in administering a fraud. An official can flawlessly carry out an unjust procedure. Reliability and morality are not identical. To call a person responsible in a richer sense, we must also care about the things for which they consider themselves accountable, not merely the regularity with which they accomplish them.
Finally, publicly accepting responsibility is not necessarily accepting it in practice. “I take responsibility” can precede an explanation, a resignation, reparation, or absolutely nothing. The formula has substance only if it changes the relationship between the speaker and the consequences. Who may ask questions? What information must be provided? What must be corrected? What power may be withdrawn? Responsibility without the possibility of being questioned and without an obligation to change anything risks remaining a verbal ceremony.
Are We the Authors of Our Actions?
Two drivers check a message while driving. Nobody appears in front of the first. A pedestrian crosses in front of the second. The initial negligence is the same; the consequences are radically different. We have reasons to treat the need for reparation differently. But do we have equally strong reasons to believe that the second driver was, at the very moment of choosing, a much worse person?
The debate about moral luck, associated in its contemporary form with Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel, begins with this conflict. We want moral assessment to concern what depends on the agent, yet outcomes, circumstances, and even part of the agent’s character depend on factors they did not choose. The problem is not only the luck of avoiding an accident. It is also the luck of never having been placed in a situation that would have exposed our weaknesses. [
We do not know how courageous we are merely because we have not been threatened. We do not know how incorruptible we are merely because nobody has offered us something important. This observation should temper moral triumphalism, not suspend every judgment. Actions remain different. Someone who resisted pressure deserves recognition. But comparing people requires more than comparing their visible outcomes.
