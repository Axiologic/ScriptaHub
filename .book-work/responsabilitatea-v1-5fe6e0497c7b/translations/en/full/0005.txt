A good test would be to ask what concrete alternatives the person had. Not a heroic alternative we can imagine after the event, but one that was accessible, intelligible, and reasonable at the time. What information did they have? What did they risk? Who depended on them? This test does not guarantee leniency. Sometimes it will show that a better choice existed. But at least it will judge a real choice, not an observer’s idealized freedom whose cost the observer never paid.
Must Help Be Earned Through Innocence?
Suppose now that the man on the step really did make serious mistakes. He refused help, lied, and hurt people close to him. Is preventing him from freezing less urgent? We can regard certain acts as condemnable while also refusing to turn homelessness into a punishment without an end date.
Emergency help and assessment of the past serve different purposes. If we mix them, every encounter with a vulnerable person becomes a summary trial. The passerby would have to decide, within seconds, whether an unknown biography is clean enough. Such a criterion is not merely harsh. It is impossible to apply fairly and favors well-told stories over actual needs.
A rule for providing help can nevertheless have conditions. Other recipients and staff must be protected. Violence or exploitation do not become acceptable because their perpetrator suffers. The question is whether a condition actually protects something important or merely asks the person to prove they deserve to be treated as human. A safety limit, a rule for living together, and a test of moral purity are not the same thing.
A randomized study published by Sam Tsemberis, Leyla Gulcur, and Maria Nakae in 2004 compared a Housing First program with an approach conditional on stages of treatment. The program provided housing and support without first requiring abstinence or adherence to treatment. The research, with 225 participants initially assigned, found advantages in housing stability and perceived choice. It detected no between-group differences in substance use or psychiatric symptoms. Interpretation requires caution: exclusions occurred after randomization, and the population and context were specific. The finding supports a bounded intervention, not the idea that housing automatically solves every difficulty. [
The experiment nevertheless opens a moral question that the data alone cannot decide: why do we sometimes treat the conditions necessary for stability as rewards to be received only after stability has been achieved? If housing helps someone keep appointments and organize their life, refusing it until they become organized can create a circle. This argument alone does not establish the best policy for every city. It shows what should be compared and tested in place of an assumption about character.
A critic may reply that unconditional help risks encouraging harmful behavior or distributing resources unfairly. The objection deserves examination. We should ask which behaviors, with what measurable effect, and compared with what alternative. Conditions have costs too: administration, excluding eligible people, interrupting support. Identifying a risk of helping is not enough; it must be compared with the risks of refusing help.
There is also the problem of the person who works hard and can barely pay for housing. They may perceive support given to others as unjust. A serious response is not to despise their dissatisfaction. It is to ask why their security is so fragile and whether the system leaves them too close to the same precipice. Solidarity becomes difficult when people are offered only a choice between their own insecurity and their neighbor’s.
This book’s position is not that every form of help is good. It is that a person’s worth should not depend on success in a contest of innocence. We can require behavior compatible with others’ safety, evaluate programs, and correct abuses without turning a basic need into a moral privilege.
And the homeless person may, in turn, have responsibilities toward someone more vulnerable at that moment. They may notice someone who has fallen, warn a child, or refrain from exploiting another’s trust. Responsibility does not descend only from respectable people toward those being helped. It arises wherever there is a relevant ability to respond. Recognizing that ability is also a form of respect.
The Worker Who Sees the Risk but Cannot Stop the Factory
At the end of a shift, a worker notices an irregularity. He knows that reporting it may stop production. He also knows his boss rewards people who “do not create problems.” He is unsure how serious the risk is. He has loan payments, dependents, and few employment alternatives. Can we ask him to speak up? Usually, we have reasons to require at least a reasonable step. But what counts as reasonable also depends on the protection the organization offers him.
Professional responsibility has a component connected with competence. Whoever performs a task must pay attention to its relevant risks. But it also has an institutional component: the person must be able to report, investigate, and escalate a problem. An organization cannot reserve decision-making power for management and transfer all blame to employees for what they lacked the power to stop.
Let us distinguish three situations. In the first, the worker knows of a clear danger, has a functioning procedure, and can report it without disproportionate cost, but hides the problem for a bonus. In the second, he reports it repeatedly and management ignores the warnings. In the third, the information is incomplete and the procedure ambiguous. All three can lead to the same accident. They do not produce the same distribution of responsibility.
“I was following orders” is not a universal excuse. But “you could have refused” is not a complete argument either. We must establish what refusal entailed, how serious the harm was, how clear it was, and what other avenues existed. The more serious the foreseeable harm and the more direct the participation, the stronger the moral pressure to refuse. Nevertheless, a society that systematically relies on heroism from those with the least power and then abandons them has not solved its problem of responsibility.
A reporting channel makes sense only if it changes something. A form can record a report and bury it. A procedure can protect the institution from scandal without protecting people from risk. Morally, what matters is whether the message reaches someone able to decide, whether it is investigated, whether the whistleblower is treated fairly, and whether the outcome can be tracked. We are not discussing particular legal rights here, which depend on jurisdiction, but the function of an institution that claims to listen.
There is also an opposite limit: not every employee can be given an unlimited veto over the entire organization merely because they invoke a risk. Fears may be mistaken, information incomplete, conflicts real. Responsibility therefore requires procedures for investigation and decision, not replacing managerial authority with the authority of any suspicion. An alarm must be taken seriously without automatically being declared true.
Management’s responsibility is greater not because managers are a different moral species, but because they have access to information, can distribute resources, and can change rules. If they impose deadlines incompatible with inspections and then punish the person doing the work for a foreseeable error, they have not discovered an individual defect. They helped construct its conditions. Evidence would be needed to support this accusation in a real case; the scenario shows only the logic of attribution.
Decently performed work also has a positive dimension, easily overlooked in a book about blame. Maintaining a safe service, explaining a limitation to a customer, properly handing over a shift, and leaving usable documentation are forms of care toward strangers. They do not always require heroism. They require continuity, skill, and conditions in which these are possible.
The night-shift worker and the homeless man do not represent two species, one responsible and the other dependent. They represent different positions in the distribution of security, options, and power. A serious concept of responsibility must see those differences without turning either person into a mere illustration of a social theory.
5. The Crowd, the Citizen, and the Invisible Hands
Why Does Nobody Help—and Why Might the Question Be Wrong?
Someone collapses in a railway station. Twenty people are nearby. Each sees that the others see. Each might assume someone else has already called for help. A photograph of the scene could become evidence of modern indifference. But the photograph does not tell us who intervened a moment earlier, who is looking for a phone, who has not understood what is happening, or whether the situation is actually an emergency. Moral judgment needs more than a convincing frame.
In John Darley and Bibb Latané’s classic experiment, published in 1968, participants heard someone apparently having a seizure over a communication system. Intervention depended on how many other witnesses they believed were present. The study helped formulate the idea of diffusion of responsibility: the perceived presence of others can weaken the feeling that you, specifically, must act. Yet this was a precise experimental situation, not a definitive X-ray of human character. [
A meta-analysis published by Peter Fischer and colleagues in 2011, involving more than 7,700 participants and 105 independent effect estimates, found an overall bystander effect: the likelihood of individual intervention could decline in the presence of others. But the results depended on context. In situations perceived as dangerous, the effect was attenuated; others could also represent support, not merely an excuse for passivity. [
A very different investigation requires further caution. Richard Philpot and colleagues analyzed 219 recordings of public conflicts in three cities. At least one bystander intervened in approximately 91% of incidents. More bystanders were associated with a higher probability that someone would help. The study concerned visible conflicts in monitored spaces, not every imaginable emergency, and did not establish that every intervention resolved the situation. [
The two kinds of finding do not automatically contradict each other. The probability that a particular person will help is not the same as the probability that at least one of those present will help. Ten people, each less willing, may together offer more chances of intervention than a single person. The exact relationships depend on their interactions; the logical distinction remains valid.
This difference also changes the moral discussion. We cannot infer from the existence of diffusion of responsibility that crowds are inevitably cruel. Nor does the frequency of helping in certain conflicts mean we can comfortably rely on someone else. Describing a mechanism does not grant an individual an exemption. It shows where coordination can fail and where it is worth building.
Let us return to the station. One bystander explicitly asks whether help has been called. Another answers. A third looks for station staff. The situation is resolved not by a sudden increase in everyone’s virtue but by turning a diffuse obligation into identifiable actions. This is a hypothetical example, not a universal recipe for emergencies. The general idea is that responsibility sometimes needs a clear sentence before it needs a moral speech.
