We can independently formulate a test for any religious explanation of suffering. If the explanation makes me more attentive to what I can repair, it has a certain moral function. If it authorizes me to refuse help because I assume the victim is guilty in some invisible history, it requires an extremely strong justification. It is not enough that the explanation cannot be disproved. The very inaccessibility of the evidence should prevent us from using it as a verdict on another person’s life.
Belief in God as judge does not produce a single conception of responsibility either. We can conceive of obedience to a command, accountability for intention, fidelity to a covenant, or the obligation to care for the vulnerable. We can also conceive of the protest of someone who suffers without acknowledging the guilt attributed to them. The Book of Job, to which we will return, makes it impossible to reduce all religious reflection to the formula “you suffered, therefore you deserved it.” [
The tension between imposed law and justifiable law reappears in another form in Kant. Moral autonomy does not mean doing whatever you wish, but considering yourself bound by principles you can rationally uphold, not merely by your immediate interests. The formula of humanity requires that we not treat a person merely as a means. Universalization asks whether the rule by which we act can also be upheld when it does not benefit us. These ideas do not automatically turn every dilemma into a simple calculation. [
Think of an employer who says he can conceal a risk because workers need their wages. He uses their very vulnerability as an instrument for securing acceptance. A perspective centered on respect for persons asks whether consent can still perform its purported moral function when important information is deliberately withheld. The signed contract does not exhaust the issue. Sometimes the paper records an agreement; at other times, it certifies an asymmetry that the signature does not heal.
John Stuart Mill’s utilitarianism places the well-being of those affected at the center of assessment, rather than the prestige of the decision-maker’s intentions. Happiness and suffering do not become relevant only when they belong to the agent. Mill also discusses justice, rules, and motivation; he does not propose simply satisfying every impulse that produces immediate pleasure. Nevertheless, the orientation toward consequences leaves open the difficult question of sacrificing some for the benefit of others. [
We should not hastily choose here between two caricatures: the rigid person who follows the rule and lets the world burn, and the accountant who justifies every cruelty with a convenient total. The rule protects precisely because it cannot be suspended for every local advantage. The consequence matters precisely because the victim is not saved by the purity of our intention. Mature judgment must bear this tension rather than replace it with a slogan about good intentions or good results.
When the World Becomes Too Large for a Single Conscience
In a small community, you can see the person affected by your gesture. In a large organization, you see a task, an indicator, a stage. The person affected may appear much later, in another place, in another department. This distance must not be confused with the absence of a connection. Sometimes the division of labor distributes expertise; at other times, it also distributes the possibility of no longer seeing the overall result.
Durkheim analyzed solidarity based on similarity and the solidarity of a differentiated society in which people depend on one another’s functions. For him, the division of labor was not merely a matter of economic efficiency but also a problem of integration and moral regulation. His analysis does not oblige us to approve every existing order. It helps us notice that an individual’s everyday autonomy may depend on a social network they never encounter in its entirety. [
The interpretation proposed here is this: the more indirect dependence becomes, the more explicit the means of seeing obligations must be. We cannot rely solely on the emotion of an encounter. Someone inspecting a facility may never know the people protected by their work. That does not diminish the inspection’s value. A responsible society must make contributions that prevent harm without producing a heroic story both intelligible and worthy of reward.
In his 1919 lecture on politics, Max Weber placed the ethic of conviction and the ethic of responsibility in tension. One emphasizes fidelity to commitment, the other acceptance of an action’s foreseeable consequences. The distinction should be read neither as a license for cynicism nor as an obligation to abandon every principle. The problem is how someone can act in the world without hiding their effects behind inner purity. [
A leader who announces a generous reform but refuses to examine its implementation does not demonstrate generosity by ignoring failure. Conversely, a leader who continually justifies injustice through “realism” does not demonstrate responsibility merely by counting constraints. Conviction needs to confront the world; the analysis of consequences needs moral limits. Without the first correction, idealism can become indifference to effects. Without the second, pragmatism can become a technique of efficient domination.
Nietzsche challenged the presentation of guilt and punishment as transparent expressions of an obvious moral order. His genealogy investigates the relationships between morality, power, debt, and the formation of conscience. It does not follow that every moral criticism is false or that vulnerable people do not matter. What follows is an uncomfortable question for any institution distributing blame: whose interests are served by the precise way it has defined it? [
Ethics of care brought dependence and the work needed to sustain life to the fore. Carol Gilligan, Nel Noddings, Eva Feder Kittay, and Joan Tronto contributed in different ways to this shift in emphasis. Care is neither a feminine biological essence nor the abandonment of justice. One of its political questions is who provides care, under what conditions, and who can afford to appear independent because someone else takes care of their needs. [
Through this lens, the image of the self-sufficient adult changes. Someone who devotes their life to ideas may depend on another person who cooks, cleans, cares for a child, or accompanies an ill parent. If we call the first activity a vocation and the second merely a natural obligation, we have already distributed prestige and burden. Responsibility concerns more than who did wrong. It also concerns who maintains the conditions under which others can succeed.
Technological power adds a problem the book will follow to the end: effects can extend beyond their authors’ lifetimes. We need not assume that we can control the future to recognize that we can narrow the options of those who will inhabit it. The history of responsibility thus remains open. It moves from the question “what will happen to me because of what I did?” to the question “what might happen to others because we can do so much?”
3. The Child, the Adult, and the Mind That Cannot
When Does Obligation Begin?
A child breaks a glass. The adult asks why the child was not paying attention. Later, the same adult breaks another glass and says it was placed too close to the edge. The difference does not necessarily lie in the facts. It may lie in who has the authority to interpret them. Education in responsibility begins in a place where adults’ responsibility is already being tested.
A child is neither a miniature adult nor a being incapable of any moral participation. We must distinguish the ability to notice a need, understand a rule, anticipate a consequence, resist an impulse, and explain a choice. They need not all be present to the same degree for a child to participate in putting something right. But their partial absence makes automatically applying adult standards unjust.
In a study published in 2006, Felix Warneken and Michael Tomasello observed instrumental helping behavior in children around eighteen months old in situations where an adult encountered a practical difficulty. The finding matters because it shows that some forms of orientation toward another person’s goal emerge very early. It does not demonstrate that a child already possesses adult morality, that helping is free of all social influence, or that altruism will dominate in every situation. [
A child can pick up a dropped object and still not understand why they must wait, why a secret can hurt, or how someone excluded from a group feels. Helping with a simple task is not equivalent to being accountable for distant social consequences. That is precisely why we should use neither the early emergence of helping nor difficulties with self-control as an all-encompassing argument. The former does not make education unnecessary. The latter do not make the child’s participation pointless.
A reasonable educational proposal is to assign responsibilities according to the task and available support, rather than a global label. A child may have the task of putting a toy away, but the adult must make the place accessible. The child may help repair damage but is not obliged to manage the adult’s anger. The child can be taught to say what happened but must not be threatened until they produce the desired confession.
Let us compare two reactions to the broken glass. In the first, the adult says that only a careless, bad child does such things. In the second, the adult makes sure nobody gets hurt, asks what happened, and involves the child, according to their abilities, in putting things right. The second reaction does not guarantee a superior moral character. But its structure is more justifiable: it connects accountability with the action, offers a way to participate, and does not turn a mistake into a permanent identity.
Is there a right to make mistakes? Not an unlimited right to hurt others. There is, however, a strong reason to create situations in which learning mistakes are possible, detectable, and repairable. We cannot demand the development of independent judgment while forbidding every opportunity to exercise it. An education that controls every choice may secure compliance; it has not yet tested whether it has developed responsibility.
Here a more difficult task arises for the adult: sometimes accepting an uncomfortable answer. A child who asks why a rule is unjust is not automatically irresponsible. They may be beginning to distinguish authority from justification. If education teaches children that good is whatever the more powerful person says, it should not be surprised when they later justify an action by saying they were told to do it.
A child’s responsibility must therefore grow with the ability to participate, but also with the right to be heard. Assigning tasks alone, without offering explanations or recognizing limits, means constructing obedience under the name of maturity. Withdrawing every task for fear of asking too much means postponing precisely the experiences through which children might learn to take responsibility.
The Child Is Not Responsible for the Adult’s Life
“After everything I have done for you” can introduce a legitimate request for gratitude. It can also introduce an endless debt. The parent provided care and now demands the choice of profession, partner, or entire way of life. Past care is transformed into a title of ownership over someone else’s future.
