Reading Note
Responsibility is discussed here as a problem of living together: what
we can ask of one another and how we justify those demands. The
book combines the history of ideas, psychological and sociological re‐
search, thought experiments, and moral arguments. Its history is se‐
lective; it does not assume a linear evolution from “primitive” cultures
to a definitive morality.
Scenes without an identified historical source are hypothetical. They
do not describe real people and should not be read as evidence of how
frequently a behavior occurs. Their purpose is to reveal distinctions
that an overly general concept can conceal.
Empirical findings are accompanied by numbered references to the
bibliography at the end. Observation, statistical association, experi‐
mental intervention, and the authors’ interpretations are distin‐
guished. A study can show how people behave under certain condi‐
tions; it cannot, by itself, establish what they are obliged to do. The
proposals concerning education, institutions, and the distribution of
obligations are arguments advanced by the book, not interventions
whose effectiveness has already been demonstrated.
The sources were consulted for this text in September 2026. The bibli‐
ography includes original works, research ethics documents, and
scholarly overviews offering guidance through philosophical disputes.
The discussion of God examines concepts and arguments without
presenting belief or unbelief as an experimental finding. Legal distinc‐
tions and examples of vulnerability are no substitute for a competent
assessment of a particular case.

PROLOGUE. The Man Outside
the Door
A man sits on the step of a building. It is cold. Beside him are a bag
and an empty cup. We do not know whether he is asking for money,
waiting for someone, or unable to continue on his way. One resident
says it is not his problem. Another says it is the city council’s problem.
Someone remarks that the man must have made bad choices. A wo‐
man asks whether anyone has called an assistance service. A child
asks why nobody lets him inside.
The scene is imaginary. Precisely for that reason, we can afford not to
invent a biography for the man that determines our reaction in ad‐
vance. We will not turn him into a professor who happened to end up
on the street so that he deserves compassion. We will not assign him
an addiction in order to withdraw his right to it. We do not know
whether he is generous, irritable, intelligent, or unbearable. We know
only that he may need help.
Who is responsible for his situation? The question seems simple until
we notice that it conceals other questions. Who helped bring it about?
Who failed to meet an obligation? Who can intervene now? Who
should bear the cost of intervening? Who ought to prevent the scene
from recurring? The answers may point to different people. Someone
may not be to blame for another person’s exposure to the cold and
still have a serious reason to open a door. An institution may have a
duty to intervene even though none of its employees caused the mis‐
fortune.
We do not ask the child to solve homelessness. But perhaps we ask the
child not to throw stones. We do not ask the man on the step to reor‐
ganize the economy. But if he can understand and control himself, we
can ask him not to strike the person approaching him. We do not ask
the resident to turn his home into a permanent shelter. But perhaps
we can ask him not to confuse uncertainty with condemnation. We do
not ask the official to be omnipotent. We ask him to use the power he
has honestly.
4


Then the question rises to another level. If there is a God who sees
everything and can do everything, why should intervention fall to
hurried, fragile, poorly informed human beings? If there is no God,
where does their obligation come from? Fear? Empathy? A contract?
The possibility that tomorrow they might be the ones on the step? And
if one of them knows for certain that he will never end up there, does
his responsibility disappear?
This book does not begin by assuming that every instance of suffering
has someone to blame. Some misfortunes cannot be neatly appor‐
tioned between perpetrators and victims. Nor does it begin with the
idea that we can live without answering to one another. A society in
which every action is merely the effect of causes, and every obligation
is someone else’s problem, does not automatically become more clear-
sighted. It may simply become harder to live in.
I will defend a position: responsibility should be demanded in propor‐
tion to a genuine ability to understand and influence a situation, but it
is not reducible to blame for the past. Power, knowledge, promises,
and participation in institutions can create obligations for the future.
These obligations must have limits; otherwise, conscientious people
end up paying endlessly for the indifferent. Those limits must, in turn,
be justified; otherwise, any privilege can declare itself powerless.
We will not find an instrument that measures blame as a thermomet‐
er measures temperature. But we can become more precise in the
questions we ask. We can distinguish an explanation from an excuse,
a limit from a pretext, an obligation from manipulation. And we can
notice something unsettling: sometimes the question “who is to
blame?” helps us achieve justice; at other times, it keeps us occupied
until there is nothing left to save.

1. When We Say Someone Is
Responsible
One Word, Several Processes
An engineer inspects a bridge. A worker installs a component. A dir‐
ector cuts the maintenance budget. A storm strikes the structure. The
bridge collapses. The next morning, the public asks who bears re‐
sponsibility. The question may mean who caused the accident, who
should be investigated, who must compensate the victims, or who
must ensure that the other bridges do not collapse. A single sentence
is trying to do the work of several investigations.
The philosophy of responsibility distinguishes being a cause from be‐
ing morally accountable. An object can cause a death without being a
moral agent. When assessing a person, control and what that person
could understand matter, among other things. Nor does an obligation
to perform a task coincide with credit or blame for its outcome. Con‐
temporary scholarship separates these questions precisely because
they overlap only in part. [1]
In this book, causal responsibility will mean contributing to an out‐
come. Retrospective moral responsibility will concern the grounds for
praising, criticizing, or blaming someone for what they did or failed to
do. Role responsibility will concern the tasks attached to a position:
parent, researcher, administrator, employee. Prospective responsibil‐
ity will concern what must be done from now on. Reparative respons‐
ibility will concern restoring, compensating for, or acknowledging
harm. These are working distinctions, not sealed compartments of
life.
There is also legal liability. It depends on rules, procedures, evidence,
and institutional authority. We will not deduce it directly from the
book’s moral judgments. An act can be legal and still reprehensible; a
moral obligation may not be appropriate for legal enforcement. Ask‐
ing a friend to be attentive is different from asking the state to punish
6


him for not being a good friend. Conversely, some compensation
mechanisms aim to distribute a risk without declaring the person re‐
quired to pay a bad human being.
We can now take the bridge case apart. The worker installed the com‐
ponent, but perhaps he was given defective material that the inspec‐
tion required of him could not detect. The financial director never
touched the structure, but perhaps she eliminated an inspection she
knew was necessary. The engineer may have done everything that
could reasonably be asked and still be the person best placed to organ‐
ize urgent inspections. Her obligation for the next day does not estab‐
lish her blame for the previous one.
The distinction protects us from two symmetrical injustices. The first
is scapegoating: we seek out the person closest to the outcome and
place the entire chain of causes in their arms. The second is the disap‐
pearance of obligations into complexity: because nobody controlled
everything, we conclude that nobody owes anything. Between them
lies the difficult work of identifying each person’s contributions,
warnings, duties, alternatives, and actual power.
There is also responsibility as a character trait. We call someone re‐
sponsible when we can rely on them. But trust needs an object. An ac‐
countant can be exceptionally consistent in administering a fraud. An
official can flawlessly carry out an unjust procedure. Reliability and
morality are not identical. To call a person responsible in a richer
sense, we must also care about the things for which they consider
themselves accountable, not merely the regularity with which they ac‐
complish them.
Finally, publicly accepting responsibility is not necessarily accepting it
in practice. “I take responsibility” can precede an explanation, a resig‐
nation, reparation, or absolutely nothing. The formula has substance
only if it changes the relationship between the speaker and the con‐
sequences. Who may ask questions? What information must be
provided? What must be corrected? What power may be withdrawn?
Responsibility without the possibility of being questioned and without
an obligation to change anything risks remaining a verbal ceremony.

Are We the Authors of Our Actions?
Two drivers check a message while driving. Nobody appears in front
of the first. A pedestrian crosses in front of the second. The initial neg‐
ligence is the same; the consequences are radically different. We have
reasons to treat the need for reparation differently. But do we have
equally strong reasons to believe that the second driver was, at the
very moment of choosing, a much worse person?
The debate about moral luck, associated in its contemporary form
with Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel, begins with this conflict.
We want moral assessment to concern what depends on the agent, yet
outcomes, circumstances, and even part of the agent’s character de‐
pend on factors they did not choose. The problem is not only the luck
of avoiding an accident. It is also the luck of never having been placed
in a situation that would have exposed our weaknesses. [2]
We do not know how courageous we are merely because we have not
been threatened. We do not know how incorruptible we are merely
because nobody has offered us something important. This observation
should temper moral triumphalism, not suspend every judgment. Ac‐
tions remain different. Someone who resisted pressure deserves re‐
cognition. But comparing people requires more than comparing their
visible outcomes.
The question goes deeper still. We did not choose our genes, family,
language, or earliest role models. If every choice comes from a per‐
sonality shaped by prior causes, where does genuine authorship be‐
gin? Incompatibilists hold that certain forms of determination
threaten the freedom responsibility requires. Compatibilists argue
that an action can be free in a relevant sense if it arises from a per‐
son’s deliberation and ability to respond to reasons, even in a causally
governed world. Simply invoking chance does not solve the problem
either: a random movement does not thereby become my choice. [1]
Let us take an example without spectacular metaphysics. One person
turns on a tap because he wants water. Another turns it on after
someone physically forces his hand. The final movement may be
identical. But the explanations differ in how the person participates in
producing it. In the first case, his information, purpose, and delibera‐
8


tion play a role. In the second, they are bypassed. Even if both epis‐
odes have causes, the difference remains relevant to how we can de‐
mand explanations and change behavior.
This does not establish the existence of absolute desert, independent
of a person’s entire history. It establishes only that the statement
“everything has causes” does not by itself erase every distinction
between coercion, reflection, manipulation, negligence, and intention.
We can reject the idea that someone deserves suffering merely to re‐
store the universe’s metaphysical balance while still preserving the
protection of victims, the establishment of facts, demands for explana‐
tions, and the obligation to make reparation.
Knowledge matters to such demands too. Not having known may be
an excuse, but sometimes the failure to check is precisely the problem.
The literature calls this the epistemic dimension of responsibility. Not
all ignorance is culpable: we must investigate whether the person
could have recognized the need to become informed and whether
they had reasonable means to do so. A standard that demands every‐
one foresee every consequence no longer distinguishes a human be‐
ing from an oracle. [3]
Imagine two people providing the same false information. One re‐
peats, in good faith, a credible announcement they had no reason to
suspect. The other ignores three warnings because the truth would
undermine their advantage. Correcting the information is necessary
in both cases. The reproach need not be identical. Here we see the ad‐
vantage of differentiated responsibility: it can demand repair without
inventing identical blame, and criticize the avoidance of knowledge
without punishing the mind’s inevitable limitations.
Real, Invented, or Both?
We cannot put responsibility in a container. But neither a promise, a
financial debt, nor a marriage is that kind of object. The absence of a
separate physical body does not make a concept unreal. Some realities
exist through shared rules and relationships. They can change lives
even though they would not exist in a universe without beings cap‐
able of understanding them.
9


Yet the fact that a rule exists socially does not establish that it is just. A
community can rigorously enforce an absurd obligation. The sociolo‐
gical reality of attributed guilt does not guarantee the moral truth of
the accusation. We must therefore separate two questions: do people
actually organize the world through responsibility? And are the par‐
ticular ways they distribute it justified? The first can be investigated
through observation, documents, experiments, and history. The
second requires arguments about reasons, rights, interests, and the
acceptable treatment of persons.
Suppose a village considers a thief’s family guilty of the theft, al‐
though its members neither helped nor knew anything about it. The
norm is socially real. It produces shame, exclusion, and perhaps viol‐
ence. But precisely its reality makes it open to criticism. We do not
need to deny the practice’s existence to deny its legitimacy. Much of
the moral progress we can imagine consists in this separation: institu‐
tions exist, but they are not sacred merely because they function.
Moral realism, constructivism, and various forms of antirealism offer
divergent answers about the ultimate status of moral judgments.
Broadly speaking, realism holds that moral truths exist; many of its
forms regard them as independent of our approval. For a constructiv‐
ist, their justification depends on how they would emerge from an ap‐
propriate rational procedure, not on mere whim. An antirealist may
explain moral language through attitudes and practices without ac‐
cepting independent moral properties. These families contain internal
disagreements, and some forms of constructivism may be compatible
with realism; the labels do not settle the issue. [4]
We can, however, move forward without claiming to have settled the
ontology of all morality. If someone accepts that their own suffering
matters, we can ask them to explain why another person’s similar suf‐
fering should count for nothing. If they accept that other people’s
promises create legitimate expectations for them, we can ask them to
explain why their own promises should be decorative. These ques‐
tions do not logically convert every egoist. They expose the cost and
asymmetry of that person’s position. Moral philosophy has no proof
that can force everyone to care; it has ways of making a refusal to
care harder to hide behind an appearance of neutrality.
10


I propose treating responsibility as a justifiable relationship between
an agent, something that depends on them, and other beings whose
interests are affected. “Justifiable” is essential. Not every expectation
others have becomes my obligation. A group may demand obedience,
a partner may demand that I give up my entire life, an institution may
demand silence. The demand must be assessed, not merely recorded.
This working definition rejects both a universe in which every pain
automatically conceals a sin and a social universe in which nobody
owes anybody anything. It leaves room for unchosen obligations: a de‐
pendent child has signed no contract with its parents; someone acci‐
dentally struck may need help from the person who struck them; an
administrator assumes tasks generated by their position. Not having
chosen the whole situation does not mean that all our reactions are
equally justifiable.
Responsibility is therefore real at least as a social practice and as a
problem of justification. Whether it also exists in the stronger sense of
independent moral truths remains a philosophical dispute. But
nobody can eliminate the problem merely by saying the word “inven‐
ted.” Bridges are constructed too. The relevant question is whether
they hold, whom they allow to cross, and who is crushed when they
collapse.

2. A History of Those Who Must
Answer
From Action to Character, from Character to
Relationship
Imagine a hearing in which everyone agrees on the facts but nobody
agrees on the question. One participant asks what the person did. An‐
other asks what kind of person they became. A third asks which role
they betrayed. A fourth wants to know what consequences they could
foresee. The history of responsibility is not merely a succession of an‐
swers to the same question. It also involves changes in which question
is considered decisive.
There is no single genealogy in which humanity departed from super‐
stition and proceeded in an orderly way to the modern rational indi‐
vidual. Different traditions have combined intention, consequence, re‐
lationship, law, and character. Disputes have existed within the same
tradition. We will follow several conceptual turning points without
turning their selection into a complete history of the world, and
without assuming that our vocabulary was already that of ancient au‐
thors.
In Book III of the Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle examines voluntary
actions, coercion, and ignorance. The difficult cases are “mixed”: the
person who throws cargo overboard to save the ship makes a choice,
but in circumstances they would not have wished for. The analysis
does not allow every act to be reduced to free choice or the complete
absence of choice. It calls for examining particular circumstances and
what the agent knew. [5]
This observation remains fruitful. A worker accepts a risky task to
avoid losing income. Nobody physically pushes them into it, but
neither are they choosing from a menu of equal possibilities. We can
acknowledge the choice without idealizing its freedom. We can criti‐
12


cize conduct without pretending that its author had the same power
as the person who imposed the conditions. This middle ground is pre‐
cisely what discussions of merit often erase.
Virtue ethics also shifts attention toward the formation of the person.
An action does not come from nothing: habits, ways of noticing, and
practical judgment contribute to it. In the Aristotelian tradition, prac‐
tical wisdom does not mean instrumental skill alone. Someone can
calculate very well how to achieve a bad end. Understanding what is
worth pursuing is part of the problem. Yet virtue ethics is not alone in
valuing character, just as rule-based ethics does not automatically ig‐
nore consequences. [6]
From this perspective, a society concerned with responsibility cannot
regard education as simply transmitting prohibitions. It must ask
what kinds of people its rewards produce. If it admires success re‐
gardless of the means, it cannot be sincerely surprised when concern
for the means weakens. This is a normative criticism of its consist‐
ency, not a psychological law according to which every person will
copy rewarded behavior.
In the Confucian tradition, moral life is closely connected with rela‐
tionships, cultivation, and roles. The idea should not be reduced to
mechanical obedience. A ruler’s conduct and moral example matter,
and relationships involve expectations of several parties, even if they
are not organized according to contemporary egalitarianism. A role
describes not only what you may ask of others but also what you must
become to fulfill it properly. [7]
Mencius uses the case of a child at risk of falling into a well to discuss
spontaneous compassion. He also connects good government with the
material conditions under which people can cultivate a moral life. In
ancient Chinese debate, this differentiated concern for those close to
us encountered the challenge of impartial concern associated with
Mozi. We therefore have no single “Eastern mentality,” but a dispute
about the relationship between affection, order, and obligations to‐
ward strangers. [8]
The dispute still concerns us. A parent who favors their child at their
own table is not doing the same thing as an official who favors that
child in a public selection process. A relationship can create a special
13


duty in one context and a conflict of interest in another. History offers
more than ornamental wisdom here: it forces us to acknowledge that
the same apparent virtue, loyalty, can protect a vulnerable being or
defend an injustice.
Duty Before a Visible or Invisible Judge
People have also located accountability beyond immediately visible
relationships. If actions have moral consequences within a cosmic or‐
der, escaping the community’s sanctions does not close the case. This
idea can limit the powerful person’s arbitrariness: winning does not
automatically mean being right. But it can also encourage the inter‐
pretation of misfortune as deserved punishment. The two uses must
be separated, even when expressed in the same religious vocabulary.
In Indian Buddhist traditions, intention plays a central role in assess‐
ing action and understanding karma. These traditions connect moral
practice with reducing suffering and transforming mental disposi‐
tions. They cannot be compressed into the slogan that every suffering
person has received exactly what they deserve. The doctrines, inter‐
pretations, and their relationship to compassion are more complex
than a popular accounting of cosmic rewards. [9]
We can independently formulate a test for any religious explanation
of suffering. If the explanation makes me more attentive to what I can
repair, it has a certain moral function. If it authorizes me to refuse
help because I assume the victim is guilty in some invisible history, it
requires an extremely strong justification. It is not enough that the ex‐
planation cannot be disproved. The very inaccessibility of the evid‐
ence should prevent us from using it as a verdict on another person’s
life.
Belief in God as judge does not produce a single conception of re‐
sponsibility either. We can conceive of obedience to a command, ac‐
countability for intention, fidelity to a covenant, or the obligation to
care for the vulnerable. We can also conceive of the protest of
someone who suffers without acknowledging the guilt attributed to
them. The Book of Job, to which we will return, makes it impossible to
reduce all religious reflection to the formula “you suffered, therefore
you deserved it.” [10]
14


The tension between imposed law and justifiable law reappears in an‐
other form in Kant. Moral autonomy does not mean doing whatever
you wish, but considering yourself bound by principles you can ra‐
tionally uphold, not merely by your immediate interests. The formula
of humanity requires that we not treat a person merely as a means.
Universalization asks whether the rule by which we act can also be
upheld when it does not benefit us. These ideas do not automatically
turn every dilemma into a simple calculation. [11]
Think of an employer who says he can conceal a risk because workers
need their wages. He uses their very vulnerability as an instrument
for securing acceptance. A perspective centered on respect for per‐
sons asks whether consent can still perform its purported moral func‐
tion when important information is deliberately withheld. The signed
contract does not exhaust the issue. Sometimes the paper records an
agreement; at other times, it certifies an asymmetry that the signature
does not heal.
John Stuart Mill’s utilitarianism places the well-being of those affected
at the center of assessment, rather than the prestige of the decision-
maker’s intentions. Happiness and suffering do not become relevant
only when they belong to the agent. Mill also discusses justice, rules,
and motivation; he does not propose simply satisfying every impulse
that produces immediate pleasure. Nevertheless, the orientation to‐
ward consequences leaves open the difficult question of sacrificing
some for the benefit of others. [12]
We should not hastily choose here between two caricatures: the rigid
person who follows the rule and lets the world burn, and the account‐
ant who justifies every cruelty with a convenient total. The rule pro‐
tects precisely because it cannot be suspended for every local advant‐
age. The consequence matters precisely because the victim is not
saved by the purity of our intention. Mature judgment must bear this
tension rather than replace it with a slogan about good intentions or
good results.

When the World Becomes Too Large for a Single
Conscience
In a small community, you can see the person affected by your ges‐
ture. In a large organization, you see a task, an indicator, a stage. The
person affected may appear much later, in another place, in another
department. This distance must not be confused with the absence of a
connection. Sometimes the division of labor distributes expertise; at
other times, it also distributes the possibility of no longer seeing the
overall result.
Durkheim analyzed solidarity based on similarity and the solidarity of
a differentiated society in which people depend on one another’s func‐
tions. For him, the division of labor was not merely a matter of eco‐
nomic efficiency but also a problem of integration and moral regula‐
tion. His analysis does not oblige us to approve every existing order. It
helps us notice that an individual’s everyday autonomy may depend
on a social network they never encounter in its entirety. [13]
The interpretation proposed here is this: the more indirect depend‐
ence becomes, the more explicit the means of seeing obligations must
be. We cannot rely solely on the emotion of an encounter. Someone in‐
specting a facility may never know the people protected by their
work. That does not diminish the inspection’s value. A responsible so‐
ciety must make contributions that prevent harm without producing a
heroic story both intelligible and worthy of reward.
In his 1919 lecture on politics, Max Weber placed the ethic of convic‐
tion and the ethic of responsibility in tension. One emphasizes fidelity
to commitment, the other acceptance of an action’s foreseeable con‐
sequences. The distinction should be read neither as a license for cyn‐
icism nor as an obligation to abandon every principle. The problem is
how someone can act in the world without hiding their effects behind
inner purity. [14]
A leader who announces a generous reform but refuses to examine its
implementation does not demonstrate generosity by ignoring failure.
Conversely, a leader who continually justifies injustice through “real‐
ism” does not demonstrate responsibility merely by counting con‐
straints. Conviction needs to confront the world; the analysis of con‐
16


sequences needs moral limits. Without the first correction, idealism
can become indifference to effects. Without the second, pragmatism
can become a technique of efficient domination.
Nietzsche challenged the presentation of guilt and punishment as
transparent expressions of an obvious moral order. His genealogy in‐
vestigates the relationships between morality, power, debt, and the
formation of conscience. It does not follow that every moral criticism
is false or that vulnerable people do not matter. What follows is an un‐
comfortable question for any institution distributing blame: whose in‐
terests are served by the precise way it has defined it? [15]
Ethics of care brought dependence and the work needed to sustain life
to the fore. Carol Gilligan, Nel Noddings, Eva Feder Kittay, and Joan
Tronto contributed in different ways to this shift in emphasis. Care is
neither a feminine biological essence nor the abandonment of justice.
One of its political questions is who provides care, under what condi‐
tions, and who can afford to appear independent because someone
else takes care of their needs. [16]
Through this lens, the image of the self-sufficient adult changes.
Someone who devotes their life to ideas may depend on another per‐
son who cooks, cleans, cares for a child, or accompanies an ill parent.
If we call the first activity a vocation and the second merely a natural
obligation, we have already distributed prestige and burden. Respons‐
ibility concerns more than who did wrong. It also concerns who main‐
tains the conditions under which others can succeed.
Technological power adds a problem the book will follow to the end:
effects can extend beyond their authors’ lifetimes. We need not as‐
sume that we can control the future to recognize that we can narrow
the options of those who will inhabit it. The history of responsibility
thus remains open. It moves from the question “what will happen to
me because of what I did?” to the question “what might happen to oth‐
ers because we can do so much?”

3. The Child, the Adult, and the
Mind That Cannot
When Does Obligation Begin?
A child breaks a glass. The adult asks why the child was not paying at‐
tention. Later, the same adult breaks another glass and says it was
placed too close to the edge. The difference does not necessarily lie in
the facts. It may lie in who has the authority to interpret them. Educa‐
tion in responsibility begins in a place where adults’ responsibility is
already being tested.
A child is neither a miniature adult nor a being incapable of any mor‐
al participation. We must distinguish the ability to notice a need, un‐
derstand a rule, anticipate a consequence, resist an impulse, and ex‐
plain a choice. They need not all be present to the same degree for a
child to participate in putting something right. But their partial ab‐
sence makes automatically applying adult standards unjust.
In a study published in 2006, Felix Warneken and Michael Tomasello
observed instrumental helping behavior in children around eighteen
months old in situations where an adult encountered a practical diffi‐
culty. The finding matters because it shows that some forms of orient‐
ation toward another person’s goal emerge very early. It does not
demonstrate that a child already possesses adult morality, that helping
is free of all social influence, or that altruism will dominate in every
situation. [17]
A child can pick up a dropped object and still not understand why
they must wait, why a secret can hurt, or how someone excluded from
a group feels. Helping with a simple task is not equivalent to being ac‐
countable for distant social consequences. That is precisely why we
should use neither the early emergence of helping nor difficulties
with self-control as an all-encompassing argument. The former does
not make education unnecessary. The latter do not make the child’s
participation pointless.
18


A reasonable educational proposal is to assign responsibilities accord‐
ing to the task and available support, rather than a global label. A
child may have the task of putting a toy away, but the adult must
make the place accessible. The child may help repair damage but is
not obliged to manage the adult’s anger. The child can be taught to say
what happened but must not be threatened until they produce the de‐
sired confession.
Let us compare two reactions to the broken glass. In the first, the adult
says that only a careless, bad child does such things. In the second, the
adult makes sure nobody gets hurt, asks what happened, and involves
the child, according to their abilities, in putting things right. The
second reaction does not guarantee a superior moral character. But its
structure is more justifiable: it connects accountability with the ac‐
tion, offers a way to participate, and does not turn a mistake into a
permanent identity.
Is there a right to make mistakes? Not an unlimited right to hurt oth‐
ers. There is, however, a strong reason to create situations in which
learning mistakes are possible, detectable, and repairable. We cannot
demand the development of independent judgment while forbidding
every opportunity to exercise it. An education that controls every
choice may secure compliance; it has not yet tested whether it has de‐
veloped responsibility.
Here a more difficult task arises for the adult: sometimes accepting an
uncomfortable answer. A child who asks why a rule is unjust is not
automatically irresponsible. They may be beginning to distinguish au‐
thority from justification. If education teaches children that good is
whatever the more powerful person says, it should not be surprised
when they later justify an action by saying they were told to do it.
A child’s responsibility must therefore grow with the ability to parti‐
cipate, but also with the right to be heard. Assigning tasks alone,
without offering explanations or recognizing limits, means construct‐
ing obedience under the name of maturity. Withdrawing every task
for fear of asking too much means postponing precisely the experi‐
ences through which children might learn to take responsibility.

The Child Is Not Responsible for the Adult’s Life
“After everything I have done for you” can introduce a legitimate re‐
quest for gratitude. It can also introduce an endless debt. The parent
provided care and now demands the choice of profession, partner, or
entire way of life. Past care is transformed into a title of ownership
over someone else’s future.
The child did not ask to be brought into the world. It does not follow
that all their subsequent relationships are free of obligations. It does
follow, however, that basic care cannot be treated as a loan taken out
by an absent debtor. A relationship can generate affection, gratitude,
and, later, mutual help. It does not automatically justify unlimited sub‐
mission.
Responsibility for providing care must not be confused with respons‐
ibility for guaranteeing the happiness of the person receiving it. A par‐
ent can do serious and reasonable things without being able to control
all the child’s suffering. An adult child can support a parent without
being able to cure every dissatisfaction. The distinction matters be‐
cause an impossible obligation can become an instrument of domina‐
tion: whatever the other person does, there is always another pain for
which to blame them.
A responsible school should teach this limit too. Sometimes a pupil
needs to learn that they may ask for help, that they need not mediate
every family conflict, and that a more powerful person’s mistake does
not become their fault. This is a proposal for the moral organization of
education, not a diagnosis of any particular family. Applying it re‐
quires attention to context and competent support when there is a
real risk to the child.
At the same time, protection must not become the elimination of
every contribution. Helping care for the home or showing considera‐
tion for a tired parent can be a natural form of cooperation. The cri‐
terion is not whether a task exists, but whether it suits the child’s abil‐
ities, whether it avoids sacrificing their fundamental needs, and
whether the adult retains responsibility for adult life. A child can help
at the table without becoming the family’s emotional administrator.
20


In school assessment, responsibility can also get lost in numbers. A
pupil has a poor grade; the conclusion is that they did not try hard
enough. That may be true. But the grade alone does not establish the
explanation. The teacher must distinguish the outcome, effort, under‐
standing, access to support, and how the task was designed. Nor
should difficulty automatically become a general excuse. A proper in‐
vestigation avoids both reflexes.
Suppose a pupil submits a text produced by an AI system and presents
it as her own work. Calling her lazy is not enough, just as saying tech‐
nology makes the situation inevitable is not enough. What require‐
ment was communicated to her? Which part was she supposed to do
herself? What forms of help were permitted? Did she understand the
difference between assistance and false attribution? If she understood
and deliberately concealed it, there is an issue of honesty. If the rules
were unclear, the institution also has something to put right.
A better assessment can require explaining decisions, discussing
sources, and revising an error. It does not eliminate every form of
cheating. But it shifts the emphasis from the appearance of a flawless
product to the ability to answer for it. That would be a deeper educa‐
tional objective than producing pupils who know how to give the ex‐
pected answer but cannot say why it should be believed.
Moral maturity does not begin when a child no longer needs anyone.
Such a moment may not exist even for adults. It begins when their
participation becomes more clear-sighted: they recognize effects, can
offer reasons, accept correction, and help put things right without be‐
lieving that every mistake excludes them from the community.
What Do We Owe Those Who Cannot Be Held
Accountable?
The expression “cannot think” gathers together profoundly different
situations. One person may be unconscious. Another may have signi‐
ficant cognitive difficulties but be able to express preferences.
Someone may not understand a complex decision yet understand a
close relationship perfectly. Another has the capacity to reflect but
21


does not use it at a particular moment. Placing all these situations in
the same category is already a moral error before it becomes a prac‐
tical one.
We must separate a person’s worth, their decision-making capacity,
and their accountability for an action. Worth is not measured by intel‐
lectual performance. Capacity must be discussed in relation to a task
and context, not automatically inferred from someone’s prestige, dia‐
gnosis, or vocabulary. Accountability for an act requires examining
the relevant ability to understand and control it. Someone may need
support in one area and participate autonomously in another.
The 1979 Belmont Report sets out a twofold requirement for research
involving human participants: respecting autonomy and protecting
people whose autonomy is diminished. The document connects con‐
sent with information, comprehension, and voluntariness. It is not a
complete theory of all human relationships, but it shows why respect
is reducible neither to abandoning someone before an impossible
choice nor to having someone else take over all their choices. [18]
Imagine a woman who cannot compare the terms of a complicated
contract on her own but knows where she wants to live and with
whom she feels safe. Needing help with the contract does not erase
her preferences about her life. Justifiable support tries to make the
choice intelligible and possible. Abusive control uses the difficulty as a
pretext for no longer asking her anything.
There are situations in which deliberation or expressing a choice is
impossible. The question then shifts toward protection, interests,
known preferences, and limits on the power of those deciding. We
cannot derive approval from silence for whatever suits us. Nor can we
pretend that procedure disappears because the person cannot request
it. On the contrary, an inability to contest makes safeguards and the
possibility of independent oversight more important.
But why should someone have rights if they cannot have reciprocal
obligations? If we base all morality on exchange between equal part‐
ners, the question becomes uncomfortable. A newborn cannot pay for
care. An unconscious person cannot promise gratitude. Someone with
22


profound limitations may never be able to participate in negotiating
the rules. A morality that excludes them for this reason would make
bargaining power the condition of worth.
The position defended here is that the need for protection should not
depend on the ability to provide something in return. Vulnerability
does not, by itself, establish the entire architecture of rights, but it
constitutes a serious reason for care. If a being can be affected, what
we do to it must be justified. Its lack of accountability is not our lack of
responsibility toward it.
This does not mean that every dangerous behavior must be allowed to
continue. Protecting others and protecting the vulnerable person may
require intervention. But the justification must be connected with ne‐
cessity, proportionality, and competent assessment, not a wish to pun‐
ish guilt the person could not have incurred. A diagnosis establishes
neither the absence of responsibility nor dangerousness. A particular
attribution requires a particular assessment.
Finally, refusing to think is not the same as being unable to. Someone
who deliberately avoids relevant information to preserve an advant‐
age may be accountable precisely for that avoidance. Nevertheless,
the possibility of knowing and the avoidability of ignorance must be
established, not inferred from our dislike. “You should have known” is
a serious accusation. It must be accompanied by an explanation of
what the person could have learned, how, and at what cost. [3]
The test of a society is not only how severely it judges those who can
be held accountable. It is also how it protects those unable to con‐
struct a convincing defense. Listening only to eloquent people would
be a very efficient way to confuse voice with worth.

4. The Homeless Man and the
Night-Shift Worker
How Free Is a Choice Without Good Alternatives?
Someone is late for an appointment and loses an important opportun‐
ity. We can say they should have organized themselves. Perhaps so.
But before issuing a verdict, we should find out whether they had
somewhere to sleep, could charge their phone, knew the route, and
had to choose between the appointment and an urgent need. This in‐
formation does not automatically turn lateness into a virtue. But it
changes what “should have” means.
When discussing the responsibility of a homeless person, two simpli‐
fications are common. The first reduces the person to a succession of
bad choices. The second reduces them to a victim without any initiat‐
ive. The first hides the constraints. The second hides the person. Both
spare us the effort of understanding a particular life.
The terms are not interchangeable either. Asking for money on the
street and lacking a stable home do not necessarily describe the same
situation. Neither condition alone provides information about hon‐
esty, intelligence, or intentions. In this chapter’s scenarios, we will dis‐
cuss people whose circumstances must be understood, not a human
type to whom we could assign an entire psychology at a glance.
Research by Anandi Mani and colleagues, published in 2013, investig‐
ated the relationship between material deprivation and performance
on cognitive tasks. The studies included prompting thoughts about fin‐
ancial problems and comparing the same farmers before and after
harvest. The authors interpreted the findings as indications that finan‐
cial pressure can consume cognitive resources. They did not establish
any inherent intellectual inferiority among poor people. The designs
do not permit generalization to every person and situation; seasonal
comparisons are not equivalent to randomly assigning poverty. The
article also received a methodological criticism published in the same
journal, followed by a response from the authors. [19, 20, 21]
24


The moral relevance is more modest and more useful than the slogan
“poverty makes you incapable.” An apparently simple requirement
can demand resources an observer considers free only because they
possess them. Keeping a document requires a safe place. Answering a
call requires a working phone. Thinking long-term requires that to‐
morrow not be entirely consumed by the problem of survival. These
are conditions for certain actions, not universal excuses for every
deed.
Let us compare declining an appointment for convenience with miss‐
ing it after a night without shelter. The administrative outcome may
be identical: the person did not show up. But a system that reacts
identically may confuse uniformity with fairness. Not every difference
in treatment is favoritism. Sometimes it is the necessary adjustment
that makes it possible to follow the rule in reality.
There is an opposite danger. If we assume that someone in difficulty
cannot decide anything, we take away precisely the opportunity to re‐
build their life. Consulting them does not mean attributing control
over the whole context to them. It means not using that context to re‐
move them from their own story. Support can expand the alternatives
and leave the choice in their hands where that is possible.
Responsibility for one’s own life makes sense as participation in what
can still be changed. It becomes cruel when used as a complete ex‐
planation of what happened. Someone can be encouraged to keep
their documents, honor an agreement, and avoid hurting others.
These requirements do not imply that homelessness is deserved pun‐
ishment. Nor does the success of someone who overcame a difficult
situation establish that everyone who failed is to blame.
A good test would be to ask what concrete alternatives the person had.
Not a heroic alternative we can imagine after the event, but one that
was accessible, intelligible, and reasonable at the time. What informa‐
tion did they have? What did they risk? Who depended on them? This
test does not guarantee leniency. Sometimes it will show that a better
choice existed. But at least it will judge a real choice, not an observer’s
idealized freedom whose cost the observer never paid.
25


Must Help Be Earned Through Innocence?
Suppose now that the man on the step really did make serious mis‐
takes. He refused help, lied, and hurt people close to him. Is prevent‐
ing him from freezing less urgent? We can regard certain acts as con‐
demnable while also refusing to turn homelessness into a punishment
without an end date.
Emergency help and assessment of the past serve different purposes.
If we mix them, every encounter with a vulnerable person becomes a
summary trial. The passerby would have to decide, within seconds,
whether an unknown biography is clean enough. Such a criterion is
not merely harsh. It is impossible to apply fairly and favors well-told
stories over actual needs.
A rule for providing help can nevertheless have conditions. Other re‐
cipients and staff must be protected. Violence or exploitation do not
become acceptable because their perpetrator suffers. The question is
whether a condition actually protects something important or merely
asks the person to prove they deserve to be treated as human. A safety
limit, a rule for living together, and a test of moral purity are not the
same thing.
A randomized study published by Sam Tsemberis, Leyla Gulcur, and
Maria Nakae in 2004 compared a Housing First program with an ap‐
proach conditional on stages of treatment. The program provided
housing and support without first requiring abstinence or adherence
to treatment. The research, with 225 participants initially assigned,
found advantages in housing stability and perceived choice. It detec‐
ted no between-group differences in substance use or psychiatric
symptoms. Interpretation requires caution: exclusions occurred after
randomization, and the population and context were specific. The
finding supports a bounded intervention, not the idea that housing
automatically solves every difficulty. [22]
The experiment nevertheless opens a moral question that the data
alone cannot decide: why do we sometimes treat the conditions neces‐
sary for stability as rewards to be received only after stability has
been achieved? If housing helps someone keep appointments and or‐
ganize their life, refusing it until they become organized can create a
26


circle. This argument alone does not establish the best policy for every
city. It shows what should be compared and tested in place of an as‐
sumption about character.
A critic may reply that unconditional help risks encouraging harmful
behavior or distributing resources unfairly. The objection deserves ex‐
amination. We should ask which behaviors, with what measurable ef‐
fect, and compared with what alternative. Conditions have costs too:
administration, excluding eligible people, interrupting support. Identi‐
fying a risk of helping is not enough; it must be compared with the
risks of refusing help.
There is also the problem of the person who works hard and can
barely pay for housing. They may perceive support given to others as
unjust. A serious response is not to despise their dissatisfaction. It is to
ask why their security is so fragile and whether the system leaves
them too close to the same precipice. Solidarity becomes difficult
when people are offered only a choice between their own insecurity
and their neighbor’s.
This book’s position is not that every form of help is good. It is that a
person’s worth should not depend on success in a contest of inno‐
cence. We can require behavior compatible with others’ safety, evalu‐
ate programs, and correct abuses without turning a basic need into a
moral privilege.
And the homeless person may, in turn, have responsibilities toward
someone more vulnerable at that moment. They may notice someone
who has fallen, warn a child, or refrain from exploiting another’s
trust. Responsibility does not descend only from respectable people
toward those being helped. It arises wherever there is a relevant abil‐
ity to respond. Recognizing that ability is also a form of respect.
The Worker Who Sees the Risk but Cannot Stop the
Factory
At the end of a shift, a worker notices an irregularity. He knows that
reporting it may stop production. He also knows his boss rewards
people who “do not create problems.” He is unsure how serious the
risk is. He has loan payments, dependents, and few employment al‐
27


ternatives. Can we ask him to speak up? Usually, we have reasons to
require at least a reasonable step. But what counts as reasonable also
depends on the protection the organization offers him.
Professional responsibility has a component connected with compet‐
ence. Whoever performs a task must pay attention to its relevant
risks. But it also has an institutional component: the person must be
able to report, investigate, and escalate a problem. An organization
cannot reserve decision-making power for management and transfer
all blame to employees for what they lacked the power to stop.
Let us distinguish three situations. In the first, the worker knows of a
clear danger, has a functioning procedure, and can report it without
disproportionate cost, but hides the problem for a bonus. In the
second, he reports it repeatedly and management ignores the warn‐
ings. In the third, the information is incomplete and the procedure
ambiguous. All three can lead to the same accident. They do not pro‐
duce the same distribution of responsibility.
“I was following orders” is not a universal excuse. But “you could
have refused” is not a complete argument either. We must establish
what refusal entailed, how serious the harm was, how clear it was,
and what other avenues existed. The more serious the foreseeable
harm and the more direct the participation, the stronger the moral
pressure to refuse. Nevertheless, a society that systematically relies on
heroism from those with the least power and then abandons them has
not solved its problem of responsibility.
A reporting channel makes sense only if it changes something. A form
can record a report and bury it. A procedure can protect the institu‐
tion from scandal without protecting people from risk. Morally, what
matters is whether the message reaches someone able to decide,
whether it is investigated, whether the whistleblower is treated fairly,
and whether the outcome can be tracked. We are not discussing par‐
ticular legal rights here, which depend on jurisdiction, but the func‐
tion of an institution that claims to listen.
There is also an opposite limit: not every employee can be given an
unlimited veto over the entire organization merely because they in‐
voke a risk. Fears may be mistaken, information incomplete, conflicts
real. Responsibility therefore requires procedures for investigation
28


and decision, not replacing managerial authority with the authority of
any suspicion. An alarm must be taken seriously without automatic‐
ally being declared true.
Management’s responsibility is greater not because managers are a
different moral species, but because they have access to information,
can distribute resources, and can change rules. If they impose dead‐
lines incompatible with inspections and then punish the person doing
the work for a foreseeable error, they have not discovered an indi‐
vidual defect. They helped construct its conditions. Evidence would be
needed to support this accusation in a real case; the scenario shows
only the logic of attribution.
Decently performed work also has a positive dimension, easily over‐
looked in a book about blame. Maintaining a safe service, explaining a
limitation to a customer, properly handing over a shift, and leaving us‐
able documentation are forms of care toward strangers. They do not
always require heroism. They require continuity, skill, and conditions
in which these are possible.
The night-shift worker and the homeless man do not represent two
species, one responsible and the other dependent. They represent dif‐
ferent positions in the distribution of security, options, and power. A
serious concept of responsibility must see those differences without
turning either person into a mere illustration of a social theory.

5. The Crowd, the Citizen, and
the Invisible Hands
Why Does Nobody Help—and Why Might the
Question Be Wrong?
Someone collapses in a railway station. Twenty people are nearby.
Each sees that the others see. Each might assume someone else has
already called for help. A photograph of the scene could become evid‐
ence of modern indifference. But the photograph does not tell us who
intervened a moment earlier, who is looking for a phone, who has not
understood what is happening, or whether the situation is actually an
emergency. Moral judgment needs more than a convincing frame.
In John Darley and Bibb Latané’s classic experiment, published in
1968, participants heard someone apparently having a seizure over a
communication system. Intervention depended on how many other
witnesses they believed were present. The study helped formulate the
idea of diffusion of responsibility: the perceived presence of others
can weaken the feeling that you, specifically, must act. Yet this was a
precise experimental situation, not a definitive X-ray of human char‐
acter. [23]
A meta-analysis published by Peter Fischer and colleagues in 2011, in‐
volving more than 7,700 participants and 105 independent effect es‐
timates, found an overall bystander effect: the likelihood of individual
intervention could decline in the presence of others. But the results
depended on context. In situations perceived as dangerous, the effect
was attenuated; others could also represent support, not merely an
excuse for passivity. [24]
A very different investigation requires further caution. Richard Phil‐
pot and colleagues analyzed 219 recordings of public conflicts in three
cities. At least one bystander intervened in approximately 91% of in‐
cidents. More bystanders were associated with a higher probability
30


that someone would help. The study concerned visible conflicts in
monitored spaces, not every imaginable emergency, and did not estab‐
lish that every intervention resolved the situation. [25]
The two kinds of finding do not automatically contradict each other.
The probability that a particular person will help is not the same as
the probability that at least one of those present will help. Ten people,
each less willing, may together offer more chances of intervention
than a single person. The exact relationships depend on their interac‐
tions; the logical distinction remains valid.
This difference also changes the moral discussion. We cannot infer
from the existence of diffusion of responsibility that crowds are inev‐
itably cruel. Nor does the frequency of helping in certain conflicts
mean we can comfortably rely on someone else. Describing a mechan‐
ism does not grant an individual an exemption. It shows where co‐
ordination can fail and where it is worth building.
Let us return to the station. One bystander explicitly asks whether
help has been called. Another answers. A third looks for station staff.
The situation is resolved not by a sudden increase in everyone’s virtue
but by turning a diffuse obligation into identifiable actions. This is a
hypothetical example, not a universal recipe for emergencies. The
general idea is that responsibility sometimes needs a clear sentence
before it needs a moral speech.
The same problem arises in public administration. A report reaches
five departments. Each registers it; none is tasked with checking the fi‐
nal outcome. All can demonstrate that they followed their procedure,
while the affected person remains without an answer. The failure
need not be explained exclusively by officials’ malice. It may be pro‐
duced by an organizational structure that distributes activities but not
the obligation to follow their consequences.
Hence a simple institutional requirement: for foreseeable problems,
there must be a connection between a report, a decision, and verifica‐
tion. Someone must be able to say not only “I passed it on” but also “I
checked what happened.” That person must be given time and author‐
ity. Naming someone responsible without providing the necessary
means is an elegant way to prepare a scapegoat.
31


Yet clarity of roles must not produce indifference outside them. “It is
not my department” can be useful information or an abdication. The
difference depends on what follows: do I point to a functioning route
or close the conversation? I do not owe a solution to every problem I
encounter. I may owe its proper transfer when doing so is simple and
abandoning it would cause foreseeable harm.
The Order, the Uniform, and the Promise That
Someone Else Knows
Another scene: a participant believes that, at a researcher’s request,
he is administering electric shocks to someone. Stanley Milgram’s
1963 experiment became one of the best-known images of obedience.
In the classic condition, 26 of the 40 male participants proceeded to
the maximum indicated level. The shocks were not real, but parti‐
cipants had not been told this. The figure of 65% describes that condi‐
tion, not a universal proportion of people willing to torture. [26, 27]
The interpretation of the experiments remains debated. Stephen
Reicher and Alexander Haslam have argued that identification with
the scientific purpose and the experimenter’s authority explains some
findings better than the image of completely blind obedience. Experi‐
mental conditions produced important variations. This reinterpreta‐
tion does not establish that people are immune to authority; it chal‐
lenges an overly simple explanation of how authority works. [27]
For responsibility, the difference is crucial. Someone may participate
in harm not only by abandoning every moral purpose but also by ad‐
opting a supposedly higher one: progress, saving the organization, the
group’s security. “We are doing this for good” can close down scrutiny
of the means at precisely the moment it should intensify it.
Suppose a team accepts a minor violation to save an important pro‐
ject. Then a second arises, justified by the investment already made.
By the end, members see themselves not as people who chose wrong,
but as people who defended something valuable against obstacles.
The scenario does not establish how frequently this happens. But it
shows why sincerity of conviction is insufficient to justify action.
32


Responsible authority must allow itself to be challenged without turn‐
ing every objection into betrayal. At the same time, a team cannot
function if every decision remains suspended until every member is
completely convinced. The solution is not the absence of authority but
differentiating decisions: some require rapid execution, others inde‐
pendent scrutiny, and some must be stopped when serious signs of
harm appear. These thresholds must be considered before the crisis,
not improvised afterward to protect management.
Groups can also support responsibility. Robert Sampson and col‐
leagues’ research on “collective efficacy” linked trust among neigh‐
bors and willingness to intervene for the common good with lower
levels of neighborhood violence. The Chicago study was observation‐
al: associations, even after statistical adjustments, are not equivalent
to experimental evidence that simply increasing trust automatically
produces safety. In his later analysis, Sampson also emphasizes re‐
sources and the wider context. [28, 29]
The moral conclusion is not that residents of a poor neighborhood are
to blame for failing to produce enough solidarity. That would conveni‐
ently invert the problem. Cooperation requires material conditions,
time, stability, and institutions that deserve a measure of trust. Asking
neighbors to compensate alone for the absence of public services
means privatizing a collective obligation.
Nor is solidarity good by definition. A tightly united group can protect
an aggressor, exclude a newcomer, or conceal abuse to preserve its
reputation. We must ask not only how much people cooperate, but for
what purpose and against whom. Mature collective responsibility re‐
quires bonds strong enough for action and rights solid enough to limit
group pressure.
A good community is not one in which nobody can say “no.” It is one
in which a reasoned refusal can become useful information rather
than evidence of impurity. Sometimes the person who temporarily
weakens a group’s apparent unity protects precisely the conditions for
cooperation worth preserving.

Citizenship Is Not a Certificate of Innocence
A citizen says: “I did not decide. I did not sign. I did not benefit.” Some‐
times all three claims are true. The question is whether it follows that
there is nothing left for that citizen to do. It does not automatically fol‐
low, because responsibility for the future is not identical to blame for
the past.
Philosophical discussions of collective responsibility distinguish at‐
tributing actions to a group from distributing obligations among its
members. They also examine whether organizations, not merely indi‐
viduals, can be responsible agents. There is no unanimously accepted
solution. One important point, however, is separating the obligation to
remedy a common problem from the assumption that every member
personally committed the same injustice. [30]
Suppose a city spent decades building services that exclude people
with reduced mobility. A new resident did not design the steps, refuse
funding, and perhaps did not even know about the problem. That per‐
son is not the author of that past. But as a participant in current insti‐
tutions, they may have reasons to support transforming them. The ob‐
ligation need not be grounded in inherited guilt; it can be grounded in
the present need to build an accessible order.
Likewise, benefiting from an advantage created through injustice does
not automatically make you morally identical to its perpetrator. But it
may matter to how the costs of repair should be shared. This is a thes‐
is requiring arguments and evidence in each situation: what advant‐
age persists, who bears the disadvantage, what measure would actu‐
ally correct the problem, and whom would it burden? Historical labels
alone do not answer these questions.
Citizenship involves a further difficulty: nobody can understand every
public issue in depth. Someone working long hours, caring for anoth‐
er person, and struggling with difficulties of their own does not have
the same capacity to become informed as a well-funded institution.
Requiring universal expertise before recognizing their right to an
opinion would be a form of exclusion.
34


Yet lack of expertise does not justify every kind of conduct. There is a
difference between not knowing a case and confidently spreading an
accusation you have made no effort to verify. We can require propor‐
tionality between what someone knows, how categorically they speak,
and how much influence they wield. The same rumor shared in a
private conversation and promoted by an organization with a large
audience does not create the same situation of responsibility.
A society cannot depend solely on impeccable citizens. It needs rules
limiting the harm caused by foreseeable mistakes, intelligible public
information, and opportunities for correction. Institutions should not
be designed for a fictional citizen who is infinitely attentive, perfectly
rational, and always rested. Nor should they be designed for a citizen
treated as permanently incapable of judgment.
There is also responsibility for not accepting every obligation claimed
in the community’s name. A state may demand unjust sacrifices, a ma‐
jority may demand a minority’s silence, and an organization may con‐
fuse loyalty with obedience. Answering to others does not mean sur‐
rendering the right to ask who makes the demand and who benefits
from it.
Responsible citizenship is therefore neither complete withdrawal into
private life nor permanent mobilization. It is limited but real particip‐
ation in maintaining our shared world, accompanied by the right to
refuse abusive demands. How extensive that participation should be
remains an open moral question. The fact that we cannot set a perfect
quota does not make every contribution optional.

6. What Do We Owe Those Who
Suffer?
The Child in the Water and the Stranger Beyond the
Screen
You are hurrying to a meeting. In a shallow pool, a child is in danger.
You can pull the child out without serious risk to yourself, but you will
ruin your shoes and arrive late. Almost all the force of this example
lies in the disproportion: something grave for another can be preven‐
ted at little cost to you. You did not push the child. You promised noth‐
ing. Yet your absence from the initial causal chain hardly seems
enough to justify walking away.
Peter Singer uses such a thought experiment in his 1972 essay “Fam‐
ine, Affluence, and Morality.” The central idea is that we have an ob‐
ligation to prevent a significant harm when we can do so without a
morally comparable sacrifice. He challenges the justificatory force of
distance and of the fact that others could also help. The example puts
pressure on the customary boundary between duty and optional char‐
ity. [31]
But the pool is a deliberately simplified case. We see the person, un‐
derstand the danger, and know reasonably well what effect our action
will have. When help passes through organizations, logistical chains,
and institutions, questions of effectiveness, cost, and consequences
arise. These differences do not automatically cancel the obligation.
They change what must be learned to fulfill it responsibly.
Let us replace geographical distance with technical distance. On a
monitor, you see someone in danger in another building. You can ac‐
tivate a reliable protective mechanism with a single button. If the im‐
age and mechanism are reliable, being unable to touch the person
hardly seems to weaken the requirement to intervene. Physical dis‐
tance alone is not a sufficient moral reason. Uncertainty and limits of
control may be relevant reasons.
36


There are several paths toward the obligation to help. One begins with
the significance of suffering: what is terrible for me does not become
unimportant merely because it happens to someone else. Another be‐
gins with the reciprocity of vulnerability: we have reasons to support
a world in which people are not abandoned in grave dangers that oth‐
ers can easily reduce. A third begins with existing ties and promises.
We need not necessarily choose one basis for every case.
A distinct path is reparation. If I contributed to harm, the demand to
help is no longer merely a vulnerable person’s appeal to a stranger. It
includes a connection to my action. A manufacturer who concealed a
defect cannot present fixing it as an act of generosity. That would turn
a duty toward those affected into an occasion for public admiration.
Likewise, a position can create a special obligation. Someone who
agreed to supervise a dangerous space is not in the same position as a
passerby. The contract does not produce the situation’s entire moral‐
ity, but it changes others’ justified expectations. A promise and a role
make some needs concern us more directly.
A weaker argument says we must help because we too might someday
need help. It is useful as an exercise of imagination but insufficient as
an exclusive foundation. What, then, do we owe someone who will
never be able to return our help? Or a being unable to participate in
an exchange? If the reason disappears entirely when reciprocity be‐
comes impossible, we have described mutual insurance, not the whole
moral obligation.
Science cannot cross this threshold on its own either. It can study pain,
the effects of poverty, or the consequences of a support program. To
conclude that we have a duty, a normative premise is also required:
that certain interests of others matter to what we are permitted to do.
Such a premise can be defended, criticized, and compared with al‐
ternatives. But it does not appear as a laboratory finding between two
columns of measurements.
That is why the question “why should I care?” has two meanings. It
may ask for reasons for an action in a particular situation. Or it may
reject from the outset any reason that does not serve self-interest. In
the second case, no fact about the intensity of suffering will produce
37


an obligation on its own if the interlocutor refuses to recognize any
value beyond personal advantage. The dispute becomes one about
what kinds of justification we accept between persons.
Arguments Against Unlimited Duty
The strongest objection is not “others do not matter” but “my life mat‐
ters too.” If every resource must be directed toward the greatest exist‐
ing need, what remains of the freedom to have a family, investigate
something without immediate utility, compose music, or simply live
without providing a public justification for every hour?
A defender of a strict separation between negative and positive oblig‐
ations may say: I must not hurt you, but it does not follow that I owe
you my labor to repair every harm I did not cause. Taking autonomy
seriously would also mean limiting claims on other people’s time and
resources. This is an argument about the boundaries of moral author‐
ity, not necessarily a denial of compassion.
One possible reply observes that even the prohibition on harming in‐
volves costs and coordination. Protecting rights does not come from
nothing. Nevertheless, this reply does not establish that every need
creates an unlimited claim on anyone. The autonomy objection re‐
mains relevant even if we reject an absolute separation between “not
doing harm” and “doing good.”
There are also special obligations. A parent has reasons to give their
child care they cannot give identically to every child. Promises, friend‐
ships, and relationships of dependence raise questions about legitim‐
ate partiality: when may we favor someone, and when does the rela‐
tionship become a pretext for injustice? The philosophy of special ob‐
ligations offers no single accepted explanation of their origins and
limits. [32]
Eliminating this partiality entirely could destroy the very forms of
care through which people come to have a secure life. Making it abso‐
lute would allow any advantage for one’s own family to justify any
harm to strangers. Between these extremes, we must construct a posi‐
tion in which relationships matter but do not make the rest of the
world morally invisible.
38


The objection of excessive demandingness is also one of accumula‐
tion. A small sacrifice may seem obligatory. But what happens when a
thousand demands arise, each presented as a small sacrifice? If we as‐
sess each request separately, we risk approving a total burden we
would not accept when viewed as a whole. Responsibility must also be
assessed over time, not only episode by episode.
This observation matters for caregivers. Someone may be the only
family member who always responds, and the others may turn that
availability into an implicit, endless obligation. The fact that the per‐
son can make one more effort does not establish that the distribution
is just. When each emergency justifies maintaining the same inequal‐
ity, emergency becomes a way of organizing exploitation.
Then there is uncertainty about effects. Help can miss its target, create
unwanted dependencies, or be diverted. These are possibilities to in‐
vestigate, not ready-made arguments against every form of support.
Conversely, good intention is not evidence of effectiveness. In a partic‐
ular case, the intervention, alternatives, and costs of nonintervention
must be compared. Demanding evidence only of help while treating
passivity as free of consequences would be a double standard.
Another objection concerns a person’s right to refuse. Perhaps what I
call help involves controlling their life. Perhaps I want to remove a
risky choice they understand and accept. The anticipated benefit does
not automatically cancel autonomy. On the other hand, a refusal ex‐
pressed without accessible information or under coercion raises dif‐
ferent problems. Again, the situation requires discernment, not a
single formula.
There are also cases with no obligation to intervene personally: when
intervention would create a disproportionate risk, when we lack the
necessary expertise and may make things worse, when an urgent ob‐
ligation to someone else takes priority, or when the demand itself is
abusive. These limits are not embarrassing gaps in a perfect morality.
They are conditions for addressing obligations to finite persons.
39


A Real Obligation Without Confiscating Life
The position defended here is that obligations to help exist, but they
are not distributed solely according to the intensity of need. Actual ca‐
pacity, contribution to harm, an assumed role, alternatives, costs, and
the power to change the situation also matter. This is a moral propos‐
al, not a conclusion demonstrated by an experiment.
When harm is grave, help is reliable, and the cost to me is low, the re‐
quirement to act is strong. When I have produced or sustained the
harm, the obligation to repair it may require greater sacrifices. When
I have accepted a protective role, I cannot invoke a simple freedom to
concern myself only with my own projects. When the problem ex‐
ceeds individual capacity, the institutional distribution of the burden
must be discussed.
The fact that others also have obligations does not automatically can‐
cel mine. But their ignoring those obligations does not automatically
transfer everything else to me. This second sentence is essential.
Without it, morality would reward the less scrupulous with additional
freedom and punish the more attentive with unlimited work.
A decent society must turn some indispensable help into predictable
services rather than leave it exclusively to the mercy of fortunate en‐
counters. This does not by itself determine the state’s precise form,
taxation levels, or the combination of public institutions, organiza‐
tions, and informal support. It establishes a requirement of justifica‐
tion: lacking the good fortune to encounter a benefactor should not
alone determine access to fundamental protections.
Responsible help must be open to examination from the recipient’s
perspective. What problem do they consider urgent? What do they
lose by accepting the offer? Can they challenge the conditions? Are
they treated as a person or as material for the donor’s reputation? An
intervention effective by a single metric may remain humiliating or
overlook important costs to the recipient.
Measurement should not be rejected either. Without comparisons, we
may continue a useless practice for years simply because it gives us
the feeling of being good. The problem arises when the indicator is
40


confused with the complete goal. The number of people contacted is
not identical to the number of lives improved; that number, in turn,
does not automatically capture every dimension of a dignified life.
The circle of obligation can also be questioned beyond currently living
humans. If a being can suffer, its inability to formulate arguments is
not, by itself, a reason to ignore its suffering. This is a conditional ar‐
gument about the moral relevance of sentience, not a solution to
every controversy about animals. Likewise, future people’s inability to
hold us accountable now does not render the foreseeable effects we
leave behind morally indifferent.
There is, however, a limit to the claim of speaking for the future. We
can invent hypothetical beneficiaries to justify certain injustices in the
present. The more speculative the forecast, the more cautious we
must be when demanding sacrifices from others in its name. Protect‐
ing the future must not become a blank check for current power.
Having a responsibility toward those who suffer does not mean con‐
stantly feeling the entirety of their suffering. Emotion can open our at‐
tention, but a just distribution of tasks cannot depend only on who is
more easily moved. We can build habits, contributions, and institu‐
tions that continue to function on days when our compassion is not in‐
tense.
Instead of asking whether we can be happy in a suffering world, we
can ask more precisely: what help falls to us, what tasks must be
shared, and what excuses prevent us from seeing the difference? Gen‐
eral guilt is no substitute for the answer.

7. Those Who Know:
Researchers, Philosophers, and
World Builders
The Researcher Did Not Ask to Run the World
A researcher discovers a powerful method of analysis. It can help
identify a disease, but also classify people without their consent. She
says: “I produce knowledge. Society decides what to do with it.” The
statement contains an important truth: the researcher must not be
transformed into the supreme political authority. But it also contains a
possible evasion: sometimes the way she produces and publishes the
result directly influences who can use it and with what effects.
Responsibility in research begins before the final application. Choos‐
ing the question, selecting data, treating participants, describing limit‐
ations, and deciding to conceal or communicate an error are already
actions with consequences. The researcher cannot control every sub‐
sequent use but can control some of the conditions through which
others come to believe and act.
The 1979 Belmont Report sets out the principles of respect for persons,
beneficence, and justice for research involving human participants.
Questions about consent, risks, benefits, and participant selection can‐
not be replaced by simply invoking scientific progress. The document
provides a historical ethical reference point, not a universal legal solu‐
tion for every contemporary project. [18]
Suppose a study would produce useful knowledge but use vulnerable
people because they can more easily be persuaded to accept risks. The
possibility that the result might help many people does not eliminate
the question of those used to obtain it. Refusing every risk would not
be a solution either: research and life involve uncertainty. The ques‐
tion is who decides, with what information, and who bears the costs
in relation to the benefits.
42


There is also the elementary epistemic responsibility not to present
mere hope as a finding. An interesting hypothesis may deserve invest‐
igation without being called a discovery. A preliminary experiment
may produce a signal without demonstrating the mechanism’s gener‐
ality. An honestly reported limitation is not an editorial defect to be
hidden to secure funding; it is part of what others need to know to
judge the result.
The researcher also has the right not to promise immediate social ef‐
fects for every idea. Curiosity, conceptual exploration, and studying
apparently remote problems can be legitimate activities. Requiring
each project to demonstrate its final utility from the outset would con‐
fuse responsibility with obedience to the priorities of the moment. Re‐
search freedom and its social obligations must be discussed together,
not invoked alternately to cancel one side.
One institutional example is the Ethics and Society Review process,
described by Michael Bernstein and colleagues in a 2021 preprint. Ar‐
tificial intelligence projects underwent a review of their social implic‐
ations before funding. The authors analyzed the first year, involving
41 proposals. Reports of changes to projects are informative, but they
do not establish through a controlled experiment that the mechanism
reduced long-term social harms. [33]
Such an example also has value in the question it makes practical:
what research decision changes after the ethical discussion? If noth‐
ing can change, the review risks becoming ceremonial. If it can block
any project merely by invoking vague fears, it risks becoming a form
of censorship without criteria. A good mechanism must allow argu‐
ments, revisions, proportionality, and examination of its own inter‐
vention’s costs.
Responsibility cannot be demanded only of the individual researcher.
A funder who rewards exaggerated promises, a university that toler‐
ates unverifiable results for prestige, and a company that imposes re‐
lease before evaluation all help shape choices. In a real case, such
statements would require evidence. As a moral structure, they show
why demanding integrity without examining incentives is insufficient.
43


Incentives are not a complete excuse either. A system’s favoring exag‐
geration explains the pressure but does not automatically make exag‐
geration justified. We simultaneously need people who resist mis‐
guided demands and institutions that do not turn that resistance into
a continuous test of professional heroism.
Does the Ability to Think Bring an Additional Duty?
Imagine a philosopher who can formulate ten elegant justifications
for a practice but never asks those who bear its effects. His intelli‐
gence is real. His responsibility may be deficient. The ability to con‐
struct arguments does not guarantee that you have chosen an honest
question or are willing to abandon a convenient answer.
Being able to philosophize may mean education, time, security, and
access to conversations in which ideas are taken seriously. Not every‐
one with this opportunity acquired it exclusively through their own
merit. But it does not follow that their entire life belongs to society. It
provides a reason to examine how they use an advantage that can in‐
fluence how others understand their condition.
There is a specific duty not to manufacture respectability for what we
cannot defend directly. If an argument serves to present people as dis‐
pensable, we must also test it from the perspective of those declared
dispensable. If a theory justifies a hierarchy, we must ask whether the
same justification would remain acceptable if its author did not know
their own place within it. These perspective tests do not decide truth
on their own, but they can expose privileges hidden in the formula‐
tion.
The philosopher has no obligation to become a universal commentat‐
or. Competence in analyzing a concept does not automatically create
competence in economics, medicine, or foreign policy. Intellectual re‐
sponsibility may require precisely a refusal to answer authoritatively
where our knowledge is insufficient. Sometimes the most useful thing
an expert can say is where their expertise ends.
On the other hand, continually invoking complexity can become a
refuge. If an institution demonstrably conceals harm, it is not enough
to observe that the meaning of harm is philosophically disputed. Dis‐
44


tinctions are useful when they make judgment more precise. They be‐
come irresponsible when used to postpone every practical con‐
sequence indefinitely, regardless of how clear the evidence is.
Philosophizing responsibly also means exposing the cost of one’s own
theory. If I defend an almost absolute freedom to retain resources, I
must say what happens to people whom voluntary help misses. If I de‐
fend a very extensive distribution of obligations, I must say which
personal freedoms it limits. A theory presenting only its admirable in‐
tentions and leaving the costs to its opponents is not yet a complete
justification.
But what about the person unable to engage in this abstract discus‐
sion? They need not demonstrate a theory of justice to have the right
to just treatment. Intelligence and education can change the speaker’s
epistemic obligations, not the fundamental worth of the person being
discussed. Someone may be unable to explain in sophisticated terms
why they are being humiliated and still be humiliated.
Simple speech does not guarantee truth either. Testimony should be
heard, not declared infallible merely because it comes from a vulner‐
able person. Respect means including it in the examination, not turn‐
ing the person into a symbol others use without checking the facts.
Taking someone seriously includes the possibility of dialogue and dis‐
agreement.
Finally, there is responsibility for making ideas usable. A text can be
precise yet useless to its intended audience. Clarity does not require
eliminating difficulties but presenting them without unnecessary
obstacles. Anyone who writes about responsibility and hides uncer‐
tainties in solemn language risks reproducing precisely the kind of au‐
thority they claim to examine.
The Machine Decided. Who Allowed It?
An institution uses an automated system to prioritize requests.
Someone is rejected. The official says the score was calculated by the
program. The supplier says the institution chose the objective. The in‐
stitution says experts recommended the method. Each statement may
be partly true, and between them the obligation to offer the person an
explanation and a route to correction may disappear.
45


The philosophy of responsibility in computing discusses this “many
hands” problem: outcomes arise from contributions by multiple
people, organizations, and artifacts. A system’s technical autonomy
must not be confused with moral autonomy. Complexity can make at‐
tributing harm difficult without that difficulty establishing the ab‐
sence of any human obligation to design, oversee, or remedy. [34]
In our scenario, the solution is not to choose a single culprit immedi‐
ately. First, we must know what kind of rejection occurred, what data
were used, whether the score suited that purpose, which uncertainties
were known, and who could change the process. Otherwise, “the al‐
gorithm is biased” can be as vague an explanation as “the user made a
mistake.”
Formally retaining a human in the loop does not solve the problem if
that person has a few seconds, no additional information, and no
power to challenge the recommendation. Their signature can become
a piece of moral scenery. Genuine oversight requires the ability to no‐
tice relevant errors, stop or change a decision, and receive support
when doing so is justified.
Responsibility must be designed before the incident. Who authorizes
using the system? Who checks whether the data are appropriate?
Who monitors the effects on people? Who can withdraw it from use?
Who responds to an appeal? These questions are not a complete tech‐
nical specification. They are conditions for ensuring that the division
of labor does not become a division of evasion.
There are also situations in which nobody could foresee a particular
error. Retrospective accusation may then be unjustified, but the oblig‐
ation to correct the system remains. Discovering a new risk changes
what must be done next. Yesterday’s lack of knowledge does not justi‐
fy tomorrow’s passivity.
How long are creators accountable for uses they did not intend? Not
indefinitely and not for every imagined connection. What matters is
how foreseeable the use was, how much they facilitated it, what con‐
trol they retain, and what proportionate measures were available.
Neither “just a tool” nor “the author is responsible for everything” re‐
solves these differences.
46


Dual-use knowledge poses a similar problem. Publication can enable
verification, innovation, and the sharing of benefits. It can also enable
abuse. Restriction can reduce a risk but concentrate power and block
legitimate research. A responsible choice must compare concrete op‐
tions, including graduated access and independent scrutiny, rather
than retreat into a slogan of absolute openness or absolute secrecy.
Could a future artificial intelligence itself become morally respons‐
ible? The question depends on capacities such as understanding reas‐
ons, controlling conduct, and being able to answer in a deeper sense
than generating an apology. We do not establish here whether or
when such conditions will be met. If we built an entity with interests
of its own and the capacity to suffer, the question of our responsibility
toward it would also arise, not just its responsibility toward us.
Until such hypotheses are clarified, attributing moral words to a ma‐
chine must not function as an automatic transfer of accountability
away from institutions. An “I’m sorry” message does not repair an un‐
just decision. Repair requires access to facts, authority to make
changes, and acceptance of costs. These are things organizations must
construct, not emotions interfaces must merely imitate.

8. Can Gods Be Held
Accountable?
The Creator and Ownership of Creation
Someone sees a child in danger, can intervene at no cost, and does not.
We demand an explanation. Now replace that person with a being we
say knows everything, can do everything, and is perfectly good. Why
should the question disappear? Perhaps we have reasons to change
the standard, but that change must be argued for. Otherwise, we
would construct a concept of power in which obligations grow up to a
point and then disappear precisely when power reaches its maxim‐
um.
Before any accusation, we must clarify which deity we mean. A very
powerful but limited god does not raise exactly the same problem as
an omnipotent creator. An intelligence that initiated the universe
without overseeing it is not identical to a providential parent. An im‐
personal reality called God is not automatically an agent who deliber‐
ates and decides. We cannot attribute all these properties to the same
concept merely because we use the same word.
Let us first imagine limited gods, with intentions and interests. If they
can understand suffering, choose, and have the power to intervene,
they seem to enter the space of moral questions. Being more powerful
than humans does not, by itself, grant an exemption. A king does not
become just because nobody can defeat him. Nor does a god become
good merely because contradicting him is dangerous.
Perhaps our obligation toward such gods would be one of prudence:
not to provoke them. But prudence and legitimacy are different
things. Submitting to avoid punishment does not establish that the or‐
der deserves obedience. This distinction also helps us understand hu‐
man authorities that demand fear be called respect.
In the Euthyphro, Plato examines the relationship between what is pi‐
ous and the gods’ approval. Adapted to morality, the question be‐
comes: is something good because God commands it, or does God com‐
48


mand it because it is good? The first direction risks making goodness
dependent on will; the second seems to recognize a standard not redu‐
cible to command. The modern adaptation is broader than the dia‐
logue’s exact problem. [35]
One possible theistic reply says the alternative is incomplete: good‐
ness would be neither arbitrary nor external to God, but connected
with his nature. This reply deserves examination in its strongest form.
It does not say that every divine whim would become good. It says a
perfect being has no such whims. But a new question arises: by what
means do we identify that nature as good, and how do we distinguish
an authentic command from a mistaken human attribution?
If the answer is that any act attributed to the deity must by definition
be called good, the concept risks losing the substance that made it im‐
portant. “God is good” no longer provides moral reassurance but
merely repeats a verbal identity. If goodness retains a meaning we can
understand at least partially, then questions about suffering cannot be
dismissed merely because they are unpleasant.
Another reply invokes the status of creator. God would have rights
over what he made. But creation does not obviously confer an unlim‐
ited right to dispose of a being with interests of its own. A parent con‐
tributes to a child’s existence without turning the child into property.
The analogy does not establish that the relationship between God and
the world is identical to parenthood. It shows that moving from “cre‐
ated it” to “may justly do anything” requires an additional argument.
A defender of theism may object that all analogies are inadequate:
God is not a more powerful inhabitant of the universe but the ground
of its existence. The objection is serious. Yet the further we remove the
deity from every intelligible category of agent, the harder it becomes
to attribute categories such as care, love, justice, or promise without
explanation. We cannot use analogy when it offers consolation and
automatically forbid it when it produces a critical question.
Calling a deity to account does not necessarily mean claiming that we
possess a cosmic tribunal. We can examine whether a concept’s prop‐
erties are compatible and what reasons we have to believe a particu‐
lar description of the world. Lacking the power to sanction an agent
49


does not make every judgment about them meaningless. Otherwise,
even a perfectly protected tyrant would become morally impeccable
merely through the absence of an effective judge.
Suffering as Evidence Against a Promise
The philosophical problem of evil takes several forms. One asks
whether the existence of evil is logically incompatible with an omni‐
potent, omniscient, perfectly good God. Another asks whether the
scale and distribution of suffering make such a hypothesis less plaus‐
ible. A defense may show that a contradiction has not been estab‐
lished without offering a convincing explanation for every instance of
actual suffering. [36]
The difference matters. Showing that a scenario is possible does not
mean showing that it describes the world. A lawyer may show that an
innocent explanation for a piece of evidence exists without having
proved that explanation. Conversely, not knowing a justification for
suffering is not identical to proving that no justification can exist. Seri‐
ous discussion must avoid both shortcuts.
Let us test the freedom hypothesis. A world in which people can
choose would also permit bad choices. If every harmful intention
were immediately stopped by divine intervention, some forms of
autonomy and real consequence would change. This is an intelligible
reason not to identify goodness with eliminating every negative out‐
come. But that reason alone does not establish how much evil free‐
dom requires.
An aggressor can retain his intention and choice even if the door he
wants to force holds firm. Saving a victim does not establish that the
aggressor’s freedom has been canceled in every relevant sense. The
free-will defense must therefore explain more than the existence of
choice: it must discuss why certain consequences are permitted and
why different limits on the power to harm could not exist. This is a
conceptual test, not proof that we know how to construct a complete
alternative universe.
Then there is suffering that does not appear to result from a moral
choice by either the victim or an aggressor. A child’s illness and the
pain of a being incapable of deliberation cannot simply be explained
50


through the affected person’s freedom. Broader connections may be
proposed, but each additional connection requires justification. Mov‐
ing the explanation somewhere we cannot examine is not enough.
Let us test the hypothesis of a world with stable laws. Responsible ac‐
tion requires some regularity: we must be able to anticipate effects. A
universe in which every accident is miraculously suspended would
differ from one in which we learn to protect one another. Yet it does
not immediately follow that this precise distribution of risks is neces‐
sary. Stable rules explain why consequences cannot be arbitrarily
eliminated without changes; they do not establish that every alternat‐
ive set of rules would be worse.
Let us then test the idea of moral development through difficulty.
Courage, patience, and solidarity require obstacles. But not every
form of suffering produces development, and the person bearing the
cost must not automatically be treated as educational material for
someone else. If an adult deliberately hurt a child to make a neighbor
wiser, we would demand an extremely strong justification. Invoking a
higher plan does not eliminate the question of using the victim as an
instrument.
The defender may reply that our judgment is too limited to assess the
whole. This argument from the limits of knowledge blocks some cat‐
egorical conclusions. But it has a cost: if divine goodness can look in‐
distinguishable from cruelty to us, what practical substance remains
in trusting it? And if our ignorance prevents us from condemning an
evil apparently permitted by God, why would it not also prevent us
from helping, since that evil might serve an unknown purpose?
A religious position can coherently reply that human duties remain
valid even when the meaning of the whole is unknown. This separates
the problem of providence from our conduct. Yet the separation must
be maintained consistently: cosmic uncertainty must not be invoked
to justify passivity before a need we understand sufficiently.
In Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion, David Hume stages a con‐
flict between explanations of the world’s order and the experience of
suffering. The text is a dialogue, not a set of statements that can be at‐
51


tributed to the author without qualification. One difficulty it explores
is the leap from the observed world to firm conclusions about its cre‐
ator’s unlimited goodness. [37]
Omnipotence must not be defined carelessly either. Thomas Aquinas
distinguishes divine power from the capacity to realize logical contra‐
dictions: an incoherent description does not become a possibility
merely because we ask it of an omnipotent being. This clarification ex‐
cludes some facile objections. But it does not, by itself, establish that
every imaginable reduction in suffering would be contradictory. [38]
The conclusion of this examination is not that God’s existence has
been established or disproved in a few pages. It is narrower: attribut‐
ing total knowledge, total power, and perfect goodness simultaneously
creates a real explanatory burden in the face of suffering. Answers
must be assessed by what they explain and what they ask us to accept
without explanation.
Job and the Refusal to Call the Victim Guilty
The Book of Job matters precisely because it offers more than the im‐
age of a world in which every instance of suffering proves a fault. The
friends try to bring his experience back into an intelligible moral or‐
der. The ending rebukes them for how they spoke about God. The reli‐
gious text thus opens a space for challenging convenient explanations
of merit, suffering, and authority. We use it not as empirical evidence
but as a work staging a moral problem. [10]
The problem remains recognizable. Someone suffers, and those
nearby feel a need to explain quickly why. Perhaps the person chose
badly. Perhaps they lacked the right attitude. Perhaps there is a lesson.
Some explanations may be true in particular cases. But offered
without evidence, they chiefly protect the speaker’s peace of mind: if
the world punishes only identifiable mistakes, the speaker can ima‐
gine themselves safe.
Refusing such an explanation does not mean claiming choices have no
consequences. It means refusing the move from consequence to a
total verdict on a person. Someone may be responsible for part of
their situation and still suffer more than that part could justify. They
may need help even if their biography is not innocent.
52


Job also raises the question of the right to protest. Faith can include
lament, questioning, and refusing false consolation. Not every moral
protest is a simple denial of the deity. It may be a demand that the
goodness invoked by faith not be emptied of meaning. Conversely, un‐
belief does not establish the absence of obligations toward others; it
changes how they are grounded.
Restoration at the end of a story does not logically cancel every loss
within it. If someone later receives other goods, it does not follow that
the lost people and experiences were interchangeable. This argument
also applies to promises of cosmic compensation. Compensation can
matter profoundly without retrospectively turning every evil into
something good.
We can imagine conceptions of the deity that relinquish one of the
properties discussed: limited power, limited intervention, or a rela‐
tionship with the world that does not resemble an agent’s governance.
These variants alter the problem of evil, but they also alter the reli‐
gious promise. A God unable to prevent a tragedy is not judged in the
same way as one who can prevent it at no cost and chooses not to.
If God exists and we have reasons to believe in a divine obligation,
what “holding him accountable” would mean remains difficult to say.
It may be a conceptual assessment, a lament, or a questioning reli‐
gious relationship, not an enforceable procedure. If God does not ex‐
ist, the discussion still serves a purpose: it forces us to see what con‐
nection we assume between power and obligation, without the usual
excuses of human powerlessness.
In shared life, people need not resolve this dispute before cooperating.
A believer may see helping as a duty toward God; a nonbeliever, as a
duty toward the vulnerable being. Ultimate reasons may differ while
justified action coincides. Cooperation requires no metaphysical iden‐
tity, only enough shared reasons not to abandon the person before us.
Perhaps the most uncomfortable question is not whether we can con‐
struct a tribunal for God. It is whether we demand that the deity justi‐
fy suffering while using that same suffering’s existence as an inevit‐
able backdrop for our own inaction. Criticism of a cosmic plan does
not repair the world by itself. Nor does belief in such a plan absolve us
of what we could do within it.

9. Taking Responsibility Without
Believing Yourself to Be God
How Much Can an Entire World Ask of One Person?
At the beginning of the book, we seemed to have a question about
blame. By the end, we have a problem of distribution: who must no‐
tice, explain, prevent, bear costs, and repair? Sometimes the same per‐
son must do all of these. At other times, the distribution must differ. A
society goes wrong when it seeks a single name for every task merely
because it is easier to display.
Let us imagine a map, not a score. A score would promise that we can
add power, intention, and need into a single number, then read off the
verdict. We have no such validated instrument here, nor reasons to
believe every relevant value is perfectly interchangeable. A map helps
us see a situation’s dimensions without pretending it can resolve them
mechanically.
The first dimension is capacity. What could the person understand
and control at that moment? We are not asking only what an ordinary
person can do under ideal conditions. Pressure, access to information,
available support, and the nature of the decision matter. Nor do we
turn every difficulty into an exemption: the difficulty must be relevant
to the choice under examination.
The second is relevant knowledge. What did the person know, and
what did they have reasonable grounds to find out? We do not de‐
mand that they predict every imaginable consequence. But neither do
we accept deliberately maintained ignorance as innocence. An official
who refuses to receive risk reports is not in the same position as
someone from whom information was concealed in a way they could
not possibly detect.
The third is the connection to the harm. Did we cause something, facil‐
itate it, authorize it, benefit from it, or simply encounter the result?
These relationships differ. Benefit can matter when allocating reparat‐
54


ive obligations without proving harmful intent. Causal contribution
can exist without blame. And the absence of a contribution does not
always eliminate the obligation to provide simple, urgent help.
The fourth is role. Some tasks fall to us because we accepted them or
because others justifiably depend on us. But roles have limits and can
be unfairly constructed. A position cannot demand every sacrifice,
and a social expectation does not become legitimate merely because it
has been repeated for a long time. We must ask whether the obliga‐
tion has a justification that can also be offered to the person bearing
its burden.
The fifth is the power to bring about change. A director can amend a
rule; an employee can only flag it; a beneficiary may at most lodge an
appeal. Asking the same of them would be false equality. Yet power is
not measured solely in titles. Whoever controls information, access, or
the agenda may wield influence that the organizational chart does not
show.
The sixth is cost, considered alongside the severity and urgency of the
need. Losing a few minutes is not the same as losing your basic secur‐
ity. Preventing an inconvenience is not the same as preventing serious
injury. Neither need nor cost should be described strategically: we do
not minimize someone else's sacrifice merely because it would suit us
for them to make it.
Finally, those affected must have a voice. They can correct the descrip‐
tion of the problem and reveal costs the decision-maker does not see.
Listening to them does not mean granting them infallibility or an
automatic veto over every collective action. It means not constructing
the judgment exclusively from the perspective of the person with the
power to impose it.
The map produces questions, not automatic verdicts. In the case of the
child who broke a cup, capacity and the educational purpose matter
greatly. In the case of the company that concealed a defect, know‐
ledge, contribution, and power carry a different weight. In the case of
the passerby who can prevent serious harm at little cost, an obligation
may exist without any prior role. No single ingredient explains
everything.
55


Shared responsibility does not always behave like a pie, either. Discov‐
ering that an organization has a serious obligation does not imply that
every individual automatically has a smaller one. The institution may
need to repair the system, while the person may need to tell the truth
about what they did. These are different obligations, not competing
percentages of the same unit of blame.
Reparation Is Not a Ceremony of Humiliation
After harm has occurred, the public may demand a culprit, the victim
may demand safety, the organization may demand an end to the scan‐
dal, and the accused may demand to be heard. These demands are not
identical. Some are compatible; others conflict. A responsible process
must distinguish them before announcing that “justice has been
done.”
First comes protection. If a risk continues, it may be necessary to re‐
strict an activity before establishing all retrospective responsibilities.
But a preventive measure should not automatically be presented as a
verdict of guilt. Its justification concerns risk and proportionality, and
its conditions must be reviewed when new information emerges.
Next comes the truth about the act. Who did what, under what condi‐
tions, with what knowledge? A compelling moral story can emerge be‐
fore the evidence. An accusation does not become truer because it fits
our beliefs about a group. Nor is a person's good reputation sufficient
to refute a specific accusation.
Then comes reparation. Sometimes this may mean returning property,
correcting a decision, funding support, or publicly acknowledging an
injustice. At other times, nothing equivalent can undo the loss. Ac‐
knowledging this limit is more honest than turning a sum of money or
a statement into a compulsory end to suffering.
Apologies have value when they identify the act, acknowledge its ef‐
fect, and connect to verifiable changes. “I regret that you felt hurt” can
avoid acknowledging the conduct itself. But even perfect wording is
not proof of a transformation. One person may speak awkwardly and
make serious amends; another may speak impeccably and repeat the
same act.
56


The victim does not owe forgiveness to make the community feel at
peace. Forgiveness may be meaningful to them, but it must not be im‐
posed as the final task of someone already harmed. At the same time,
the absence of personal forgiveness does not by itself determine all
the future rights of the wrongdoer. Shared life needs criteria that do
not reduce every decision to a single emotion, however understand‐
able it may be.
Punishment raises a further problem. It may be defended through
desert, prevention, protection, or the reaffirmation of limits. These
justifications are not equivalent. If a measure does not protect, deter,
or repair, and merely humiliates, its defense must explain why addi‐
tional suffering would be a good in itself. This book does not resolve
the entire philosophy of punishment, but it refuses to confuse severity
with moral seriousness.
There is also a legitimate interest in the possibility of returning. A per‐
son who has done wrong need not inevitably be reduced to their
worst act. This does not require restoring every position or relation‐
ship. Some roles demand a level of trust that can be lost. But we must
be able to distinguish between a justified restriction on a role and the
person's indefinite exclusion from every possible future.
A culture in which every admission of error brings social destruction
creates an incentive to conceal. This is an observation about the logic
of incentives, not a claim that we can eliminate wrongdoing through
leniency. The response to mistakes must make truth and correction
possible without turning confession into a license to repeat them.
The same logic applies to institutions. Firing the most visible person is
not enough if the rules that contributed to the incident remain intact.
Nor is announcing a “culture change” enough without examining indi‐
vidual acts. Serious reparation combines a personal response with
changes to the conditions that made the harm possible.
A School and a Society That Do Not Demand
Omniscience
A school of responsibility would not teach children only to identify
who broke the rule. It would teach them to ask what the rule protects,
what information is missing, what can be repaired, and when the rule
57


itself must change. These are philosophically motivated educational
proposals, not a presentation of a program whose effectiveness we
claim has been demonstrated in a controlled study.
One exercise could begin with the same event described from several
positions: student, teacher, parent, affected classmate. Students would
have to explain what changes when new information appears. The
aim would not be to find an excuse every time, but to learn the differ‐
ence between consistency and stubbornly maintaining a verdict after
its grounds have disappeared.
Another exercise would separate intention, means, and outcome.
Well-intentioned help can cause harm; a good outcome can arise from
an imprudent choice. The student would be invited to assess what
could have been anticipated, rather than merely rewarding the win‐
ner in retrospect. Luck must not become the only teacher of morality.
Collective responsibility should also be practiced without confusing it
with shared guilt. If a space is damaged and the perpetrator is un‐
known, maintaining it may be a shared task, but this does not justify
declaring everyone responsible for causing the damage. If a class has
an unfair rule, students can help change it without being forced to
confess to fictitious guilt.
At the social level, the same education should be supported by institu‐
tions capable of learning. An appeal should not be treated solely as an
administrative inconvenience. An incident report should allow causes
to be examined without predetermining the conclusion. A service
must be able to discover when the people it exists to serve cannot use
it. These are criteria for moral design, not guarantees that every spe‐
cific mechanism will work.
Let us bring the book's cases together in a hypothetical situation. A
city introduces an automated procedure for accessing support. A
homeless person cannot complete the application. An official notices
that the problem keeps recurring. The researcher who evaluated the
system receives a complaint. A citizen sees only the queue at the
counter and concludes that the beneficiaries are irresponsible.
The applicant may have the task of providing information they can
obtain and understand. The official may have the task of recording the
problem and using an existing support channel. Management may
58


have an obligation to change the procedure and provide resources.
The researcher must examine whether the evaluation overlooked a
relevant type of failure. We can ask the citizen not to turn an incom‐
plete picture into a confident verdict. None of these tasks is identical
to the others.
If the system was reasonably designed and the problem is new, there
may be less blame for its initial occurrence. Once the effect becomes
known and can be remedied, the justification for inaction changes. Re‐
sponsibility is not only an archive of the past. It is also how we re‐
spond to information that has just become available.
There remains one's own life. A responsible person is allowed to sleep,
to enjoy themselves, to be unavailable, not to read every news story,
and not to respond to every request. These freedoms need not be justi‐
fied solely by making them more productive at helping others. Their
life has value to them as well. Including them among the people who
matter is not a departure from morality.
But limits must not be used as a formula for disappearing. Between “I
cannot do everything” and “nothing falls to me” lies a large space for
action. Within that space are promises kept, errors corrected, risks re‐
ported, regular contributions, and improved institutions. They are not
spectacular every day. That is precisely why a society should not re‐
cognize responsibility only when someone makes a dramatic sacrifice.
Taking responsibility without believing yourself to be God means ac‐
cepting two limits simultaneously. You cannot control the entire
world. Nor are you completely outside it. Between the illusion of om‐
nipotence and the comfort of powerlessness lie finite, contestable, yet
real obligations. Seeking them honestly is harder than declaring your‐
self innocent or a savior.

EPILOGUE. The Door Remains
Ours
The man outside the door has not, in the meantime, become a simple
problem. We do not know all the causes of his situation. We do not
know whether he will accept help, what kind of help he needs, or
what can be achieved that evening. We have not discovered the bio‐
graphy that would give us the comforting feeling that he unquestion‐
ably deserves not to be abandoned.
We can begin with a respectful question and an assessment of the im‐
mediate need, without promising what we cannot do. We can seek ap‐
propriate support when the situation calls for it. We can avoid turning
him into a spectacle. The next day, we can also ask why the only avail‐
able solution was improvised by passersby. Immediate action and the
institutional problem do not exclude each other.
Perhaps one of the people in the building indirectly caused some of
the world's injustice. Perhaps another is barely staying on their feet.
Perhaps someone helped yesterday and can do no more today. A re‐
sponsible account does not assume it knows all this at a glance. Nor
does it wait to know everything before making any possible gesture.
The child asks why the man is sleeping there. One easy answer would
say that people end up where they deserve. Another easy answer
would say that no one is responsible for anything, because everything
has causes. Both would close the conversation too soon. A more hon‐
est answer would begin like this: we do not yet know all the reasons;
some concern choices, others circumstances, and the fact that we do
not know does not, by itself, tell us what to do now.
Perhaps responsibility is not a hidden property we find inside people,
like eye color. Perhaps it is a practice through which we connect
power to explanation, action to consequences, and vulnerability to
justified demands. This practice can be unjust. It can be used to discip‐
line the weak and protect the powerful. That is precisely why it must
be examined, not abandoned.
60


The reality of suffering does not, by itself, settle every moral dispute.
But it places a serious limit on the pleasure of treating everything as a
game of concepts. Beyond our formulations, someone bears the con‐
sequences. A philosophy of responsibility is worth keeping insofar as
it helps us see more precisely who bears them, who can bring about
change, and what demand can be justified.
We will not leave this book with our innocence proven. Nor should we
leave with the impression that we are personally obliged to repair the
universe. Those would be two ways of avoiding the same work: identi‐
fying our share, examining it, and fairly distributing the rest.
That evening, someone opens the door. We do not know whether the
gesture will solve much. We need not invent a happy ending to recog‐
nize its meaning. Sometimes responsibility begins precisely with re‐
fusing to demand certainty of a good ending before doing something
justified.

How to Read a Study Without
Turning It into a Verdict
A psychological finding can change how we judge a situation without
delivering a verdict on every person in it. If an experiment shows that
a particular pressure changes behavior on average, it does not follow
that we know how a specific individual reacted. The group effect and
the biographical explanation are different levels.
An experiment compares conditions constructed by researchers. Ran‐
dom assignment, when properly carried out and maintained, can help
separate the intervention's effect from other differences between
groups. But the conclusion remains tied to the intervention actually
tested, the measurements used, and the population studied. After
reading, we cannot expand the meaning of a narrow task into “human
morality in general.”
Observational research examines differences in the existing world. It
can identify important associations and attempt to account statistic‐
ally for alternative explanations. Yet the fact that two characteristics
vary together does not by itself establish causal direction or the ab‐
sence of other causes. That is why the study of collective efficacy must
not be read as a promise that a neighborhood will become safe if res‐
idents are simply encouraged to trust more.
A meta-analysis combines findings from several studies, but does not
automatically turn their diversity into a universal law. We need to
know what types of situations were included, how effects were meas‐
ured, and how different the conditions are. The average can be useful
while variation across contexts remains essential to a practical de‐
cision.
The absence of a detected difference also requires care. Simply stating
it does not prove that two interventions are identical. The precision of
the estimate and what the study was capable of detecting matter.
When a study in this book found no differences on a dimension, that
wording must not be expanded into a claim that the dimension cannot
be influenced in any way.
62


There is also the question of the unit of analysis. A study of the prob‐
ability that an individual will help answers a different question from
one about the probability that at least one person in a group will in‐
tervene. Findings may seem contradictory if the reader shifts un‐
noticed from one question to the other. Sometimes an apparent con‐
troversy is, first of all, a confusion about what was measured.
Finally, a finding can be correct and still be misused. An average diffi‐
culty observed in a group does not justify withdrawing the rights of
every member. A mechanism of obedience does not absolve every act
committed under authority. An effect of material constraints does not
turn a person into an object without initiative. Further arguments are
needed between description and the moral treatment of people.
Responsible reading thus keeps two questions separate: “What do the
data warrant believing?” and “What do we have reason to do in light
of them?” The second needs the first, but is not fully contained within
it.

A Short Vocabulary of
Responsibility
Moral agent. A being to whom we can address reasons and to whom
we can attribute actions in a sense relevant to moral evaluation. The
exact conditions of this status are disputed; merely producing an ef‐
fect is not enough.
Morally considerable person or being. Someone whose interests
matter in justifying our conduct. Having this status does not necessar‐
ily entail the capacity to answer morally for one's own acts.
Causal responsibility. Contribution to bringing about an outcome. It
does not automatically entail intention, sufficient control, blame, or
an obligation to punish.
Retrospective responsibility. Assessment of what an agent did or
omitted to do, and of the grounds for praise, criticism, or blame.
Prospective responsibility. The obligation to act in the future: pre‐
vention, protection, verification, or coordination. It can exist without
blame for the origin of the problem.
Role responsibility. Obligations attached to a position or relationship.
The role must itself be justified and does not automatically override
other moral demands.
Reparative responsibility. The obligation to redress, compensate for,
or acknowledge harm. Reparation does not mean that every loss can
be undone.
Legal liability. An obligation or consequence established within a
framework of law and procedures. It is not synonymous with the gen‐
eral moral assessment of a person.
Being accountable. Being open to questions about a decision and re‐
quired to provide explanations, information, and, where appropriate,
corrections. A simple declaration of accepting responsibility does not
exhaust this relationship.
64


Excuse and justification. An excuse may reduce the agent's blame
without making the action good. A justification maintains that the ac‐
tion was permitted or required in the circumstances.
Moral luck. The influence of unchosen factors on the situations, con‐
sequences, or characteristics for which people are morally evaluated.
Compatibilism. The family of positions according to which a relevant
form of freedom and responsibility can be compatible with determin‐
ism. It does not assume that every constraint is unimportant.
Culpable ignorance. Ignorance for which there are grounds for re‐
proach, because the agent had the relevant opportunity and obliga‐
tion to become informed. Not all ignorance falls into this category.
Special obligation. A requirement connected to specific promises,
roles, or relationships, distinct from what we would owe any person.
The limits of legitimate partiality remain disputed.
Supererogation. A morally good action that exceeds what is obligat‐
ory. Where this boundary lies, especially in helping those in need, is
one of the book's central disputes.
Problem of evil. The family of arguments examining the relationship
between the existence and nature of suffering and certain conceptions
of divine power, knowledge, and goodness.
