George Orwell’s 1984

This four-part reading of Orwell’s novel follows a single question: how can a state make its account of reality more powerful than a person’s own memory, language, and relationships? Its chapters move from the visible machinery of control to the pressure placed on thought and intimacy, and finally to the Party’s pursuit of power for its own sake.

1. Totalitarianism and social control

Oceania has an Inner Party that governs, an Outer Party that carries out administration, and a much larger population of proles. Big Brother’s image gives this hierarchy a personal face. Telescreens, informants, and the Thought Police make surveillance feel constant, so people learn to monitor themselves. The threat of being erased from public memory extends fear beyond arrest or punishment.

The chapter stresses that force alone does not explain the Party’s stability. Public records change, yesterday’s claims disappear, and doublethink trains citizens to accept contradictions. Uniform routines and suspicion weaken independent bonds. Organized anger is redirected into official rituals such as Hate Week and the Two Minutes Hate. When people cannot rely on shared memory or on one another, resistance becomes harder to imagine and harder to coordinate.

Orwell’s society is therefore more than a collection of cruel rules. It is a system that joins surveillance, historical revision, social isolation, and emotional direction. The chapter asks readers to notice how these mechanisms reinforce each other: fear discourages disagreement, while a changing record makes disagreement difficult to prove.

2. Language and the manipulation of thought

Newspeak is designed to reduce, rather than enrich, the range of available words. In the chapter’s account, the Party removes meanings linked to political freedom and independent judgment so that dissent becomes harder to formulate. Syme’s work on the dictionary embodies this ambition. Language is not merely a way to report thought here; it becomes a boundary around the thoughts that can be stated and shared.

Party slogans such as “War is Peace” rely on doublethink. Repetition makes contradiction feel familiar, while propaganda fills posters, broadcasts, education, and children’s reading. Winston’s work at the Ministry of Truth changes old newspapers to match current Party claims. Once the archive is rewritten, citizens lose a stable reference point for testing the present against the past.

The chapter connects these practices without saying that every change in vocabulary has the same effect. Its concern is a deliberate concentration of linguistic, educational, and archival power. If a government determines the words, the lessons, and the surviving evidence, citizens must struggle to name a disagreement and to show why it matters.

3. Human relationships and resistance

Winston and Julia’s private relationship gives them a space where they can speak and act outside Party expectations. Their rented room appears to offer shelter from telescreens, and physical intimacy defies a regime that treats attachment as a rival to loyalty. Yet secrecy cannot remove fear. Each person must wonder whom to trust, and the possibility of betrayal reaches even their closest bond.

O’Brien exploits Winston’s hope that another person might share his opposition. Children are encouraged to report their parents, and Winston’s memories of his mother contrast with this engineered suspicion. His diary, the glass paperweight, and conversations about the past are small attempts to preserve a self and a history the Party cannot author completely. Julia resists more through immediate private pleasures than through a program for political change.

The chapter does not present these forms of resistance as secure. Winston and Julia are captured, tortured, and separated. Their fate shows how power can attack the trust on which collective action depends. At the same time, the very effort to make a private life reveals what the Party must keep suppressing: attachment, memory, and the wish to choose one’s own commitments.

4. Power, truth, and human nature

The final chapter asks what the Party wants after it has already secured obedience. O’Brien’s account points to power itself: domination must reach beyond conduct into belief and identity. The Ministry of Truth rewrites events, while doublethink demands acceptance of the latest version even when it conflicts with memory. The Party aims to make its authority the test of what is real.

Winston’s interrogation pushes this claim to its limit. Torture is used not just to force a statement but to alter how he understands himself and other people. Room 101 turns his strongest fear against his bond with Julia. The chapter treats this as a question about the fragility of identity under extreme pressure, rather than as proof that human beings lack any capacity for freedom.

Together, the four chapters describe a sequence: control the surroundings, narrow the language, weaken relationships, and claim authority over truth. The reading’s lasting question is how people can protect shared evidence, independent thought, and trust before these supports are dismantled.
