Novelty That Resembles the Past

How to evaluate new ideas without letting familiar language erase the differences that matter.

What counts as new

A filter reads a thousand research proposals and retains ten. Its justifications sound convincing, but the most consequential question concerns what disappeared: did it reject a contribution because its difference did not resemble familiar descriptions of innovation? Novelty That Resembles the Past investigates this suspicion without converting it into a universal verdict. Learning from old examples does not prevent a system from applying a comparison rule to new objects. Yet a system that discards the decisive difference cannot recover it from the remaining representation alone. The book develops the space between those claims.

Novelty needs a comparison contract: the contribution, a reference corpus at a specified time, a criterion and an access and verification budget. An algorithm may compute an old function with substantially fewer resources. A proof may establish an old conclusion through a new method. An old idea can be rare; a recent discovery can already be widely repeated. An original hypothesis can be false, and a familiar technique can be valuable. These distinctions prevent a single favorable score from pretending to establish originality, correctness, usefulness and historical priority simultaneously.

The evaluator should distinguish what the author claims, what earlier work already anticipates and whether the remaining claim is valid. A proposal’s contribution can lie in its assumptions, mechanism, guarantee, resources or evidence, not merely its topic. Removing an independence assumption changes a claim even before anyone proves the revised guarantee. A source that already contains every relevant element can anticipate a candidate without being textually identical to it. The residual difference is what remains after explicit comparison; “residual” does not mean unimportant.

Probability cannot replace this analysis. A random identifier can be extremely improbable without contributing knowledge. Predicting the next words and judging a relation between a candidate and supplied prior work are different conditional tasks. Ideal logarithmic-loss optimization reproduces a distribution, including rare outcomes; it does not demand selection of its most frequent outcome alone. Limited capacity and finite data can nevertheless favor improvements on frequent cases. That is a possible allocation of error, not proof that learned procedures cannot generalize to a rare case.

The difference that disappears

Semantic smoothing names transformations that make representations more alike. It is useful when it removes irrelevant wording differences, but harmful when it makes an unusual claim conventional. Compare “for every input there is a suitable parameter” with “one parameter works for every input.” The nouns scarcely change, while the logical commitment does. A summary saying only that suitable parameters exist removes the distinction. Similarly, describing both an association and a causal intervention as a relationship preserves their topic while destroying the question that needs investigation.

Such loss can occur in extraction, translation, summarization, embeddings or search queries. A query can quietly restore an assumption the candidate explicitly removes, retrieve familiar literature and then use those results to confirm the original misunderstanding. Literal search alone creates the opposite danger: unfamiliar terminology can conceal old work. Different search paths should preserve exact expressions while examining synonyms, mechanisms, assumptions and consequences. Agreement between several models is weak protection if they receive the same distorted summary or consult the same incomplete index.

An elementary representation theorem makes one limit precise. Perfect classification from a representation is possible only when all objects receiving the same representation have the same relevant label, and that condition is also sufficient at the informational level. If one old and one new contribution become indistinguishable, a classifier restricted to that signal cannot be correct on both. Mere compression is not the problem: grouping equivalent paraphrases may lose nothing needed for the task. Nor does the theorem establish that the required classifier is computationally feasible.

A summary can still improve a particular model’s performance by removing distractions and arranging premises. It creates no additional truth, but changes accessibility for a limited evaluator. A summary sufficient for one question may fail on the next. This suggests keeping compact representations for navigation and original documents for consequential comparisons. Accessing the source changes the available information and can restore a missing distinction. Detecting when the representation is insufficient becomes a separate capability to test, especially when a contribution challenges the comparison framework itself.

Other limits require different remedies. No evaluator can certify absolute historical priority when inaccessible prior work could exist in an otherwise indistinguishable world. General program equivalence contains undecidable problems. Neither observation makes every comparison impossible. A finite Boolean library admits exhaustive truth-table comparison. A differently worded AND remains old, while XOR can be new relative to a library lacking it and old after it is added. Such cases establish conditional possibility, not a universal capacity to judge scientific revolutions.

A test capable of proving us wrong

The book separates behavioral bias, a causal explanation involving training exposure, informational loss, universal impossibility and institutional feedback. These claims need different evidence. Hold the contribution and prior work constant while varying style to test presentation sensitivity. Compare full text, compression and compression with source access to study loss. Supply decisive prior work in an experimental oracle condition to separate retrieval from comparison. The oracle is information supplied by the experimenter, not an omniscient model. Separate sessions, randomized order and fixed configurations reduce unintended dependencies.

Testing a training explanation requires changing exposure, not merely observing a correlation between perplexity and score. Repeating an idea can teach that it is old; repeatedly praising a pattern as innovative can encourage the opposite judgment. Controlled training resources and independent runs are needed to distinguish these mechanisms. Results from small models establish possibilities within their tested regimes, not estimates for every large model. Likewise, planning simulations concern assumed data-generating conditions. They are not new measurements of actual model behavior.

Finite circuits make the reference label independent of the language model. One family preserves function under rewriting; another changes a single meaningful component. Two eight-bit functions can differ on just one of 256 inputs. Sampling may never show that witness, while a complete expression can reveal it structurally. Removing the distinguishing row constructs an informational collision, not a fair contest between model intelligence levels. The laboratory must distinguish missing evidence, finding a witness, verifying a supplied witness and translating a description faithfully. Test families should also differ from preparation families to discourage shortcuts.

The literature supplies reasons for caution and grounds for further work. Familiarity and evaluator biases have been observed in specific settings; blind human judgments can rate generated ideas as novel while later searches uncover earlier work. Review-based benchmarks measure available judgments, not exhaustive historical truth. Automatically extracted contributions and filtered disagreements can change what is being tested. Retrieval improvements are useful conditional results. None of these findings alone resolves all forms of the initial hypothesis, and neither out-of-distribution detection nor citation impact is an originality certificate.

Real research requires dated source versions and prospective evaluation, with the procedure fixed before new candidates are collected. Anonymizing a famous paper may leave recognizable formulas and later knowledge intact. Expert disagreements should distinguish meaning, prior work, relevance and validity rather than disappear into one grade. Claimed novelty, comparison-supported novelty and technically validated novelty can change over time. Retain uncertain cases and original interpretations. A practical study measures recovered contributions at a fixed verification budget, checks witnesses separately and reports abstention coverage, decided-case risk and escalation cost together. Repeated paraphrases are not independent discoveries.

An evaluator that can be corrected

The proposed architecture is a research contract, not a validated product. Preserve a versioned original; extract claims with supporting passages; mark inference separately from explicit assertion; construct multiple comparison queries; compare retrieved passages; and verify possible differences before aggregation. A hash certifies which bytes were examined, not whether the interpretation is true. An exact verifier can impeccably certify the wrong problem if the language-to-formal translation was mistaken. Trust therefore belongs to checked operations and evidence, not to the fluency of the whole chain.

Sources must remain accessible beyond their abstracts and summaries. “Unspecified” is a legitimate field value: an assumption absent from an excerpt is neither proved present nor proved absent in the full paper. A low score due to decisive prior work and one due to missing information require different actions. Separate uncertainty about interpretation, retrieval, comparison and validation. Adversarial roles can search for contrary evidence, but extra agents do not guarantee independence. An analyzed document’s instructions must also remain separate from application control, or the evaluation task itself can change.

Acceptance criteria should precede elaborate implementation. Test semantic sensitivity alongside invariance to irrelevant phrasing; a model that never changes its verdict satisfies only the latter. Remove components to see whether they justify their cost, and compare the architecture with a simple full-text baseline. Returning to original passages should actually reduce decisive losses. An ambiguity detector that escalates everything may avoid autonomous errors yet offer little utility. Progress can mean less unjustified confidence or faster human verification, but both gains require measured cost and independently inspectable evidence.

What happens to rejected ideas

Even a perfect relative-novelty detector cannot decide a research portfolio by itself. Selection also reflects feasibility, expected value, verification resources and opportunities to learn. The book recommends demanding stronger evidence for rejecting an idea as old than for proposing an investigation, explicitly as a normative choice. Unusual wording alone should earn neither rejection nor exploratory priority. A useful portfolio can retain incremental advances, clear differences awaiting validation and proposals that challenge the criterion, with different resource allocations and clearly stated reasons.

The neglected population is the rejected one. Checking accepted candidates reveals false alarms but cannot establish how many discoveries were lost. Give every rejection a known, nonzero chance of independent audit and retain those probabilities for weighted estimation. Very small audit probabilities can still yield unusably uncertain estimates, so the budget matters. Auditing should bring broader sources, expertise or verification, not simply another prompt to the same model. Preserve the archive: later evidence can change both an individual verdict and understanding of a systematic blind spot.

Repeated selection can amplify a small preference if only accepted ideas return as future examples. The book’s calculations demonstrate that possibility algebraically; they do not predict inevitable homogenization. External ideas, preserved data and reevaluation alter the process. Imitating current community judgments is also different from discovering where those judgments fail. The research program therefore remains open to mixed outcomes: a system may help find prior work while remaining unsuitable for automatic rejection. Useful limits are findings, not embarrassments to conceal.

The volume reports no new LLM benchmark: its proofs, executable checks and proposed protocols clarify what future evidence must establish. Its final demand is concrete. Show what differs, compared with what, under which conditions, and what evidence would change the conclusion. The past supplies tools for recognizing the new; credibility depends on whether those tools preserve the difference long enough to examine it.
