Preface. The Problem of an
Evaluator Educated by the Past
A system is asked to read a thousand proposals and retain ten genu‐
inely new ideas. It was trained on texts from the past, learned regular‐
ities of scientific discourse and recognizes expressions such as “a new
perspective,” “a unified framework” or “an unexplored combination.”
The uncomfortable question is not whether the system can produce
ten elegant justifications. The question is whether what it rejects in‐
cludes precisely the contribution that does not yet sufficiently re‐
semble what the community calls innovation.
This book investigates a precise suspicion: language models might
confuse novelty with the familiarity of patterns of novelty. The hypo‐
thesis is neither ridiculed through an abstract appeal to “generaliza‐
tion” nor turned into a theorem merely because it sounds plausible. It
is broken down into different claims with different truth conditions.
Some describe experimental risks. Others are demonstrable informa‐
tional limits. Still others are too strong and admit constructive
counterexamples.
The position defended here is that a model trained on the past is not,
for that reason alone, incapable of recognizing a new difference. Con‐
versely, a system that has lost the relevant difference before evalu‐
ation can no longer recover it from the remaining representation un‐
less it receives additional information. Almost the entire practical
problem lies between these two claims. We must determine what in‐
formation the evaluator sees, what comparison it performs, what it
understands by prior work and what evidence allows it to move from
resemblance to identity or from difference to contribution.
The text is intended for researchers and programmers who want to
design tools for analyzing ideas, selecting proposals, examining the lit‐
erature or automating research. The intended level is advanced, but
the necessary concepts are introduced before they are used. Familiar‐
ity with information theory, causal inference or computability is not
5


assumed. What is assumed is the reader’s willingness to follow argu‐
ments, distinguish definitions from discoveries and accept that a rig‐
orous negative result can be more useful than an impressive score.
One editorial distinction is essential. Accounts of published experi‐
ments are accompanied by numbered references. Working defini‐
tions, elementary proofs, constructed examples and proposed proto‐
cols are presented as such. The book does not report a new bench‐
mark run on commercial or open models. The small calculations and
logical examples are didactic checks; they are not presented as empir‐
ical findings about an LLM’s capabilities. The architectural proposals
are research projects, not descriptions of an already validated
product.
The literature is a critical selection of primary sources, checked
through September 11, 2026, not an exhaustive systematic review. The
versions consulted are identified in the bibliography. Results concern‐
ing particular models, datasets and procedures are not automatically
generalized to all future models. Likewise, agreement with a reviewer
is not treated as identity with historical truth, and failure to find prior
work in a search engine’s results is not treated as proof of its absence.
The book can be read sequentially, from foundations toward experi‐
ment, or used as a design manual. The chapters on information and
computability establish what can coherently be required of an evalu‐
ator. The methodological chapters show how a failure mechanism can
be identified. The final chapters discuss how to build a system that
preserves its ability to be corrected. The appendices offer additional
proofs, a preregistration protocol, an executable example and a tech‐
nical vocabulary.
The aim is neither to defend nor condemn a class of models. It is to
protect a fragile intellectual operation: noticing a difference before
the language, statistics, criteria or institution evaluating it reduces it
to something already known.

Chapter 1. What the Hypothesis
Actually Claims
1.1. From Intuitive Suspicion to Separable
Propositions
The formula “an LLM finds only what it has encountered before”
mixes at least two senses of the verb to find. A system can retrieve a
stored example, but it can also apply a learned rule to an instance that
did not exist in training. To recognize that two numbers differ, it need
not have previously memorized their pair. To determine that a pro‐
gram produces an output absent from a finite library, it need not have
encountered that particular program. The fact that a competence was
learned from the past does not imply that the objects on which it can
operate are limited to the past.
This observation does not solve the problem of language models. It
eliminates only an invalid inference. It remains to be established
whether the model actually applies a comparison procedure or re‐
sponds on the basis of superficial regularities. An architecture might
allow an operation without the training procedure inducing it suffi‐
ciently well. A competence might exist on simple problems and be lost
on long texts. A difference might be recognized correctly but wrongly
interpreted as unimportant. Logical possibility and actual perform‐
ance must therefore be examined separately.
The behavioral form of the hypothesis says that the evaluator favors
ideas expressed through familiar patterns of innovation even when
the semantic contribution is held constant. The causal form claims
that this preference is produced by frequencies or correlations in
training. The informational form claims that certain intermediate rep‐
resentations erase the differences needed for classification. The uni‐
versal form claims that no LLM can detect a genuinely new contribu‐
tion. These propositions are not equivalent. A proof of the informa‐
tional form for a particular representation does not prove the univer‐
sal form. A correlation between perplexity and score does not, by it‐
self, identify the training process as the cause.
7


There is also an institutional form of the suspicion: selection systems
can turn a modest preference into an important cumulative effect. If a
filter more often rejects ideas with unusual vocabulary, and only ac‐
cepted ideas are then discussed, cited and used to train other filters,
the space of future examples may narrow. This is a hypothesis about a
dynamic process, not a property demonstrated by merely analyzing a
single answer. It requires observations of selection, allocated re‐
sources and information fed back into the system.
To avoid ambiguity, we will call familiarity the fit with regularities
already learned or frequent in a representation; we will call novelty
the relevant difference relative to an explicit reference corpus. Famili‐
arity can be linguistic, conceptual, methodological or social. Novelty
can concern the mechanism, result, applicability conditions or proof.
A highly familiar formulation can describe a new contribution. A
strange formulation can conceal a banal idea. Neither “familiar” nor
“new” should be used without specifying the object of comparison.
A constructed example shows why this discipline matters. The first
proposal says that a protocol works when fewer than one third of the
participants behave adversarially. The second claims the same guar‐
antee under a different assumption about synchrony. A thematic sum‐
mary can represent both texts as “a distributed protocol tolerant of
malicious participants.” At this level they seem identical. At the level
of assumptions and guarantees, decisive differences may exist. It is
not necessary to assume that the second proposal is correct. To detect
the claimed contribution, it is enough to notice that the claims are not
the same.
The example introduces a separation that will recur throughout the
book. Identifying the stated difference, checking whether prior work
exists for that difference and demonstrating its validity are distinct
operations. If an evaluator compresses them into a single verdict, an
error can no longer be diagnosed. “It is not new” may mean that the
mechanism is old, that the model has not understood it, that it does
not believe the proof or that it prefers a more popular problem. A
score without decomposition can conceal all these situations.
A good hypothesis must also specify what result would weaken it. If a
model receives two contributions new to it, the correct prior work
and an exact criterion, then robustly identifies the difference under
8


controlled reformulations, the claim of general impossibility becomes
untenable. If, however, novelty is detected only when the text contains
conventional expressions of originality, the behavioral suspicion gains
strength. If changing training frequencies changes evaluations
without changing the ground truth, a causal explanation also comes
closer.
The book’s methodological position can be stated simply: it is not
enough to ask whether the model “understands novelty.” We must
construct situations in which an explanation based on understanding
the difference and one based on recognizing the packaging make dif‐
ferent predictions. Only then does the experiment separate the mech‐
anisms.
1.2. Novelty Is Not Rarity, Truth or Value
Rarity is a property of a distribution. Novelty is a relation to prior
work. An old method ignored by the current community may be rare
in the analyzed corpus yet not historically new. A recent discovery
may be repeated in thousands of documents and still remain new rel‐
ative to the state of knowledge last year. Corpus frequency, training
exposure and historical priority are different variables. Confusing
them can produce both enthusiasm for rediscoveries and rejection of
discoveries that have already become visible.
Linguistic novelty concerns the form of expression. Conceptual nov‐
elty concerns what is claimed or the mechanism proposed. Operation‐
al novelty concerns a new capacity to do something. Epistemic novelty
concerns what becomes known or justified for an agent or com‐
munity. Historical novelty concerns the absence of relevant prior
work before a given time. These distinctions are the book’s working
definitions. They do not claim that a single taxonomy accepted by all
disciplines exists.
A new proof of a known theorem illustrates the independence of the
dimensions. The conclusion may be old, the proof method new and
verification time shorter. An algorithm may compute the same func‐
tion but have different complexity, memory requirements or privacy
properties. If the evaluator reduces the contribution to the input–out‐
put relation, it will legitimately declare the absence of functional nov‐
9


elty, but will be wrong if it extends the conclusion to algorithmic nov‐
elty in general. The semantic object chosen determines what can be
observed.
Truth constitutes another axis. A hypothesis can be original and false.
An observation can be true and well known. A proposal can be origin‐
al in its precise form but impossible under its stated conditions. In a
research tool, identifying a claimed contribution need not be suspen‐
ded until its complete validation. It is useful to know exactly what the
author claims to change before checking whether the change is feas‐
ible. At the same time, the originality of a false claim must not auto‐
matically be converted into scientific merit.
Value depends on purposes and consequences. A minor variation can
have great practical value if it eliminates a dominant cost in an applic‐
ation. A highly original construction may currently have limited use‐
fulness. These judgments can be perfectly compatible. The problem
arises when the evaluator calls everything it likes “novelty” or
everything it finds useless “lack of novelty.” At that point, the criterion
becomes a global preference and conceptual precision disappears.
Originality also has a provenance dimension. Two people can inde‐
pendently produce the same idea. The second person’s contribution
may be psychologically original without having historical priority.
Conversely, a text may present an idea unknown to the evaluator but
borrowed from an omitted source. Detecting prior work does not
automatically establish the author’s intention. An accusation of plagi‐
arism involves questions of access, attribution and source-use prac‐
tices that cannot be reduced to the semantic identity of two texts.
Boden distinguishes combinatorial, exploratory and transformational
forms of creativity. This perspective is useful because changing the
rules of the search space is not the same as finding another solution
within existing rules. However, the typology of creativity does not by
itself provide a detector of historical priority. It helps formulate the
type of change we should look for. [R01]
We can therefore define a contribution profile instead of a single
score. The profile can separate the result, mechanism, assumptions,
justification procedure and scope of application. Not every dimension
must be numerical. Sometimes a comparative statement is more in‐
10


formative than a number: “the same guarantee, but without the inde‐
pendence assumption” or “the same function, but with memory sub‐
linear in the stated parameter.” Such formulations force the evaluator
to specify the difference it recognizes.

Problem

ledge available up to time t, C represents the criterion of equivalence
or relevance, and B describes the budget for access, computation and
verification. We are not proposing a magic formula through this nota‐
tion. It serves to prevent omissions. A novelty claim without a refer‐
ence corpus, a criterion and declared resources is generally more am‐
biguous than it appears.
The criterion C may say that two contributions are equivalent if they
have the same function on the same domain. In another application,
equivalence may require the same security assumptions, the same
complexity and the same adversarial model. A change that disappears
under the first criterion may remain essential under the second. In
this sense, novelty is not arbitrary, but it depends on the question
asked. Once the criterion is fixed, the comparison can become very
precise.
The corpus K_t must be distinguished from the whole world. The in‐
dexed library, the documents accessible to the evaluator and all exist‐
ing prior work are not the same set. An unindexed article, a thesis in
another language or an earlier version of a report can change the his‐
torical verdict. The system should not be condemned for lacking inac‐
cessible information. It must, however, be prevented from turning
limited access into unlimited certainty.
11


Time introduces a further difficulty. When we evaluate an idea retro‐
spectively, the model may know later versions, replications, contro‐
versies or the community’s assessment. Even if we remove the au‐
thors’ names, information about the result may survive in the word‐
ing. A test asking “how new was this contribution in year t?” is not
automatically valid if the model was trained on documents after t. It
may recognize the discovery’s reputation instead of reconstructing the
situation at the time.
The budget B is included because being able to verify in principle does
not mean being able to verify with the available resources. Prior work
may be retrievable after a two-day search, but not within the thirty
seconds allotted to filtering. An equivalence may be decidable but too
costly for a million candidates. Comparing systems without equalizing
the budget risks turning an access or computational advantage into a
supposed conceptual superiority.
One might object that adding these parameters dilutes the notion of
novelty. The objection is legitimate if every failure is excused by con‐
veniently changing the definition. That is why the parameters must be
fixed before evaluation. Relativization serves not to avoid testing but
to make the verdict testable. “New relative to library K, under cri‐
terion C, with the decisive prior work supplied” is a narrower claim
but methodologically stronger than “the model understands original‐
ity.”
A practical detector can produce three types of conclusions. It can
identify prior work that anticipates the contribution. It can identify a
difference relative to the prior work examined. It can determine that
the data are insufficient for a conclusion. These should not be artifi‐
cially arranged on a single ordinal scale. Missing information is not
medium novelty, and the existence of an unvalidated difference is not
evidence of importance.
At the end of this clarification, the initial hypothesis becomes more
productive. The question is no longer whether the past forbids the fu‐
ture. It is whether the procedure learned from the past preserves and
examines those differences through which the future might be more
than a reformulation of the past. This question allows both proofs of
conditional impossibility and constructions that demonstrate possibil‐
ity.

Chapter 2. Probability,
Information and Surprise
2.1. Why Low Probability Does Not Mean High
Novelty
A probabilistic model assigns weights to possible outcomes. If we have
a distribution p over a vocabulary, p(w) describes the probability of a
symbol w under the modeled conditions. In a language model, the dis‐
tribution is conditioned on context: the probability of a word after
“the capital of France is” differs from its probability across the whole
corpus. This elementary difference is crucial. An expression’s global
frequency does not by itself determine its probability in a question
comparing two ideas.
For a sequence of symbols, the chain rule allows the joint probability
to be factored into a product of conditional probabilities. This factoriz‐
ation is not a theory of meaning; it is a probabilistic identity. An
autoregressive implementation attempts to estimate each factor using
the available prefix. [R02, R03]
A long sequence can have very low probability even if every step is
banal. If each choice has probability one half, a sequence of one hun‐
dred choices has probability 2 to the power minus one hundred. The
improbability of the entire sequence does not imply that it contains a
discovery. This is why a document’s raw surprisal cannot be com‐
pared without controlling length and the granularity of representa‐
tion.
The informational surprisal of an outcome is the negative logarithm
of its probability. Choosing a base-two logarithm expresses informa‐
tion in bits. An outcome with probability one half has one bit of sur‐
prisal; one with probability one thousand in a million has approxim‐
ately ten bits. These values describe rarity relative to the chosen dis‐
13


tribution, not the outcome’s epistemic or moral importance. Shannon
formulated communication theory precisely by separating the trans‐
mission problem from the semantic interpretation of messages. [R02]
A random identifier can have high surprisal without saying anything
interesting about the world. A simple equation can have low textual
surprisal and important consequences. Moreover, a false statement or
typographical error can be improbable. A detector based only on sur‐
prisal would therefore also favor noise. Eliminating noise requires ad‐
ditional criteria; at that point, the detector is no longer measuring
probability alone.
Perplexity is the exponential of the average logarithmic loss over sym‐
bols. It partially normalizes the effect of length and is useful for evalu‐
ating prediction. But comparisons between models with different
tokenizations require caution: an average over tokens does not have
the same effective unit when one model splits a word into two parts
and another into five. In addition, perplexity depends on the context
under which it was calculated. Saying that an idea has “low perplex‐
ity” without specifying the text, tokenization and conditioning is insuf‐
ficient for a reproducible analysis.
The novelty problem is often classificatory rather than generative. We
want the probability of a label or relation after seeing the contribu‐
tion and prior work. This is a different distribution from the contribu‐
tion’s probability in ordinary language. A text can be highly improb‐
able yet correctly classified as incoherent. A highly predictable text
can contain a small but detectable difference relative to explicit prior
work.
The two expressions define different probabilistic objects that cannot
be substituted for one another. Their values might coincide accident‐
ally; that coincidence would not make the tasks identical. The genera‐
tion distribution places weights on texts; the classification distribution
14


places weights on labels conditioned on the text and reference. The ar‐
gument that a generator favors familiar continuations does not,
without further steps, demonstrate that the same system cannot com‐
pare a contribution with a library.
Bayes also shows why a novelty signal can mislead when discoveries
are rare. Suppose, in a constructed example, that one thousand out of
a million candidates are genuinely new under the chosen criterion.
The detector retains nine hundred of them but incorrectly classifies as
new one percent of the nine hundred and ninety-nine thousand old
candidates. We obtain nine hundred correct cases and nine thousand
nine hundred and ninety false alarms. Only approximately 8.26% of
the selections are genuine novelties.
Here π is the prevalence of novelty, TPR is the detection rate for new
cases and FPR is the false-alarm rate among old cases. The formula
follows directly from joint probabilities. It does not say that the detect‐
or is useless, but that performance must be judged relative to preval‐
ence and verification capacity. In a discovery filter, the cost of a few
false alarms may be acceptable if it prevents the loss of a rare contri‐
bution. Choosing the threshold is an operational decision, not an auto‐
matic consequence of the score.
2.2. What Learning Optimizes and What It Does Not
Promise
Consider a true distribution P and an estimated distribution Q. If we
evaluate predictions using logarithmic loss, the average cost of Q de‐
composes into the entropy of P and the Kullback–Leibler divergence
between P and Q. The proof is algebraic: we add and subtract the log‐
arithm of P in the sum defining the loss. The entropy term does not de‐
pend on Q; the divergence term is nonnegative.
In the ideal case where the model family contains the correct distribu‐
tion and optimization finds it, the solution is Q=P. It does not place all
the mass on the most frequent outcome. On the contrary, it also repro‐
15


duces the probabilities of rare events. Confusing minimization of log‐
arithmic loss with exclusively memorizing the distribution’s mode
means confusing distribution estimation with a decision rule that
chooses only the most probable outcome.
The practical situation is more difficult. Data are finite, model capacity
is limited, optimization is imperfect and the test distribution may dif‐
fer from the training distribution. A rare event may receive too few
examples for the model to learn the relevant structure. In an objective
averaged over all examples, improvement on frequent cases may nu‐
merically dominate improvement on a rare class. This observation is
an argument about error allocation, not a theorem that rare cases are
impossible to learn.
A model can learn a shared rule that also covers rare cases. If fre‐
quent examples enable it to learn addition, it can calculate a sum ab‐
sent from the data. If it learns only local associations between expres‐
sions and labels, generalization will be weaker. The difference cannot
be deduced solely from the objective. The same objective can admit
solutions with different internal strategies. Identifying the strategy re‐
quires tests that prevent solving the task through superficial correla‐
tions.
The model distribution must also be separated from the decoding pro‐
cedure. Choosing the most probable symbol at each step, sampling
and search with multiple hypotheses can produce different behaviors
from the same probabilities. Locally choosing the most probable sym‐
bol does not guarantee the globally most probable sequence. Increas‐
ing temperature can diversify outputs, but it does not turn the distri‐
bution into a novelty verifier. Textual diversity and validation of a dif‐
ference are separate problems.
An evaluator answering “new” or “old” technically produces tokens.
This observation does not imply that it cannot perform a comparison.
An equivalence-checking program also ultimately produces bits,
without the nature of its output exhausting the nature of its computa‐
tion. The relevant question is what information and operations de‐
termine the output. Reducing a competence to the form of its final in‐
struction does not constitute a sufficient functional analysis.
16


At the same time, a high probability for the token “new” must not be
confused with a well-calibrated epistemic probability of historical pri‐
ority. It may reflect prompt conventions, preferences introduced
through post-training or the fact that hesitant answers were rewarded
less. Calibration must be measured on clearly defined events. Re‐
search by Guo and colleagues shows, in a neural-classification context,
that numerical confidence and empirical correctness can diverge and
that post-processing can improve calibration. This does not imply a
guarantee for LLM novelty judgments. [R18]
A rigorous protocol keeps the internal score, textual verdict and qual‐
ity of justification separate. An answer can be correct with poorly cal‐
ibrated confidence, incorrect with a fluent justification, or supported
by a justification that identifies the correct difference but aggregates it
into an unsuitable score. Reducing all these dimensions to a single
percentage prevents understanding the failure mechanism.
2.3. Relevant Information and the Limits of
Compression
Entropy measures a variable’s average uncertainty. Mutual informa‐
tion measures how much observing one variable reduces uncertainty
about another. In our problem, X can represent the original document,
Y the novelty label under a fixed criterion and Z the representation
the system constructs. We are interested not only in how much Z re‐
tains about X. We want to know whether it retains what is needed for
Y.
A representation can eliminate almost every detail of the document
and still be sufficient for a narrow question. If we want to know
whether a circuit ever outputs one, a correctly computed bit can sum‐
marize the answer. If we later want to know which inputs produce
one, that bit is no longer sufficient. Good compression is not an abso‐
lute property. It depends on the family of future questions.
For a chain in which Z is derived only from X and the decision uses
only Z, processing cannot create information about Y that was missing
from the original observation. The intuition can be proved without
metaphysics: any rule operating on Z can be simulated by a rule that
receives X and first computes Z. Consequently, the best evaluator with
17


access to X cannot be worse than the best evaluator limited to Z,
provided resources and decision classes impose no further restric‐
tions.
This formulation leaves room for an apparently paradoxical fact. A
particular model can perform better on a summary than on the full
text. No additional information about the truth of the label has been
created; its accessibility to a limited evaluator has changed. The sum‐
mary can remove distractions, rearrange premises and reduce com‐
putational cost. The difference between a particular algorithm’s per‐
formance and the optimal performance permitted by the information
is fundamental.
The risk arises when the summary removes not noise but the relevant
difference. Suppose two proposals have the same thematic description
and differ only in a quantifier: “for every input” versus “for almost all
inputs.” If the representation retains the topic and generic result, the
two become identical. This is no longer merely a matter of evaluator
preference. The information needed for the distinction has disap‐
peared from the channel it receives.
At the engineering level, this observation suggests that simply increas‐
ing vector dimensionality is not a complete solution. A large repres‐
entation can ignore a relevant variable, while a small one can retain it
explicitly. What matters is the representation objective, the invariant
being pursued and the existence of a path back to the source. A nov‐
elty system should be able to say not only “these documents are simil‐
ar,” but also “in what respects we decided to consider them similar
and what we removed in the process.”
This is where information theory becomes a design discipline. It does
not give us a universal metric of originality, but it forces us to ask
what differences are preserved, what questions remain answerable
and where additional access to the original documents is needed. Nov‐
elty cannot be deduced from the number of bits of surprisal. Con‐
versely, losing certain semantically decisive bits can make its detec‐
tion impossible for a given architecture.

Chapter 3. What a Language
Model Learns and How It Comes
to Judge
3.1. From Tokens to a Conditioned Procedure
A language model does not receive ideas as ideal objects already sep‐
arated from their formulation. It receives a sequence of discrete units
called tokens. Tokenization can split words, keep mathematical sym‐
bols separate and represent two similar formulations differently. Be‐
fore any judgment of originality, there is therefore an encoding chan‐
nel. This observation does not automatically imply loss of meaning,
but it introduces a first control variable: task difficulty must not be
confused with the difficulty of representing the text in the tokeniza‐
tion used.
Tokens are transformed into vectors. In the Transformer architecture,
attention mechanisms allow information from different positions to
be combined using weights dependent on the current representations.
Successive layers modify these representations, and the output pro‐
duces scores for continuations. The paper that introduced the Trans‐
former describes this organization in the context of sequence model‐
ing. The architecture does not demonstrate general reasoning or ori‐
ginality-assessment capabilities. [R03]
For our problem, attention must be understood functionally. It can al‐
low a condition in one paragraph to influence interpretation of a
guarantee in another. But a communication path between positions
does not guarantee that the model tracks the correct condition. An ar‐
chitecture can express a computation without the learned weights im‐
plementing it reliably. Saying “the model can attend to the entire doc‐
ument” is a claim about structural access; saying “the model compares
all relevant assumptions” is a claim about behavior.
A programmer can view an LLM as an approximate interpreter of a
task formulated in text. The prompt specifies an incomplete contract,
examples and context supply data, and the model produces an output
19


according to learned regularities. This analogy is useful provided it is
not taken literally. There is not necessarily a stable internal separation
between parser, planner, memory and verifier. Nevertheless, in ex‐
ternal evaluation we can construct separate tests for understanding
the instruction, extracting claims, retrieving prior work and applying
the criterion.
In-context learning is adaptation of behavior using examples in the
prompt, without updating parameters during that interaction. Brown
and colleagues documented important few-shot results for a large
autoregressive model. These results show that performance is not
fully described by the question “did this exact example exist in train‐
ing?” They do not, however, guarantee learning any new rule from a
few examples. [R04]
In a novelty test, the context can define the reference world itself. We
can supply a library of three mechanisms and ask the model to de‐
termine whether a fourth is equivalent to one of them. If the mechan‐
ism names are generated randomly after the model is fixed, recogni‐
tion by reputation becomes harder. The model must use local defini‐
tions. Success would demonstrate comparison competence in a con‐
trolled world; failure would indicate a concrete obstacle without im‐
mediately identifying which internal layer produces it.
The term “semantic representation” must be used modestly. A vector
can correlate strongly with useful meanings or relations without con‐
stituting a complete, stable and interpretable description of the idea.
In a retrieval system, we care whether vector proximity brings relev‐
ant documents. In a verifier, we care whether differences between as‐
sumptions and conclusions are preserved. The same representation
can be suitable for the first operation and insufficient for the second.
It would be a design error to treat the thematic vector as the identity
of the contribution. Two papers about the same problem must be
close for retrieval to work. Precisely for that reason, their proximity
should not be used directly as evidence that no difference exists. A
representation optimized to bring relevant documents together may
compress distinctions that the final comparison must reintroduce. A
good semantic neighbor is not automatically prior work that anticip‐
ates the contribution.
20


Consequently, evaluating LLMs must begin with a functional diagram
of the available information, not assumptions about what the model
“knows.” What text does it receive? What parts are truncated? What
instruction defines novelty? What sources are added? What output
form is requested? An incorrect answer can originate at any of these
points. Without this decomposition, the explanation “the model re‐
peats the past” risks becoming a universal label applied to very differ‐
ent failures.
3.2. Generalization, Memorization and Priorities
Introduced through Post-Training
Memorization and generalization are not mutually exclusive states of
a model. A system can memorize certain sequences while also learn‐
ing regularities applicable to new sequences. For novelty detection,
both capacities can be useful or problematic. Memorizing prior work
helps reject a rediscovery. Memorizing a paper’s reputation can con‐
taminate a retrospective test. Generalizing a comparison rule helps
identify differences; overgeneralizing a category can erase the very
exception that matters.
Showing that the model can produce familiar fragments is not enough
to conclude that every verdict retrieves fragments. But absence of lit‐
eral reproduction does not prove conceptual comparison either. A
model can reformulate a memorized association. Evaluation must
construct situations in which memorization and comparison make
different predictions: changed names, reversed relations, assumptions
moved between candidates and prior work, and cases in which famili‐
ar wording conceals the wrong conclusion.
A useful test is a controlled reversal of the world. In one condition, the
library contains method A and the candidate proposes B. In another,
the same pair is reversed and the library contains B. If the label de‐
pends on the reference, the verdict must change coherently. An evalu‐
ator that always declares B newer because B uses sophisticated vocab‐
ulary fails to respect the task’s local definition. This manipulation is
more informative than simply requesting further explanations.
21


Models used as assistants are generally not fully described by autore‐
gressive pretraining. There may be stages of supervised fine-tuning
and preference optimization. Ouyang and colleagues describe a pro‐
cedure combining human demonstrations, answer comparisons and
learning from feedback. Rafailov and colleagues propose a direct pref‐
erence optimization method without constructing the entire reward-
learning loop in the same way. These papers show that the final policy
can be modified to favor particular types of answer. They do not es‐
tablish that the preferences introduced are necessarily hostile to nov‐
elty. [R05, R06]
In our problem, post-training is a causal candidate that must be separ‐
ated from pretraining. If human evaluators prefer cautious, explicit
and conventional formulations, the system may learn to produce or
appreciate them. If evaluation examples reward mentioning standard
terms, those terms may become cues. But these are hypotheses about
data and objectives. They cannot be demonstrated merely by ob‐
serving that an answer is conservative.
We could compare a base model with its fine-tuned version, but the
comparison does not automatically isolate a single mechanism. In‐
struction-following ability, style, answer length and context use may
all change simultaneously. A causal evaluation requires either more
controlled interventions or a limited interpretation: “a difference
between two training procedures,” not “the pure effect of a preference
for conformity.”
Model-generated explanations are useful as objects to verify, not as
direct access to the verdict’s internal cause. A model can produce a
plausible explanation after selecting its score from other cues. To test
whether prior work was decisive, we can remove or modify it and ob‐
serve the behavioral change. To test whether a difference was under‐
stood, we can request a prediction that depends on it. Intervening on
information is stronger than mere textual introspection.
One verification strategy separates verdict from witness. The model
must identify an earlier passage anticipating the contribution or a
case in which the candidate and prior work behave differently. The
witness can be checked independently. If the score says “new” but the
witness is false, we have a detectable error. If the witness is correct
22


but the score too low, the problem shifts to the relevance or aggrega‐
tion criterion. This separation reduces dependence on explanatory
fluency.
Finally, a model’s competence must not automatically be attributed to
the entire system, or vice versa. An LLM with access to search, compu‐
tation and verifiers can perform differently from the same LLM in
isolation. A contribution found by a symbolic verifier does not prove
that the model solved the problem alone. But using a verifier does not
invalidate the system’s usefulness either. Reporting must specify who
proposed the comparison, who executed it and who certified the res‐
ult.
3.3. Evaluator Failures That Resemble One Another
Only on the Surface
An evaluator can miss an idea because prior work is absent. This is a
coverage failure. It can receive the prior work but omit the relevant
paragraph. This is a failure of effective context access. It can read the
paragraph and confuse the quantifiers. This is an interpretation fail‐
ure. It can identify the difference and mistakenly judge it unimport‐
ant. This is a failure to apply the criterion. It can produce all the cor‐
rect elements but an inconsistent final score. This is an aggregation or
instruction failure.
The distinctions matter because the remedies differ. Expanding the
search engine can improve coverage but does not resolve confusion
between “there exists” and “for all.” A more explicit prompt can im‐
prove adherence to the criterion but does not supply the missing doc‐
ument. A larger model can reduce some errors but cannot recover in‐
formation permanently removed by an intermediate summary.
Without diagnosis, increasing inference cost risks producing only
longer explanations of the same error.
There are also failures of information positioning. Lost in the Middle
reported performance dependent on the position of relevant informa‐
tion in long contexts for the models and tasks tested. This does not
demonstrate that all future models will have the same problem. It
does, however, provide a concrete reason to control document order
and the location of decisive prior work in experiments. [R16]
23


A novelty filter is especially exposed because the important difference
can be small relative to the text’s total length. If the document con‐
tains ten pages of familiar context and one sentence changing the
scope of the guarantee, success depends on the operational attention
given to that sentence. Correctly summarizing most of the document
is not enough. A local error can reverse the global verdict.
Let us construct a simple case. The prior work sorts a collection in
memory, while the candidate claims the same order using sequential
access to external data. A thematic evaluator may observe that both
are sorting algorithms. An operational evaluator must compare the
access model and resources. If the task asks about novelty in the com‐
puted function, the two may be equivalent. If it asks about novelty in
the execution model, the difference may be central. The verdict can‐
not be judged without knowing the evaluation contract.
Another constructed case concerns negating an assumption. One text
says that the procedure requires an authenticated channel. Another
claims that this requirement can be replaced by a different assump‐
tion. A model normalizing the second formulation to the first can
make the text more familiar but less faithful. This “correction” is not
necessarily intentional. It may result from the most probable inter‐
pretation. Nevertheless, for detecting unusual contributions, intention
is secondary; preserving the difference is what matters.
The tendency to produce evaluations matching the user’s tone must
also be controlled. If the prompt asks “why is this idea revolution‐
ary?”, the task already implies a verdict. If it asks “why is this merely
a banal variation?”, it creates another pressure. A benchmark com‐
paring only affirmative versions of the same question does not meas‐
ure epistemic stability. Favorable, neutral and skeptical formulations
must be tested on the same content without suggesting that any one
represents the truth.
This chapter’s conclusion is functional. “The LLM judges novelty” is
not an elementary operation. It is a chain of transformations and
choices, some implemented by the model, others by the surrounding
application. The familiarity hypothesis becomes scientifically useful
only when located within such a chain and when an intervention can
distinguish the proposed mechanism from alternative explanations.

Chapter 4. An Operational
Semantics of Contributions
4.1. The Idea as a Structure of Claims, Not a Topic
A topic is a territory of discourse. A contribution is a definite change
within that territory or a change in its rules. “Artificial intelligence for
research” names a topic. “A verifier that detects contradictions
between the stated assumptions of two experiments” describes a func‐
tion. “A verifier that does this without access to raw data, under condi‐
tions X” introduces a possible difference. For novelty evaluation, mov‐
ing from topic to structure is essential.
An operational representation can describe the problem, assump‐
tions, mechanism, guarantees, costs and evidence. Not every field
must be completed in every document. A missing field is itself inform‐
ation: if a proposal does not state its assumptions, the evaluator
should not invent them to make it comparable. It can formulate mul‐
tiple interpretations and mark which verdict depends on each.
We will schematically write a contribution as a structured object. The
notation is an analytical tool, not a requirement that every idea fit one
universal form.
P denotes the problem, A the assumptions, M the mechanism, G the
claimed guarantees or results, R the resources and E the evidence sup‐
plied. The difference between two contributions can occur in any
component or in the relation between components. The same guaran‐
tee under weaker assumptions is not the same claim. The same mech‐
anism with a different proof can constitute another type of contribu‐
tion. The same results obtained with fewer resources may have opera‐
tional novelty even if the final function remains identical.
To avoid turning the scheme into a rigid ontology, every field must be
linked to original passages. The representation must not replace the
document without the possibility of auditing it. An evaluator can say
25


“I extracted assumption A from this passage” and retain an alternat‐
ive version if the text is ambiguous. Structuring aims to preserve dif‐
ferences, not force them into familiar vocabulary.
Negation, modality and quantification are especially important. “May
improve” is not equivalent to “improves in every case.” “There exists a
parameter set” is not equivalent to “for every parameter set.” “Under
the independence assumption” is not a stylistic note. These elements
can be short in the text and large in their consequences. A summary
that retains the nouns but loses logical operators can remain themat‐
ically correct and semantically useless for novelty.
A contribution structure can be viewed as a small graph. Nodes rep‐
resent claims, assumptions, results and sources. Edges can indicate
dependence, justification, contradiction or the transformation of an
assumption. The graph must not be confused with a proof: linking two
nodes with a “justifies” edge does not certify the relation’s validity. It
does, however, provide precise places where a verifier or expert can
intervene.
This representation enables better questions than “is the idea new?”
What guarantee is absent from the prior work? What assumption has
been removed? What observation would distinguish the mechanisms?
What claim is merely a reformulation? What part of the contribution
depends on an unverified assumption? Each answer can be evaluated
separately. We do not automatically obtain truth, but we obtain a bet‐
ter surface for correction.
4.2. Equivalence, Anticipation and Residual
Difference
Equivalence is a stronger relation than resemblance. Two objects can
resemble one another without being equivalent; they can be equival‐
ent under one criterion and different under another. For functions on
a finite domain, extensional equivalence means identical outputs for
every input in that domain. For scientific papers, the criterion can be
much more complex. A small distance in vector space therefore can‐
not be treated directly as proof of conceptual equivalence.
26


We will use x equivalent_C y to denote that two contributions are
equivalent under criterion C. If the relation is genuinely an equival‐
ence relation, it must be reflexive, symmetric and transitive. Many
similarity scores do not satisfy these requirements when thresholded.
We can have A similar to B and B similar to C without A being similar
to C. Constructing classes of ideas through the transitive closure of
such a relation can group together contributions that should not have
been confused.
Anticipation is often asymmetric. Prior work can already contain all
the relevant elements of the candidate while also including additional
results. In this case, the candidate need not be identical to the prior
work for its novelty claim to be weakened. We can represent the rela‐
tion through inclusion of relevant content, provided we specify what
content means and which inference rules are permitted.
Here, Ant_C(k,x) means that prior work k anticipates contribution x
under criterion C. This is a working definition in a closed world, not a
legal definition of novelty or a guarantee concerning the entire his‐
tory of ideas. In real applications, several sources may jointly contrib‐
ute to what appears new in a candidate. If we allow any combination
of prior work, we must establish when the combination is considered
already available and when constructing it is itself the contribution.
Without such a rule, the evaluator can declare every idea “a combina‐
tion of known things,” which no longer discriminates anything.
A useful concept is residual difference. After identifying the closest
prior work, we extract what remains unanticipated under the stated
criterion. The difference can be empty, clear or uncertain. It may con‐
sist of a removed condition, an introduced type of object or a new re‐
lation between old components. “Residual” does not mean minor; it
means what remains after an explicit comparison.
A constructed example: prior work A solves problem P for acyclic in‐
puts. Prior work B handles cycles, but only in a restricted case. Can‐
didate C claims a solution for all inputs in a larger class. A summary
saying “combines A and B” is insufficient to deny novelty. We must
show either that applying the prior methods together already pro‐
27


duces C’s guarantee or that C does not actually deliver its claimed
guarantee. In the first case, the novelty claim is anticipated; in the
second, validity is challenged. These are different arguments.
An instructive difficulty arises in formal mathematics. If we define
knowledge as all the logical consequences of accepted axioms, every
provable theorem was already implicitly contained in the axioms. No
new proof would then expand the set of consequences. This definition
may be logically useful, but it does not describe the epistemic progress
of a resource-limited agent well. Having an implicit consequence is
not the same as having identified or proved it within a practical time.
We can introduce a resource-dependent closure: the consequences
that specified procedures can obtain from K within budget B. A new
proof can expand what is accessible even if it does not expand ideal
logical consequence. This idea must be used cautiously because the
result depends on the procedure and representation. Nevertheless, it
reveals a type of novelty that simple semantic inclusion misses: turn‐
ing an abstract possibility into effectively usable knowledge.
In computer science, the same phenomenon occurs when a compila‐
tion method turns a correct but impractical computation into an effi‐
cient one. The computed function can remain identical while the con‐
tribution is substantial. A detector identifying only new functions
would miss it by definition, not through lack of intelligence. The cri‐
terion must therefore be chosen according to the type of progress we
want to observe.
4.3. When the Idea Changes the Very Vocabulary of
Comparison
The most difficult situation is not always a new value in a known field.
Sometimes the contribution challenges the fields. Instead of proposing
a better mechanism for the accepted problem, it redefines the prob‐
lem. Instead of optimizing a guarantee, it suggests that the guarantee
is unsuitable. Instead of adding a category, it changes the criterion by
which categories were constructed. An evaluator requiring all ideas to
conform to the same scheme can miss precisely this type of change.
28


It does not follow that every rejection of existing vocabulary is pro‐
found. Some reformulations produce no additional predictions, opera‐
tions or clarifications. Others conceal incoherence. The real difficulty
is that the novelty test must allow the criterion to be challenged
without abandoning verification. The solution proposed here is evalu‐
ation at two levels: comparison within the current framework and
reasoned examination of a change in framework.
At the first level, the system can say that the proposal introduces no
new function under the existing criterion. At the second, it can ask
whether the proposal exposes a limitation of the criterion, offers ex‐
amples the framework cannot distinguish and introduces an alternat‐
ive with verifiable consequences. A change of framework becomes
more than a metaphor when it shows what can be observed or done
differently. Conceptual originality is thus neither reduced to lexical
distance nor exempted from the obligation to produce intelligible dif‐
ferences.
A constructed example for programmers is a change in the cost mod‐
el. Two algorithms may be equivalent if we count only arithmetic op‐
erations but different if data transfer is the dominant cost. Proposing
another cost model does not change the computed function; it can,
however, change the optimization question and the ordering of pre‐
ferred solutions. The evaluator must distinguish a merely convenient
change of metric from a change justified by application conditions.
Another example concerns privacy. If the system describes two pro‐
cedures only through accuracy and time, it cannot evaluate their dif‐
ference in information disclosure. Introducing an explicit adversary
model can show that the old representation was insufficient. We need
not claim that every new adversarial model is historically original.
The example shows only that novelty can consist in adding a relevant
dimension, not a new value on old dimensions.
In an automated system, an ontology change must be managed
without retroactively rewriting the data. One solution is to preserve
multiple interpretations and a translation relation between them. The
contribution remains linked to its original formulation, and the evalu‐
ator can compare results under alternative criteria. If all interpreta‐
tions are compressed into one at the outset, the possibility of revision
29


disappears. If they are retained without limit, the cost becomes uncon‐
trollable. The problem is how to allocate the budget among plausible
interpretations.
An important research direction appears here: detecting representa‐
tional insufficiency before detecting novelty. A system might observe
that two texts are very close in the current representation yet contain
incompatible claims or explicit differences the scheme cannot encode.
Instead of declaring them identical, it could request an expanded
scheme or human verification. This is an architectural proposal, not a
capability demonstrated in this book.
Such a mechanism must also be tested against misuse. If every lexical
difference triggers a new ontology, the system becomes useless. The
escalation criterion should depend on a concrete question to which
the interpretations give different answers. In this way, representation‐
al change remains connected to consequences rather than a vague im‐
pression of depth.
This chapter closes with a warning and an opportunity. We cannot de‐
tect every type of novelty in a space that has removed its constitutive
differences from the outset. But we can build systems that declare
their criteria, preserve sources and allow the representation to be re‐
vised. Such discipline does not promise infallible recognition of con‐
ceptual revolutions. It at least prevents confusing the limits of a
scheme with the limits of what can be new.

Chapter 5. How Familiarity Can
Become a Substitute for Novelty
5.1. Convenient Cues and Evaluation Shortcuts
Suppose that in an evaluator’s data, contributions regarded as original
are more often presented with a particular structure: an important
problem, a limitation of previous work, a new mechanism and con‐
vincing results. The structure may be good communication practice.
But it can also become a statistical cue for the label “original.” A mod‐
el learning the cue can often succeed without actually comparing the
mechanism with prior work. When the cue and the truth diverge, per‐
formance may deteriorate.
This is a hypothetical shortcut-learning mechanism. It does not imply
that rhetorical structure is useless or that the model uses no content at
all. Most likely, combinations of cues must be investigated in a con‐
crete application. A score can depend partly on the real difference and
partly on fluency, vocabulary, length, authority or fit with a familiar
domain. The experimental question is how much each factor contrib‐
utes and under what conditions one substitutes for another.
The rhetoric of novelty can be separated from the stated novelty. In a
controlled pair, the same content can be presented as “we introduce a
revolutionary framework” or “the procedure differs from prior work
in the following condition.” If the score changes without the claims
changing, there is sensitivity to packaging. This sensitivity should not
immediately be called an effect of training frequency. It may come
from instructions, post-training, implicit clarity criteria or stylistic
preferences. Identifying the cause requires further interventions.
Another cue is terminological density. A proposal combining fashion‐
able terms can seem more advanced than a plain explanation of the
same operation. But removing all technical terms is not a good solu‐
tion: some are necessary for precision. The experimental manipula‐
tion must preserve information, not merely the general impression.
31


Two versions declared “equivalent” by the very LLM being evaluated
may contain subtle differences. The manipulation’s equivalence must
be independently checked or constructed in a formal domain.
Familiarity can also work in reverse. An unusual text may receive a
high score precisely because it seems different. A superficial evaluator
may confuse strangeness with originality, not only originality with fa‐
miliarity. The hypothesis must be formulated to allow this possibility.
There is no single mandatory direction of bias. A model may prefer fa‐
miliar clarity in one context and extravagance in another, depending
on what it has learned is meant by “creative.”
Source prestige is another distinct variable. If the author’s or institu‐
tion’s name changes the verdict for the same text, we have an attribu‐
tion effect. This is not identical to the effect of an idea’s frequency. An
author can be familiar without the mechanism being old. A model
may use the name as a prior about probable quality, and this prior
can be reasonable for some tasks and unsuitable for anonymous nov‐
elty evaluation. The evaluation contract must state whether such in‐
formation is admissible.
The problem of convenient cues is not ontologically specific to artifi‐
cial intelligence. Any limited evaluator can use heuristics. Automa‐
tion’s practical difference is that the same heuristic can be applied at
scale with a uniform style of justification. This statement is a deduc‐
tion about multiplying a decision rule, not an empirical estimate of
human communities’ behavior. Comparing humans and models re‐
quires the same texts, the same sources and the same verification re‐
sources.
An important consequence is that robustness to reformulation should
not be measured only on comfortable paraphrases. If all variants are
produced by the same model in the same style, the test may confirm
invariance over a very narrow space. Technical, neutral, minimal and
explanatory variants are needed, along with controlled changes in or‐
der and terminology. At the same time, we must not turn resilience to
defective prose into the definition of novelty. A detector may legitim‐
ately need clarification.

5.2. Semantic Smoothing: When Interpretation
Repairs the Very Exception
We will use the term semantic smoothing for a family of transforma‐
tions that reduce variation between texts. Some remove irrelevant
variations, such as the order of equivalent explanations. Others nor‐
malize terminology. Still others choose the most probable interpreta‐
tion of an ambiguous passage. Smoothing is not a defect by definition.
Without certain invariances, the system would mistake every para‐
phrase for a new contribution.
The risk arises when the system smooths a relevant variation. A text
claims that the mechanism works without a usual condition. Because
this claim is unusual, the model may interpret it as an omission or im‐
precise formulation and reintroduce the condition in the summary.
The result becomes more plausible but less faithful. The very claim to
be evaluated has been transformed into a conventional version.
We can describe this phenomenon as a competition between fidelity
and the plausibility prior. When interpreting noisy text, choosing the
probable meaning is often useful. When evaluating an idea claiming
to overcome a limit, the same choice can be dangerous. The system
must distinguish between “the author probably meant something or‐
dinary” and “the author explicitly claims something unusual that
must be checked.” The second preserves the object of criticism; the
first may eliminate it.
A constructed example uses a quantifier. The prior work guarantees
that for every input there is a suitable parameter. The candidate guar‐
antees the existence of a single parameter that works for all inputs.
The two propositions may contain the same nouns and almost the
same words. The order of quantifiers, however, radically changes the
claim. A summary such as “there are parameters that ensure opera‐
tion” can make them indistinguishable. This is not ornamental loss; it
is logical loss.
A second example concerns causality. Prior work reports an associ‐
ation between two variables. The candidate claims that intervening
on one changes the other under certain conditions. A summary saying
“shows a relationship between variables” preserves the topic and re‐
33


moves the difference between association and intervention. The can‐
didate may be wrong in its causal claim. But if the difference is erased,
the system no longer even knows what should be validated.
A third example concerns the domain. Two methods have the same
formula, but one is defined on finite objects while the other claims an
extension to an infinite space. If the representation retains the for‐
mula and ignores the domain, it will overestimate equivalence. Again,
we need not assume that the extension is correct to establish that the
claims differ. Detecting the claimed contribution and validating it
must remain separate.
Smoothing can occur in claim extraction, summarization, translation,
embeddings, keyword selection and search-query reformulation. In a
multistage chain, a small difference can be lost repeatedly. A stronger
final model cannot reliably recover it if all the representations it re‐
ceives are already identical. Evaluating only the final answer can
therefore conceal the error’s origin.
An appropriate test compares three paths. The first preserves the ori‐
ginal text. The second supplies only the compressed representation.
The third supplies the representation and access to the decisive ori‐
ginal passages. If performance falls in the second and recovers in the
third, there is evidence that loss or inaccessibility of intermediate in‐
formation matters. To attribute the decline specifically to a semantic
collision, we must verify that the representation no longer encodes
the difference, not merely that the evaluator finds it harder to use.
An auxiliary task is also useful: after compression, can a verifier re‐
cover the answer to the question that distinguishes the candidate
from prior work? For the quantifier example, the question is whether
the parameter may depend on the input. If the summary does not per‐
mit an answer, we have a direct measure of relevant information loss.
A generic summary-fidelity score can miss this defect because most of
the content was correctly preserved.
Not every detected loss justifies abandoning compression. The cost of
retaining everything must be compared with the value of the differ‐
ence and the frequency of the cases. An architecture can retain sum‐
maries for navigation and sources for verification, include special
fields for assumptions and exceptions, or escalate cases in which two
34


interpretations produce different consequences. The goal is not the
absence of all abstraction, but explicit control of abstractions that af‐
fect the verdict.
5.3. Attraction to Prior Work and Confirmation
Loops
Retrieving related documents seems the natural solution to the nov‐
elty problem. But a system may search through a reformulation that
has already removed the candidate’s difference. It will then retrieve
precisely the familiar literature and confirm its own initial interpreta‐
tion. Not because the literature demonstrates identity, but because the
query excluded what needed investigation. This is a possible architec‐
tural confirmation loop, not an inevitable property of every search en‐
gine.
Suppose the proposal concerns a guarantee without global synchron‐
ization. The generated query becomes “guarantee in synchronized
systems” because that is the familiar category. Retrieved results con‐
tain abundant thematically related prior work. The evaluator con‐
cludes that the idea is well known. The final error looks like a novelty
judgment, but its cause arose in translating the search request. A
provenance log must preserve queries as well as the final documents.
There is also a symmetric risk. If the query uses only the candidate’s
unusual vocabulary, the engine may miss prior work described in oth‐
er terminology. The system declares novelty because it found no lexic‐
al matches. The solution is not to choose literal or semantic search ex‐
clusively, but to maintain several paths: exact terms, synonyms, mech‐
anism, assumptions, relations and consequences. Each path must be
evaluated for its contribution to coverage.
Consensus among several models does not automatically solve the
problem. If all receive the same summary or use the same index, their
errors may be correlated. Diversity of model names does not guaran‐
tee diversity of information or criteria. An ensemble can reduce ran‐
dom variation while reinforcing a shared bias. Demonstrating a bene‐
fit requires measuring error complementarity, not just final agree‐
ment.
35


An adversarial role can be useful: one evaluator searches for prior
work while another tries to construct a case distinguishing the candid‐
ate. Nevertheless, the dispute must not be treated as a magic method
for producing truth. Two agents can generate persuasive but false ar‐
guments. Value arises when arguments contain verifiable objects:
sources, locatable quotations, calculations, counterexamples or expli‐
citly separated assumptions. Debate without witnesses risks being
merely rhetorical competition.
The institutional loop appears when verdicts are reused as data. If ac‐
cepted ideas become positive examples without auditing, the system
may learn its own preferences. If rejected ideas are not retained, there
is no longer a way to estimate what was lost. In such a process, the ab‐
sence of observed errors may reflect a lack of observation rather than
correct selection. Archiving rejections and randomly reevaluating
them are therefore epistemic components, not merely administrative
features.
It should not be inferred that every use of synthetic data inevitably
produces collapse. Studies of recursive training show important de‐
pendencies on whether data are replaced or accumulated. These res‐
ults will be discussed separately. Here the argument is narrower: an
evaluation that becomes its own ground truth can lose its ability to be
corrected. This possibility exists even without retraining if earlier de‐
cisions are treated as authoritative precedents in every new round.
The familiarity hypothesis must therefore extend from the model to
the information circuit. Sometimes the problem is a learned prior. At
other times it is a search query, a compressed representation, an ag‐
gregation rule or a data-retention policy. A responsible system does
not attribute every error to “model hallucination,” but preserves
enough traces to identify the transformation that made the difference
invisible.

Chapter 6. Genuine
Impossibilities, Conditions of
Possibility and Proofs
6.1. Missing Information and the Impossibility of
Absolute Priority
Let us rigorously define an evaluator as a procedure that receives ob‐
servations O and produces a verdict. Observations may include the
candidate document, accessible sources, search-engine responses and
tool outputs. The complete world may also contain inaccessible docu‐
ments. If two worlds produce exactly the same observations for the
evaluator, the procedure cannot distinguish them on the basis of those
observations. This is an elementary identifiability limit.
We construct two worlds. In world W0, contribution x has no prior
work before time t. In world W1, prior work exists in a document the
evaluator cannot access. All accessible documents are identical in the
two worlds. The training history and evaluator state are also identical.
The correct historical verdict differs, but the procedure’s input does
not. A deterministic evaluator will produce the same answer in both
worlds and be wrong in at least one.
Randomization does not remove the problem. A randomized evaluat‐
or will produce the same answer distribution in the two worlds be‐
cause its observations have the same distribution. It cannot have
probability one of being correct in both. If we assign equal probabilit‐
ies to the two worlds, the best verdict without further information
cannot exceed probability one half of identifying the correct world.
With other priors, it can favor the more probable world, but it does
not obtain informational certainty.
This proof does not say that searching for prior work is useless. It says
that an absolute guarantee requires assumptions about completeness
of access. In a finite, fully enumerated library, we can check the ab‐
sence of an element under a decidable criterion. In an open history of
37


knowledge, we can produce a conclusion conditional on the sources
examined. The difference between these situations must be expressed
in the final report, not hidden in the fine print.
The limit applies to humans, search engines and hybrid systems. It is
not a special property of language models. It therefore cannot be used
to demonstrate that LLMs are weaker than humans. A relative com‐
parison must supply the same sources and budget or measure the ac‐
cess advantage separately. Otherwise, competence and information
are confused.
A more modest guarantee can be formulated: “we found no prior
work in K using procedure P, and the procedure’s coverage was evalu‐
ated on a family of similar cases.” This is a verifiable claim. It is not
equivalent to historical absence, but it may suffice for a practical de‐
cision. Confidence depends on the domain, indexing and the proced‐
ure’s ability to find prior work with different vocabulary.
A negative search result has value only relative to search sensitivity. If
the procedure frequently misses known prior work, finding nothing
for the candidate is weakly informative. A useful test involves con‐
cealing the identities of papers in a controlled way and checking
whether they can be retrieved from their contributions. This test does
not prove historical completeness, but estimates a component needed
to interpret the absence of results.
The formal conclusion is narrow and strong: no evaluator can univer‐
sally certify the absence of inaccessible prior work without additional
assumptions. This proposition does not imply that recognizing a new
difference relative to an accessible library is impossible. An unjusti‐
fied move from the first to the second would turn an informational
limit into a denial of comparison.
6.2. The Didactic Theorem of Sufficient
Representation
Consider a set X of objects, a deterministic labeling Y:X→{0,1} and a
representation φ:X→Z. The question is whether there exists a classifier
g:Z→{0,1} that perfectly decides the label using only the representa‐
38


tion. The following result is elementary and is not presented as a new
theorem of this book. It makes the mechanism of difference loss expli‐
cit.
Proposition. A perfect classifier g with Y=g∘φ exists if and only if Y is
constant on every fiber of φ. A fiber is the set of all objects receiving
the same representation.
The proof of necessity is immediate. If two objects have the same rep‐
resentation, g receives the same input and produces the same output.
To be correct on both, their labels must be equal. The proof of suffi‐
ciency constructs g: for each representation z, we choose the common
label of the objects in its fiber. The assumption that the label is con‐
stant guarantees that the definition does not depend on the chosen ob‐
ject. On representations not reached by φ, g can be defined arbitrarily.
The proposition is informational: the existence of the function g does
not guarantee that it is computable or efficient on an infinite domain.
In the finite case, we can construct a decision table. In the general
case, computability remains separate from the information-preserva‐
tion condition.
The consequence for novelty is precise. If a new contribution and an
old one, under the same criterion and reference, map to the same rep‐
resentation, no evaluator restricted exclusively to that representation
can be perfect on both. It does not matter how fluent it is, how many
parameters it has or how long it reflects. It may guess better using pre‐
valences, but it cannot recover the lost distinction from an identical
signal.
There is, however, an often omitted condition: mere noninjectivity of
the representation does not demonstrate failure. A representation can
group many paraphrased texts, all with the same label, without losing
anything relevant to the task. If φ removes only irrelevant variations,
classification can remain perfect. The problem is not that two differ‐
ent texts have the same vector. It is that two texts with different labels
provide the same information for the decision.
39


Moreover, the criterion and library must be included in the total in‐
put. The same contribution may be new relative to K1 and old relative
to K2. A candidate representation, however good, is insufficient if the
evaluator does not receive the reference. We can define the total ob‐
ject as u=(x,K,C). The theorem applies to the representation of u. This
clarification prevents a common error: treating novelty as a fixed la‐
bel of a text, independent of context.
For a finite example, suppose we have four equally probable objects.
The first two are old, the next two new. Representation Z has two val‐
ues: one groups the first old object with the first new object, while the
other groups the remaining two. In each fiber, the labels are equally
mixed. Any classifier based only on Z is wrong on half the objects. If
the representation changes so that each fiber contains only objects
with the same label, the minimum error becomes zero, even though
the representation remains compressed.
In the discrete probabilistic case, the minimum Bayes risk for binary
classification from Z is the sum, over representations, of the minority
class mass in each fiber. The formula simultaneously includes repres‐
entational loss and any label noise.
For equal class priors, the same bound can be expressed through the
total variation distance between the class-conditional representation
distributions. Total variation measures how different the distributions
are; if they are identical, there is no discriminative signal.
These formulas do not allow automatic estimation of an LLM’s error
from an arbitrary vector. The relevant distributions must be known or
estimated, and internal representations are generally complex. They
do, however, offer a conceptual target for experiments: we seek trans‐
formations that reduce label separability, not merely text length. We
can measure this reduction directly in controlled finite worlds.
A corollary concerns the representation budget. With B bits, we can
distinguish at most 2 to the power B states. If the family of future
questions requires distinguishing more relevant classes, some must be
40


merged. But a single binary question may require much less informa‐
tion than full reconstruction of the document. There is therefore no
simple relation of the form “short summary, hence novelty im‐
possible.” The limit depends on how many distinctions must be pre‐
served for the permitted tasks.
Finally, source access changes the problem. If the evaluator receives Z
and can request passages from X, it is no longer limited to Z. The the‐
orem does not prohibit recovering the difference through a new ob‐
servation. This observation underpins the architecture proposed later:
compression for navigation can be combined with verifiable access to
the original for decisions that depend on details.
6.3. Computability, Generalization and Constructive
Counterexamples
Some novelty limits arise from computation rather than missing docu‐
ments. If ideas are arbitrary programs and the criterion requires be‐
havioral equivalence for all inputs, we enter general problems of pro‐
gram semantics. Rice-type results concern the impossibility of total al‐
gorithmic decision for nontrivial semantic properties of partial com‐
putable functions. The paper by Hemaspaandra and Rothe explicitly
discusses the difference between such results and complexity ana‐
logues for restricted classes. [R25]
Delimitation is essential. Undecidable does not mean that no instance
can be solved. It means there is no algorithm that halts and correctly
decides every instance in the general class. Many concrete programs
can be compared, many properties can be proved and restricted
classes may be decidable. Invoking computability to declare every
novelty detector useless would be an invalid extension of the result.
We can see the difficulty through a simple construction. From an ar‐
bitrary program M and an input w, we construct program P, which
simulates M(w) and returns zero if the simulation terminates. If it
does not terminate, P produces no result. A universal evaluator able to
decide whether P is equivalent to the constant-zero function on all in‐
puts would decide whether M(w) halts. Thus, a general equivalence
41


question can conceal the halting problem. The construction explains
the limit without claiming that all novelty tasks have the same diffi‐
culty.
For Boolean circuits with a finite number of inputs, the situation dif‐
fers. We can evaluate all 2 to the power n inputs and compare truth
tables. The procedure always halts. The cost may be high, but the
problem is decidable. If the library is finite, we can check whether the
candidate’s function coincides with any stored function. This is a con‐
structive counterexample to the claim that relative novelty detection
is impossible in principle.
The system must also be distinguished from its linguistic component.
An LLM can translate a description into a circuit, and an exact verifier
can decide equivalence. Success of the whole system shows that LLMs
can participate usefully in novelty detection, not that their translation
is infallible or that the model alone proved everything. We can evalu‐
ate translation fidelity and verifier correctness separately. This separ‐
ation makes the experiment more informative than a monolithic ver‐
dict.
There is also a simple counterargument to the thesis that a model can
recognize only objects it has encountered. A comparison rule learned
on old pairs can be applied to pairs generated later. If a model cor‐
rectly reads truth tables and identifies a different row, that exact pair
need not have existed in training. We are not claiming that every LLM
does this robustly; we are saying that learning’s historical origin is not
a proof of impossibility.
No-free-lunch results are often invoked too broadly. In a world where
unknown labels are arbitrary and independent of previous observa‐
tions, no rule can systematically generalize better than another on av‐
erage over all labelings. But real problems are not uniformly distrib‐
uted over all possible functions. They have structure, regularities and
criteria. The correct conclusion is that generalization requires induct‐
ive assumptions, not that generalization is impossible.
For novelty, the desired inductive assumption is not “the future al‐
ways resembles the past.” It may be “the comparison rule remains val‐
id for new objects.” A circuit comparator does not assume that the
new circuit is identical to an old one; it assumes that gate semantics
42


and the input domain are stable. This difference between regularity of
procedure and regularity of outcome is central. A system can use
known rules to identify an unknown difference.
A clear map of limits emerges. Without information about inaccess‐
ible prior work, absolute priority is not identifiable. After a relevant
distinction is lost, perfect classification from the remaining represent‐
ation is impossible. For general programs, certain semantic comparis‐
ons are undecidable. Conversely, in finite worlds with explicit criteria,
relative novelty can be decided exactly. The empirical question about
LLMs begins between these limits: how well do they translate, com‐
pare, search and preserve information in tasks where a correct ver‐
dict is possible?

Chapter 7. What the Literature
Shows and What It Cannot
Demonstrate
7.1. Familiarity, Self-Evaluation and Perceived
Creativity
The study Self-Preference Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge by Wataoka, Taka‐
hashi and Ri investigates evaluators’ preference for answers familiar
from the model’s perspective. The authors report an association
between lower perplexity and more favorable evaluations, more pro‐
nounced than in human judgments, including beyond the text’s
merely originating from the same model. The result supports investig‐
ating familiarity as an evaluation factor. It does not directly demon‐
strate rejection of radically new scientific ideas or by itself identify
pretraining frequency as the sole cause. [R07]
This interpretive limit matters. Preference for an easily anticipated
answer may reflect clarity or correctness in some tasks. To demon‐
strate a bias against novelty, we need cases in which familiarity
changes while quality and conceptual difference remain controlled. A
result about an evaluator’s overall preference is one piece of the argu‐
ment, not the entire argument.
In their paper on MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena, Zheng and colleagues
examine LLM evaluation and discuss position, verbosity and self-fa‐
voring effects alongside reasoning limitations. The study shows both
the practical usefulness of evaluators in the tested configurations and
the need to control certain biases. High agreement with human judges
on a conversational benchmark cannot automatically be transferred
to historical priority or proof evaluation. [R08]
An experimental design can use these results as a starting point
without exaggerating them. If answer order changes preference in a
comparison, the order of candidates and prior work must be random‐
ized. If length affects the score, it must be controlled separately from
44


information completeness. If the model favors its own style, comparis‐
ons between human- and model-generated ideas must separate con‐
tent from the source or form of writing.
Si, Yang and Hashimoto conducted a study involving over a hundred
NLP researchers in which LLM-assisted ideas were rated as newer
than human ideas in the blind human evaluation used, with a result
reported as statistically significant. The authors also discuss limita‐
tions involving feasibility, diversity and self-evaluation. The result
contradicts a simplistic version of the claim that models can produce
only texts perceived as banal. It does not establish the historical prior‐
ity of all the ideas and is not a direct test of novelty-detection ability.
[R09]
A major methodological distinction lies here: an idea can be judged
original in a blind reading and still have obscure prior work. This is
not a contradiction between the results of the two procedures. They
measure different operations. Evaluating the impression of novelty
may be relevant to communication or initial selection; checking prior
work is necessary for stronger claims.
Gupta and Pruthi examined fifty AI-generated research documents
with thirteen experts. In their sample, 24% were identified as para‐
phrases of or substantial borrowing from existing papers, with addi‐
tional checks of the identified links. The study illustrates the differ‐
ence between an original appearance and explicit examination of pri‐
or work. The percentage must not be extrapolated to all systems or
generation types. Substantial similarity does not, by itself, permit in‐
ferring a psychological intention to defraud. [R10]
Read together, these results do not impose a single verdict on “AI cre‐
ativity.” They show that generation, perceived originality, retrieval of
prior work and allocation of credit must be evaluated separately. A
book about novelty detection would be wrong to use generation suc‐
cess as evidence of a good detector or a case of borrowing as proof
that every generated idea is derivative in the same sense.

7.2. Novelty Benchmarks and the Ground-Truth
Problem
RINoBench, presented by Schopf and Färber in 2026, contains 1,381
research ideas and tools for evaluating scores and justifications. The
consulted version is listed as accepted at LREC 2026. The authors re‐
port that resemblance between model-produced justifications and ref‐
erence reasoning does not ensure agreement on scores. Dataset con‐
struction uses OpenReview evaluations, filtering of certain disagree‐
ments and LLM processing of ideas and justifications. The original di‐
mensions combine novelty with technical or empirical significance.
These choices matter for interpreting the measured construct. [R11]
NovBench, by Wu and colleagues, contains 1,684 paper–review pairs
and is associated with ACL 2026 in the metadata of the consulted ver‐
sion. It includes novelty descriptions extracted from introductions and
expert evaluations. The framework examines relevance, correctness,
coverage and clarity of generated evaluations. The authors report lim‐
itations in the tested models’ understanding of novelty and instruc‐
tion-following difficulties in some fine-tuned models. Interpretation
must take into account that an introduction does not necessarily con‐
tain all the details needed to compare the contribution. [R12]
Liu and colleagues propose an approach combining conceptual de‐
scriptions of contributions, retrieval of prior work and evaluation by
language models. On their novelty-detection benchmarks, they report
improvements from idea-adapted retrieval. The result is evidence of
practical possibility in a specific configuration, not universal certifica‐
tion of originality. It supports evaluating the entire access-and-com‐
parison procedure instead of assuming that the model must decide
from parametric memory alone. [R13]
The general problem with any such benchmark is separating the
available label from the target property. A review can combine origin‐
ality with importance, clarity or credibility. A score may represent an
expert’s impression after limited reading, not an exhaustive search for
prior work. A dataset can remain very useful even if it does not meas‐
ure absolute historical priority, provided this limit is explicit.
46


Another risk arises when the evaluated object is itself produced
through automated reformulation. If one model extracts the contribu‐
tion from the paper, another model judges the extracted representa‐
tion. A failure may originate in extraction, while success may reflect
conformity to the extractor’s style. This is a general methodological
observation, not proof that any particular benchmark is invalid. Es‐
timating the effect requires comparisons between originals, human
extractions and automated extractions, retaining the same cases.
Filtering disagreement can improve label consistency but change the
population studied. If we eliminate cases on which experts disagree,
we evaluate a more consensual subset. We do not automatically know
how the system would perform on ideas challenging a framework or
requiring unusual expertise. A benchmark can have a consensual lay‐
er for stable comparisons and a contested layer for studying limits.
Both are informative, but they answer different questions.
Evaluator circularity must also be avoided. If an LLM produces the
reference justification, another LLM scores the candidate justification
and the main criterion is semantic resemblance between them, the
result may reflect a shared stylistic culture. This risk does not prohibit
automated evaluation. It calls for independent checks: concrete prior
work, verifiable differences, expert-analyzed subsets and measure‐
ment of the automated evaluator’s own errors.
A strong benchmark should publish not just scores but each case’s
genealogy: the original document, reference time, transformations,
criterion, decisive prior work and reasons for disagreement. Without
this genealogy, scores are hard to interpret when models differ. A sys‐
tem may perform worse because it detects a real ambiguity concealed
by the single label.
7.3. Related Evidence and the Limits of Transfer
between Domains
Research on detecting out-of-distribution observations provides a rel‐
evant warning, although it is not identical to our problem. Nalisnick
and colleagues show that certain generative models can assign high
likelihood to data from a distribution different from the training dis‐
tribution. Ren and colleagues investigate likelihood ratios to control
47


effects of background statistics. These results weaken the naive identi‐
fication of probability density with semantic membership. They do
not turn out-of-distribution detection into a detector of scientific con‐
tributions. [R17, R21]
The conceptual difference matters. An idea can be new and formu‐
lated in perfectly ordinary language, thus not appearing outside the
linguistic distribution. A text can be out of distribution because of lan‐
guage or formatting while describing an old idea. A system detecting
anomalies may help route cases for verification, but cannot automat‐
ically call an anomaly a “discovery.”
Uzzi and colleagues analyzed reference combinations in scientific lit‐
erature and observed an association between citation impact and
combinations blending a conventional foundation with atypical ele‐
ments. The study offers a perspective on the relationship between fa‐
miliarity and the unusual in science. It does not prove that a particu‐
lar combination is true, original or causally responsible for impact.
Citations are not identical to epistemic value. [R22]
For a detector’s architecture, the lesson is not to mechanically reward
rare combinations. A combination may be rare because it is unsuit‐
able. Nor should we assume that a new contribution must be strange
in every component. A new mechanism may need conventional
presentation to be verifiable. Tests must allow a mixture of familiar
foundation and local difference, not just an artificial opposition
between banality and extravagance.
Shumailov and colleagues studied model degradation under recursive
training on generated data and the loss of aspects of the original dis‐
tribution. Gerstgrasser and colleagues show that accumulating real
and synthetic data can avoid certain forms of collapse observed when
data are replaced. The results do not authorize the conclusion that
every use of synthetic data destroys diversity. They require specifying
the data-recycling mechanism and continued access to external obser‐
vations. [R23, R24]
In novelty evaluation, an analogous scenario can be formulated but
must be tested separately. If only accepted ideas return as positive ex‐
amples while rejected ones disappear, the visible distribution may
narrow. However, the effect depends on selection policy, external
48


ideas and archive preservation. It must not be presented as an inevit‐
able consequence of the language model. It is a hypothesis about the
decision ecosystem.
The balance of the literature is therefore asymmetric. There is evid‐
ence that LLM judges can have biases, that novelty evaluation re‐
mains difficult and that clear explanations do not guarantee correct
comparisons. There are also results showing the usefulness of assisted
generation and retrieval. Neither group demonstrates a universal
thesis about all models and criteria. What the literature justifies is a
more demanding validation program, not a metaphysical verdict.

Chapter 8. How to Identify a
Familiarity Bias Experimentally
8.1. Hypotheses, Interventions and Alternative
Explanations
A good experiment begins by defining the claim that could be wrong.
“Models prefer the familiar” is too broad unless we specify familiarity
and the task. We can define a behavioral hypothesis: for the same con‐
tribution and prior work, conventional wording receives a higher
novelty score than unusual wording under conditions of comparable
clarity. We can define an informational hypothesis: a particular com‐
pression stage reduces recovery of the decisive semantic difference.
We can define a causal hypothesis about training: increasing exposure
to a contribution pattern, with other factors controlled, changes its
evaluation independently of genuine novelty.
These hypotheses require different experiments. Reformulating the
prompt does not intervene on pretraining. Comparing two commer‐
cial models does not isolate data frequency because the models may
differ in architecture, capacity, post-training and tools. Observing a
correlation between perplexity and score does not separate clarity,
correctness and familiarity. A study must declare the level at which it
claims a conclusion.
We can informally represent a causal mechanism through variables. F
is training exposure, S style, N reference novelty, D semantic difficulty,
R access to prior work, Z the intermediate representation and J the
verdict. F can influence both Z and J. S can influence difficulty and
perceived quality. N is not necessarily observed directly; sometimes
we have only an expert label Y influenced by other factors as well. An
experiment ignoring these relationships can attribute to novelty what
belongs to style or difficulty.
Factorial design allows factors to vary independently. We can combine
old and new contributions with familiar and unusual formulations,
then with prior work absent, automatically retrieved or supplied by
50


an experimental oracle. In another block, we can compare full text
with a summary. We need not run every combination immediately.
But we must understand what interactions might change the result. A
style effect may appear only when prior work is absent; that is a dif‐
ferent problem from a bias persisting with complete information.
The experimental oracle is not an omniscient agent. It is the condition
in which the experimenter supplies exactly the decisive prior work
and the information needed for comparison. In a finite world, this in‐
formation can be checked exhaustively. In a real study, “oracle” must
be used more modestly: an expert-adjudicated package of prior work.
The condition’s role is to separate search failure from comparison fail‐
ure.
A simple causal contrast is the difference between scores for the same
idea under two styles, with random assignment of order and in‐
stances. For idea i, we can denote potential outcomes J_i(1) and J_i(0),
where 1 and 0 indicate the stylistic treatment. The target average ef‐
fect is the mean difference. The definition does not automatically re‐
solve identification: memory effects between evaluations must be
avoided and content equivalence ensured.
In practice, the same conversation session can create dependencies
between conditions. If the model has already seen the original ver‐
sion, evaluation of the paraphrase is no longer independent. We can
use separate sessions and randomization. If an interface retains
memory or dynamically changes the model, these conditions must be
documented. A repeatable experiment needs the version, date, para‐
meters and complete input–output log.
Hypotheses must be accompanied by criteria for local refutation. If
the style effect is smaller than a preregistered practical threshold with
sufficiently small uncertainty, the hypothesis of a relevant effect in
that configuration is weakened. A nonsignificant result in a small
sample is not a refutation. Likewise, a significant but tiny effect may
have little importance for selection. Effect size and decision cost must
be discussed together.

8.2. Measurement: From Scores to Recovered
Contributions
Overall accuracy is insufficient when novelties are rare. A classifier
calling every idea “old” can have high accuracy and no usefulness for
discovery. Saito and Rehmsmeier discuss why precision–recall curves
are often more informative than ROC curves for evaluation on imbal‐
anced classes. In our application, metric choice must be explicitly
linked to the filtering objective. [R19]
Recall measures the proportion of genuinely new contributions re‐
tained. Precision measures the proportion of selections that are genu‐
inely new. They express different costs. For a team able to examine
twenty proposals a week, the practical question may be how many
new contributions are in the top twenty. For an alert system, missing
almost no cases may matter even with many false alarms. There is no
universally optimal threshold.
An appropriate metric is recall at a fixed budget. For each method, we
allow the same number of candidates to be sent to an expert. We com‐
pare how many novelties are recovered. We can add the total cost of
tokens, searches and expert time. A system detecting five percent
more contributions but consuming ten times more verification may be
better or worse depending on the application. Cost must not be hidden
behind a single curve.
The quality of the identified difference must be measured separately.
The model can correctly select a candidate for a false reason. We can
evaluate whether the cited passage exists, whether the prior work is
relevant, whether the anticipation relation is valid and whether the
difference witness works. A correct selection with a false explanation
is not equivalent to an evidence-supported selection, especially when
other systems will reuse the result.
Abstention must be handled explicitly. An evaluator may answer “in‐
sufficient information.” If we count every abstention as an error, we
favor systems that guess. If we exclude it completely, a system can ab‐
stain on every difficult case and report artificially high precision. Cov‐
erage, risk on decided cases and the cost of escalated cases must be re‐
ported together. A good selective system allocates its uncertainty use‐
fully rather than hiding it.
52


Calibration can be evaluated by comparing probabilities with ob‐
served frequencies. But good average calibration can conceal large er‐
rors on rare contributions, underrepresented languages or ideas
changing assumptions. Analysis of subgroups defined before the test
is necessary. We should not create dozens of subgroups after seeing
the results and report only those supporting the preferred hypothesis.
Conformal prediction methods can offer marginal guarantees under
assumptions such as data exchangeability. The introduction by An‐
gelopoulos and Bates explains these mechanisms and limits. In a
stream of discoveries, the distribution may change precisely because
new contributions appear. A guarantee calibrated on the past must
therefore not be presented as individual certification of historical nov‐
elty or automatic protection under distribution shift. [R20]
The statistical unit must be chosen correctly. Ten paraphrases of the
same idea are not ten independent discoveries. A hundred runs of the
same model on the same pair are not a hundred replications of the
training effect. The data have hierarchical structure: problem famil‐
ies, ideas, variants, models and seeds. Confidence intervals must re‐
flect this dependence through idea-level aggregation, cluster bootstrap
or suitable hierarchical models.
One possible statistical model, presented here as a planning tool, uses
logistic regression for verdict correctness, with effects of novelty,
style, access and compression, plus preregistered interactions and
random intercepts for family and idea. The model does not turn ob‐
servation into causality. Causal interpretation comes from the inter‐
vention design; regression estimates contrasts and uncertainty under
its assumptions.
8.3. Testing Training Frequency and Statistical
Power
Testing the initial hypothesis’s exact explanation requires control of
exposure. We can construct an artificial domain where certain contri‐
bution forms are presented as innovative more often and others less
often. In the test, novelty is determined independently of these fre‐
53


quencies. If evaluations follow the frequency of the “innovative” label
despite the controlled truth, we have evidence for the proposed causal
mechanism in that regime.
Two manipulations must be separated. The first changes the fre‐
quency of an idea’s content. The second changes how frequently an
idea form is associated with an assessment of novelty. These can have
opposite effects. Repeating an idea can help the model recognize it as
old. Repeating praise for a pattern can make the model regard it as in‐
novative. The initial hypothesis is more interesting in the second form
because it concerns learning an evaluative concept, not merely mem‐
orizing content.
Total token count, optimization steps, difficulty distribution and label
proportions must be controlled. Otherwise, a higher-exposure condi‐
tion may simply receive more useful training. We can redistribute the
same resources among categories and use multiple independent ini‐
tializations. Comparing a single pair of models does not allow the ef‐
fect to be robustly separated from optimization variation.
Small models or local adaptations can provide an accessible causal
laboratory. A result in such a laboratory must not automatically be
transferred to the pretraining of a very large model. It can demon‐
strate the mechanism’s existence and the conditions in which it ap‐
pears. The next step is testing transfer to different capacities, object‐
ives and data regimes. This progression is stronger than attempting to
deduce an opaque system’s training data from a few answers.
Sample planning must start from the minimum relevant effect. For a
paired contrast between two styles, information comes especially
from cases where verdicts differ. If almost all pairs produce the same
verdict, a larger sample may be needed to estimate a small difference.
For ordinal scores, we must decide whether distances between grades
are treated as equal or whether an ordinal model is used. The choice
must not be made after seeing which analysis produces the desired
result.
A power simulation is useful if the generating assumptions are expli‐
cit. We can assume particular error rates and correlations between
paraphrases, generate synthetic data and estimate how often the pro‐
cedure would detect an effect. These results concern statistical design
54


under the chosen assumptions, not actual LLM performance. The
book does not turn such planning simulations into empirical evidence
for the familiarity hypothesis.
Preregistration must fix the primary outcome, contrasts, relevance
threshold, exclusions, handling of abstentions and rules for explorat‐
ory analyses. It should not prohibit unexpected discoveries. It should
separate confirmation of a prior prediction from formulation of a new
one after observing the data. An interesting finding discovered later
remains valuable but needs independent replication before being
presented as confirmation.
Ultimately, the hypothesis should be evaluated through a profile of
results. A style effect without a controlled-exposure effect supports a
behavioral bias, not its training explanation. A compression effect that
disappears with access to the original supports a remediable informa‐
tional mechanism. Persistent failure with perfect prior work and a
simple criterion indicates a comparison limit in the tested configura‐
tion. Robust success in the finite world refutes universal impossibility
without automatically certifying competence in open science.

Chapter 9. The Controlled
Laboratory: Novelty with Exact
Ground Truth
9.1. Why Finite Circuits Are a Suitable Laboratory
To test a detector, we must know what it should have detected. In open
science, this condition is difficult: prior work may be missing, experts
may have different criteria and the contribution may not yet be valid‐
ated. A finite laboratory does not replace reality, but it allows certain
mechanisms to be isolated. Boolean circuits are suitable because they
have exact semantics, can be reformulated in many ways and can dif‐
fer on a single case.
A Boolean circuit takes n binary inputs and produces a binary output.
AND, OR, NOT and XOR gates have determinate semantics. For fixed n,
the circuit’s function can be represented by a table with 2 to the
power n rows. If two circuits have the same table, they are extension‐
ally equivalent on that domain. If the tables differ, at least one input
witnesses the difference.
We define a library K of circuits and a candidate c. Relative functional
novelty is one if c’s function coincides with none of the functions of
elements in K. This definition is deliberately narrow. It does not say
that c has an original implementation, is more efficient or was dis‐
covered for the first time in history. It says that the library contains no
identical function. The advantage is that the verdict can be calculated
exactly, independently of a linguistic evaluator.
A minimal example uses two inputs a and b. The library contains AND
and OR. The candidate “negation of the disjunction of the negations”
is equivalent to AND and must be classified as old despite different
wording. Candidate XOR differs from both and must be classified as
new relative to this library. If we add XOR to the library, the same can‐
didate becomes old. We thus test both invariance to reformulation
and correct dependence on the reference.
56


This example is too easy for a broad evaluation because gate names
may trigger memorized knowledge. We can define operations with
random names and supply their tables locally. We can construct ex‐
pressions with different trees, redundant operations and variable per‐
mutations. We can require the model to use only the definitions in
context. The aim is not to conceal meaning artificially but to eliminate
the possibility of answering solely from a name’s reputation.
The verifier and generator must remain separate. The generator cre‐
ates candidates and variants. The verifier calculates tables and labels.
The LLM produces the verdict or translates the description into a veri‐
fiable representation. If the same model generates the labels and is
then evaluated against them, the laboratory loses the very independ‐
ence that makes it useful. In the executable appendix, labels are calcu‐
lated by exhaustive enumeration without calling a model.
A finite laboratory can also test operational novelty, but the criterion
must change. Two equivalent circuits can have different depths or
gate counts. If the new candidate is smaller than every implementa‐
tion in the library, we have another type of relative novelty. It does
not follow that it is the globally minimal circuit; that would require
exploring a larger space or proving a bound. Every extension of the
criterion brings a verification obligation.

A critical test of the initial hypothesis must include rare differences.

put except one. If inputs are uniform, the difference appears with
probability 2 to the power minus n. For n equal to eight, only one row
out of 256 distinguishes the functions. Functional novelty is exact, but
a small sample of behaviors can miss it.

analysis shows that failure can result from the absence of a distin‐

tion received.
We can compare three conditions. In the first, the model sees only a
sample without the witness. In the second, it sees a sample containing
it. In the third, it receives complete expressions and can reason sym‐
bolically. The first condition does not permit reliable identification of
the difference from examples alone; the second provides local evid‐
ence; the third tests the ability to deduce the difference from struc‐
ture. These conditions must not be aggregated into a single success
rate.
To test smoothing, we can construct a representation that deliberately
removes the distinguishing row or retains only the circuit’s thematic
category. The collision is then known by construction. An evaluator
based exclusively on the compressed representation cannot distin‐
guish the pair. This experiment checks the pipeline’s informational
limit, not a particular LLM’s inability. We can subsequently replace
the deliberate compressor with a linguistic summarizer and measure
how often a relevant collision occurs.
Another family uses reformulations without changing the function.
We can apply double negation, De Morgan’s laws, reordering of com‐
mutative arguments and introduction of redundant subexpressions.
These transformations produce different texts with the same label. If
the model considers them new only because the expression is long or
unusual, we have a false positive for syntactic originality. If it cor‐
rectly normalizes all expressions, we have useful semantic-invariance
competence.
The complementary family changes a single relevant component
while keeping the form as close as possible. Replacing an OR gate with
XOR can produce a local functional difference. Changing the input do‐
main can turn equivalence into nonequivalence. Moving a quantifier
in a description can change the objective. These minimal pairs are
useful because they reduce the chance that the model uses length or
topic as the main cue.
58


One danger of synthetic datasets is the emergence of artifacts. If every
new example has an extra level of parentheses, the model can learn
the parentheses. If every old example is obtained through a single re‐
writing rule, it may recognize the rule without general comparison.
The generator must balance length, structure and vocabulary
between classes. More importantly, the test must be split by latent
transformation families, not merely by randomly dividing nearly
identical examples.
We can reserve a rewriting family exclusively for testing, a set of base
functions exclusively for testing and new names for each round. This
does not guarantee absence of every shortcut, but it makes generaliza‐
tion more demanding. Results must be reported for each family. A
good average can conceal failure precisely on the rare differences or
quantification changes motivating the research.
9.3. What a Positive or Negative Result Would
Demonstrate
Suppose a model correctly classifies new circuits, recognizes old refor‐
mulations and produces valid difference witnesses on families not
used in preparing the test. The result would demonstrate comparison
ability in a finite domain under the experimental conditions. It would
strongly weaken the thesis that every recognition of novelty is merely
retrieval of memorized ideas. It would not demonstrate that the mod‐
el can examine the open scientific literature or assess an idea’s im‐
portance.
If the model succeeds only when given complete tables but not de‐
scriptions, the problem seems connected to interpretation or symbolic
computation. If it succeeds when the witness is supplied but cannot
find it, we separate verification from witness search. If it fails even
when the difference is explicitly highlighted, we can investigate the
instruction, context or ability to apply the criterion. These results are
more informative than simply saying “it does not understand.”
If all models fail after compression has removed the witness and
structural information, we do not have a useful comparison between
models. We have constructed an unidentifiable task. The experiment’s
59


value is to demonstrate the architectural defect. A good detector
should at least recognize insufficient information and abstain. Cer‐
tainty in a verdict under this condition is itself a calibration result.
If style affects the verdict even though the circuit and library are
identical, there is a behavioral effect. Interpreting it requires checking
that the variants describe exactly the same circuit. We can generate
descriptions from a controlled grammar and then include validated
human variants. Increasing linguistic realism reduces formal control;
a progression from controlled language to natural explanations is
therefore useful, rather than abruptly replacing one with the other.
A negative laboratory result does not prove that the model will fail on
every real task. Humans can use analogies and domain knowledge
poorly represented by random circuits. But success on subjectively as‐
sessed real tasks does not cancel formal failure either. The two tests
measure different components. A good research program preserves
the tension between control and relevance instead of choosing only
the favorable test.
For reporting, each case should include the expression or description,
library, criterion, exact label, existing witnesses, stylistic transforma‐
tions and the evaluator’s raw output. If the model proposes an inter‐
mediate circuit, it must be retained separately from the reference cir‐
cuit. A correct answer obtained from an incorrect translation may be
accidental. An incorrect answer from a correct translation indicates
another problem.
The laboratory can be extended to finite automata, regular expres‐
sions on bounded domains, finite Datalog relations or discrete causal
models. Each domain offers different distinctions: temporal behavior,
quantification, dependencies or interventions. Extensions should not
be made merely to add benchmarks. They must test a precise hypo‐
thesis about what kind of difference is lost or recognized.
The laboratory’s ultimate aim is not to proclaim a winner. It is to con‐
struct an anatomy of error. A system missing prior work needs a dif‐
ferent remedy from one that reads it and erases the difference. A sys‐
tem that can verify a witness but not search for one has a different
profile from one that confuses the criterion. Only after this anatomy is
established is scaling to real documents worth discussing.

Chapter 10. Validation on Real
Research
10.1. Temporal Reconstruction and the Limits of
Retrospective Tests
A test on real papers has obvious appeal: the ideas are authentic, the
language rich and the difficulties resemble those encountered in use.
But document authenticity does not guarantee label validity. Evaluat‐
ing novelty at publication requires reconstructing what was available
beforehand, what the paper claimed and under what criterion it was
considered a contribution. A contemporary database can easily mix
documents from before and after the event being evaluated.
The first principle is temporal freezing of the reference corpus. Every
source must have a verifiable version and date. The preprint, final art‐
icle and updated documentation may have different content. If a de‐
cisive passage was added after the reference date, it must not be used
as evidence that the idea was already known. Likewise, a later article
retrospectively explaining the contribution cannot be treated as prior
work.
The second principle is recognizing the limits of anonymization. Re‐
moving names and the title does not necessarily erase a famous pa‐
per’s identity. Its formula, dataset or argument structure may allow
recognition. A model trained after publication may know the historic‐
al outcome of the evaluation. In such a test, we cannot automatically
attribute success to local comparison. We must report possible con‐
tamination and use analyses that reduce it.
One solution is to combine a retrospective study with a prospective
one. In the prospective study, the model and procedure are fixed be‐
fore candidates are collected. Contributions are evaluated before
stable public consensus exists. Subsequently, discovered prior work,
validations and revisions are tracked. This design reduces some forms
of contamination but costs time and does not immediately produce fi‐
nal truth. Real research can remain controversial for years.
61


Prospective candidates must not be selected only from ideas that have
already passed an LLM filter. Otherwise, the test population is condi‐
tioned on the suspected mechanism. An independent sample is
needed, possibly stratified by domain, contribution type and maturity.
Rejected or unclear ideas must be retained in a separate layer rather
than eliminated without a trace. Otherwise, the very cases that could
confirm the hypothesis disappear before evaluation.
A realistic protocol can distinguish claimed novelty, novelty supported
by comparison with prior work and novelty remaining after technical
validation. These states can change over time. A proposal may seem
new and then be anticipated by a source discovered later. It may seem
wrong and later receive a proof. The system must allow the verdict to
be revised without falsifying its history. Evaluated novelty is a ver‐
sioned object, not a property fixed forever at first reading.
10.2. Experts, Disagreement and Verifiable Labels
Experts are indispensable in open domains, but they are not perfect
oracles. A reviewer may know one subfield very well yet miss a con‐
tribution from a neighboring area. They may interpret “new” as “im‐
portant,” penalize lack of validation or use a different definition of the
problem. These possibilities must not be used to relativize every criti‐
cism. They justify a protocol requiring reasons and sources, not just
grades.
A useful expert evaluation begins by extracting the contribution. The
reviewer must specify exactly what is being compared. They then
identify relevant prior work and describe the relation to each item. Fi‐
nally, they separate the verdict on the difference from the verdict on
correctness and value. A justification such as “the idea is incremental”
must be translated into a concrete relation: what already existed and
what remains. Without this translation, agreement between evaluat‐
ors may be merely agreement on an impression.
Disagreement must be classified. Sometimes experts disagree about
the text’s meaning. Sometimes about the existence of prior work.
Sometimes about the difference’s relevance. Sometimes about technic‐

al validity. These disagreements require different remedies: clarifica‐
tion, further search, specifying the criterion or experiment. A single
agreement coefficient does not locate the problem.
We can preserve multiple labels and a reasoned adjudication. Adjudic‐
ation must not erase the initial positions. If newly found prior work
changes the verdict, the log must show the change. For some cases, the
correct label may remain “undetermined under current conditions.”
These cases are not statistical rubbish. They matter for evaluating ab‐
stention and understanding the system’s limits.

10.3. A Longitudinal Study That Can Change the
Conclusions
A realistic program can begin with a small, well-documented retro‐
spective corpus to check the annotation procedure and tools. The next
stage introduces new proposals, blindly evaluated by models and ex‐
perts. The longitudinal stage follows what happens to them: prior
work appears, they are proved, falsified or remain unclear. These
stages have different roles and must not be combined into a single
success score.
The primary outcome could be recovery of contributions later con‐
firmed, at a fixed initial verification budget. But “later confirmed”
must be carefully defined. Publication is not identical to truth, citation
is not identical to novelty and lack of follow-up may reflect lack of re‐
sources. A longitudinal evaluation must track evidence and comparis‐
ons, not merely an idea’s social success.
To avoid circularity, experts assigning later labels should not know the
model’s initial score, insofar as this is practically possible. If the score
influenced funding or attention to the idea, the later outcome is no
longer independent of selection. A favored idea may receive more de‐
velopment and subsequently appear more valuable. This is an effect
of intervening on resources, not necessarily a correct prediction of a
preexisting property.
We can reduce the problem through an audit sample funded or evalu‐
ated independently of the score. Some rejected ideas are randomly se‐
lected for in-depth examination. This procedure allows false negatives
to be estimated. Without it, we know in detail what happened to ac‐
64


cepted ideas and almost nothing about those eliminated. A filter may
look excellent precisely because it has made its errors impossible to
observe.
Costs must be recorded by component. How long does contribution
extraction take? How many searches are needed for prior work? How
much time does verifying a false witness consume? What does clarify‐
ing an ambiguity cost? A model producing many apparently precise
but hard-to-check justifications may transfer costs to the expert. Effi‐
ciency is not merely time to the first verdict but time to a supported,
auditable conclusion.
Negative results are informative when localized. If a contribution
class is repeatedly missed, we must determine whether its prior work
is hard to retrieve, it changes the ontology or it contains small logical
differences. Calling it “radical novelty” after the model fails is insuffi‐
cient. The category must be defined through properties independent
of the outcome. Otherwise, the hypothesis becomes tautological: we
call radical what the system does not recognize and then conclude
that the system does not recognize the radical.
At the end of such a study, the conclusion may be mixed. The system
may be very good at identifying close rediscoveries, useful for map‐
ping prior work and poor at evaluating framework changes. It may
save time without being suitable for automatic rejection. Such a result
is not a partial victory to conceal. It is exactly the operational profile a
community needs to use technology without attributing undemon‐
strated capabilities to it.

Chapter 11. The Architecture of
an Evidence-Based Evaluator
11.1. Preserving the Evaluated Object and
Separating Operations
The architecture proposed in this chapter is not an implemented, val‐
idated product. It is a research contract for a system using LLMs
without turning their verdict into opaque authority. The central idea
is that the model must construct verifiable objects: claims located in
text, relations to prior work, explicit differences and questions that
tools or experts can resolve.
The system’s input must be retained as an immutable or versioned ar‐
tifact. The original document, date, provenance and any transforma‐
tions are needed for auditing. A hash can help identify the version but
does not demonstrate the truth of its content or the fidelity of its inter‐
pretation. Cryptography can certify that we are examining the same
bytes; it does not certify that we understand them correctly. This sep‐
aration matters to avoid confusing technical integrity with epistemic
validity.
The first operation extracts claimed contributions. Every contribution
must be linked to passages and a degree of explicitness. Some claims
are directly stated; others are inferred by the evaluator. The categories
must be marked differently. If the system invents a guarantee to make
the proposal more coherent, it changes the evaluated object. A charit‐
able interpretation may be retained as an alternative hypothesis, not
silently substituted for the original text.
The second operation constructs comparison questions. For each con‐
tribution, the system searches for prior work concerning mechanism,
result, assumptions and resources. It must not assume that a single
query captures all these dimensions. A contribution can have the
same mechanism with a different guarantee, or the same guarantee
with different assumptions. Search paths must reflect this structure
and preserve unusual terms that may be decisive.
66


The third operation compares retrieved sources with the candidate.
The result is not yet a global score. It is a map of relations: the same
claim, a narrower result, a different assumption, merely thematic pri‐
or work, contradiction or insufficient information. Relations must be
accompanied by evidence. A relevant passage in a paper cannot be re‐
placed by simply citing the paper’s title.
The fourth operation attempts to verify differences. For programs, it
may search for distinguishing inputs. For a proof, it may check a form‐
alizable step. For an empirical proposal, it may identify the experi‐
ment that would separate its mechanism from prior work. Not every
difference can be certified automatically. But the system can specify
what kind of evidence would be needed and which part of the verdict
remains conditional.
Only then comes aggregation. A score, if needed for prioritization,
must be derived from relations and an explicit policy. Users must be
able to see whether a low score comes from strong prior work or
missing information. These situations should not receive the same op‐
erational label. “Anticipated” and “undetermined” require different
actions.
An output format can be described through a few fields without im‐
posing an exhaustive ontology.
Field

Contribution
The evaluated claim and the original passages supporting it.
Reference
The corpus, date and criterion under which novelty is judged.
Prior Work
Related sources, relevant passages and search limitations.

What remains unanticipated and under which interpretation.
Witness
Example, calculation, counterexample or verification procedure.
Verdict
Conditional decision, uncertainty and the reason for any abstention.

This contract can be implemented as a graph of operations with expli‐
cit inputs and outputs. Each node consumes versioned artifacts and
produces a result with provenance. If a source changes or new prior
work appears, only dependent conclusions need be invalidated. We
need not claim that the LLM’s entire internal reasoning is transparent.
We can demand transparency about the data, transformations and ex‐
ternal evidence invoked by the verdict.
11.2. Diverse Retrieval, Precise Comparison and
Return to the Source
Retrieval-augmented generation architectures combine information
in the model with documents retrieved at use time. Lewis and col‐
leagues demonstrated the usefulness of such a combination in know‐
ledge-intensive language tasks. This idea offers a principle for access
to prior work, not a guarantee that retrieved sources are complete or
the novelty relation is evaluated correctly. [R14]
A lexical index is good for exact terms, formulas, acronyms and rare
expressions. A semantic index can retrieve related descriptions
worded differently. Multivector representations can preserve finer in‐
teractions between parts of text. ColBERT, by Khattab and Zaharia, is
an example of retrieval with late interaction between token represent‐
ations. Using such a technique does not mean that novelty differences
are automatically preserved; that must be evaluated on the concrete
task. [R15]
The proposed strategy is justified retrieval diversity. We search for
both the topic and the removed assumption, both the mechanism and
the distinguishing consequence. We retain results that are close on
different dimensions. A global top-k can eliminate important prior
work if it is lexically distant but semantically decisive. We can allocate
separate budgets to search paths and then deduplicate, without as‐
suming that a single similarity score suffices.
The final comparison must use passages complete enough to include
the conditions. An abstract may state a guarantee without listing
every assumption. A sentence in the conclusion may be broader than
the demonstrated result. The system must be able to follow internal
68


references and check where the claim is justified. This capability can
be costly; separating initial filtering from in-depth examination is
therefore useful.
Summaries must be treated as views, not permanently authoritative
replacements for documents. A summary is useful for navigation and
identifying questions. When the verdict depends on an exception, neg‐
ation or quantifier, the system must return to the source. An escala‐
tion rule can require original access when two possible interpreta‐
tions would change the decision. This rule can be tested through min‐
imal pairs.
A comparator can produce an internal table of assumptions and guar‐
antees, but must allow the value “unspecified.” Automatically filling
gaps with typical assumptions can create false equivalence. If prior
work does not state a property in the available passages, we cannot
assume either that it has or lacks that property. Relevant context must
be sought or uncertainty marked. Absence of a claim from an excerpt
is not identical to its negation in the full paper.
An adversarial verifier can attempt to refute each proposed relation.
If the model says A anticipates B, the verifier searches for a difference.
If it says B is new, the verifier searches for prior work or an equival‐
ent interpretation. The roles must produce evidence, not just opinions.
Agent count is no substitute for evidence independence. Three agents
using the same mistaken interpretation can reinforce the error.
The architecture must also handle documents as potentially hostile
data. An article may contain instructions or text attempting to influ‐
ence the evaluator. These must not be confused with application in‐
structions. Separating analyzed content from tool control is an engin‐
eering requirement. In a research system, compromising this separa‐
tion can affect not just security but experimental validity: the model is
no longer answering the same task.
11.3. Uncertainty, Audit and Criteria for Accepting
the Architecture
An evidence-based system must represent uncertainty by component.
It may be certain that two formal expressions differ, uncertain that
translating the text into expressions was faithful and very uncertain
69


that the library covers all prior work. A single confidence percentage
conceals this structure. It is more useful to distinguish uncertainty of
interpretation, retrieval, comparison and validation.
Not all these uncertainties need be well-calibrated probabilities from
the outset. They can be operational states accompanied by reasons: in‐
sufficient sources, alternative interpretations, an unverified witness
or an incomplete corpus. What matters is that labels lead to appropri‐
ate actions. Missing prior work may call for further search; an am‐
biguous translation may call for author clarification; a verified formal
difference may proceed to value assessment.
An audit must preserve the decision path. Which model version was
used? Which prompt? Which documents were accessed? Which quer‐
ies were executed? Which passages supported the conclusion? Which
operations were repeated? This information enables reproduction
and diagnosis. It must not be confused with guaranteed exact repro‐
duction of an external service that may change. The report must spe‐
cify what can be rerun and what depends on the provider’s infrastruc‐
ture.
Architecture acceptance criteria must be formulated before full imple‐
mentation. For example, in the finite laboratory, semantically equival‐
ent transformations should not change the verdict beyond the
preregistered threshold. In cases with supplied prior work, anticipa‐
tion relations must be supported by correct passages. On deliberately
unidentifiable cases, the system must abstain or formulate a condi‐
tional verdict. On the real stream, rejection audits must estimate the
rate of lost contributions.
The architecture would be refuted in its current form if it adds sub‐
stantial cost without reducing decisive errors, if evidence is fre‐
quently fictitious or if access to the original does not correct compres‐
sion-induced losses. It would need revision if the ambiguity detector
escalates almost every case or if witness verification costs more than
direct human evaluation. A technical proposal does not become good
merely by being more complex than a simple prompt.
A minimal architecture might actually be better: two independent
contribution extractions, diverse search, passage-level comparison
and output separating prior work from uncertainty. Additional com‐
70


plexity must be justified through ablation experiments. If removing
an agent does not change results, its role may be redundant. If a
simple quantifier-preservation rule resolves many errors, it may be
worth more than an expensive evaluator ensemble.
The final principle is that the system must be correctable. A new
source must be able to change the verdict. An interpretation error
must be locatable. A lost difference must be recoverable through ac‐
cess to the original. These properties do not guarantee discovery of
every new idea, but reduce the risk that a fluent, familiar verdict ends
the investigation.

Chapter 12. Filtering Rare Ideas
as a Decision Problem
12.1. Recognizing Is Not the Same as Selecting
Even a perfect detector of relative novelty does not automatically
solve selection. A team may need feasible ideas, impactful results or
projects compatible with its resources. Novelty is one decision com‐
ponent, not the sole objective. If these criteria are mixed from the out‐
set, we can no longer determine whether the system missed original‐
ity or made a different strategic choice.
We can represent the decision through the expected utility of examin‐
ing a proposal. Utility may depend on the probability that the differ‐
ence is real, the possible value of the result, verification cost and the
opportunity to learn. These components are the decision-maker’s as‐
sumptions. They are not properties the LLM can objectively infer from
the text. A system must request or declare the selection policy.
A simplified example can have two actions: in-depth verification or
postponement. If verification costs c, a valid contribution brings value
v and the estimated probability of relevant validity is p, the rule p·v>c
can justify verification in the minimal model. But v may be highly un‐
certain, and verification can produce useful information even if the
idea is false. The example shows the decision’s structure, not a univer‐
sal research threshold.
Very rare novelty complicates estimation. If we have few confirmed
examples, probabilities are hard to calibrate and model errors may
dominate. A cautious policy can combine exploitation of scores with
exploration of unusual cases. We need not randomly select half the
projects. But a system that never explores beyond its confidence re‐
gion will not systematically learn where it is wrong.
Semantic distance should not be used as a direct substitute for explor‐
ation value. A very distant text may be noise. A small difference in a
central mechanism may matter much more. Exploration can be strati‐
fied by uncertainty type: hard-to-find prior work, assumption
72


changes, contributions in different languages or cases where models
and verifiers disagree. The choice of strata is a hypothesis that must
be evaluated through auditing.
There is also the negative value of a false verdict that something is
old. If the system says “this already exists” without concrete prior
work, the author may abandon a promising direction. In a brain‐
storming application, this cost may exceed that of a false novelty
alarm. In an editorial application, costs may be distributed differently.
Response policy must reflect context, not merely average accuracy.
A normative recommendation of this book is that research systems
should be more demanding when rejecting than when proposing an
investigation. Claiming “worth checking” requires a plausible lead.
Claiming “there is nothing new” requires a comparison sufficient to
eliminate the claimed differences. This asymmetry is not a statistical
law; it is a choice justified by the irreversible cost of eliminating rare
ideas.
12.2. How We Observe Errors Concerning Rejected
Ideas
A filter produces two populations: accepted and rejected candidates.
Usually, accepted candidates are checked more carefully. We thus
learn much about false alarms but little about missed novelties. If we
report only selection precision, we may perceive an excellent system
while recall remains unknown. This observational asymmetry must
be corrected through design.
One solution is random sampling of rejections for auditing. Every re‐
jected candidate receives a known, nonzero probability of being
checked. Probabilities may differ between strata, for example to over‐
sample highly uncertain cases. But they must be retained for weight‐
ing estimates. If we check only rejections that seem interesting retro‐
spectively, we do not obtain a representative estimate.
Let A_i indicate that idea i was audited, q_i its inclusion probability
and Y_i the novelty label established through auditing. The total num‐
ber of novelties in the rejected population can be estimated using the
following weighted sum. The justification is elementary: the mean of
A_i/q_i is one if inclusion follows the stated probability.
73


The estimate can have high variance when audit probabilities are
very small. A formally nonzero chance is not enough. The budget
must allow useful uncertainty intervals. If novelties are extremely
rare, auditing may require extensive examination. This is a property
of the problem, not an error in the formula. An adaptive strategy can
increase sampling in strata where signals appear while retaining ap‐
propriate estimation rules.
The audit must also track the reason for rejection. A system can miss
contributions because it considers them old, unclear, infeasible or in‐
sufficiently supported. If the policy permits rejection for infeasibility,
cases where novelty was recognized must be separated. Otherwise, a
false-negative analysis may attribute to the detector a decision made
by another component.
Another question is who assigns the label after auditing. If the same
LLM and sources are used, the procedure may reproduce the initial
error. Auditing must bring additional information or verification: ex‐
perts, broader search, formal tools or time for experimentation. Re‐
peating the same verdict with a different prompt can test stability but
is insufficient as an independent audit.
The rejection archive also has a longitudinal role. An idea considered
unclear may become intelligible after a new framework appears.
Earlier work may be discovered later. Preserving versions enables ree‐
valuation and learning from errors. Deleting rejections makes selec‐
tion irreversible, and irreversibility reduces the system’s ability to
learn what types of novelty it fails to recognize.
12.3. Selection Dynamics and the Risk of
Homogenization
We can construct a very simple model of selection’s cumulative effect.
We have a distribution p_t(x) of visible ideas at round t and an accept‐
ance probability a(x). If only accepted ideas return in the next round,
74


the following distribution is proportional to the product of visibility
and acceptance. The model is a didactic idealization, not an empirical
description of a community.
If two classes have constant acceptance probabilities a1 and a2, their
frequency ratio is multiplied at each round by a1/a2. A small but per‐
sistent advantage can become large after many rounds. This conclu‐
sion is algebraic. It does not say that the advantage is unjustified or
every ecosystem works this way. It shows why a modest bias deserves
dynamic evaluation as well as measurement of the score difference in
a single round.
Introducing external ideas changes the process. We can mix the selec‐
ted distribution with a distribution of new input r, weighted by λ. If λ
is positive and r maintains certain classes, they do not automatically
disappear. Archive preservation and reevaluation can also change ac‐
ceptance probabilities. Homogenization is therefore not inevitable; it
depends on input and correction mechanisms.
In a real system, acceptance probability may depend on frequency. An
idea may be favored because it is familiar or, conversely, because it is
rare. If rarity is mechanically rewarded, the system may produce su‐
perficial variations to avoid resemblance. If familiarity is mechanic‐
ally rewarded, it may preserve dominant patterns. The objective
should not be to maximize either extreme, but to preserve the ability
to verify relevant differences.
A portfolio policy can retain projects with different profiles: robust in‐
cremental contributions, ideas with clear differences but incomplete
validation, and explorations challenging the criterion. They need not
all receive the same resources. But evaluating them should not force
them onto the same scale without explanation. A conceptual-clarifica‐
tion project and an operational-optimization project can have differ‐
ent types of outcome.
75


This discussion separates two normative questions. Do we want the
system to imitate the community’s current judgments, or help the
community discover its blind spots? The first task can be evaluated
through expert agreement. The second requires cases where disagree‐
ment is investigated and sometimes proves justified. A model per‐
fectly reproducing consensus is not thereby a perfect detector of con‐
tributions that will change consensus.
Opposition to consensus should not be romanticized. Many rejected
ideas are wrong or insufficiently developed. But a research system
cannot learn which exceptions deserve preservation if it equates un‐
familiarity with meaninglessness. Selection policy must allow verifica‐
tion of cases current scores do not favor, and audit results must be
able to change the procedure.
The operational conclusion is that the novelty detector and attention-
allocation policy must be designed together but evaluated separately.
The detector identifies relations and uncertainties. The policy decides
how much cost to accept for exploration and how much risk for rejec‐
tion. Mixing them into a single score can conceal both epistemic bi‐
ases and normative choices.

Chapter 13. The Strongest
Objections in Both Directions
13.1. The Skeptical Case: The Model May Learn
Innovation’s Reputation, Not Innovation
The strongest version of skepticism does not say that a model cannot
produce a sequence absent from training. That would be too easy to
contradict. It says that the evaluative notion of novelty is learned from
a community’s verdicts, and those verdicts contain conventions, hier‐
archies and preferences. The model may become a good predictor of
innovation’s social recognition without becoming a good detector of
differences not yet socially recognized.
This is a serious argument because it separates the label from the
property. If the data say that certain ideas are “new” because they
were published in prestigious contexts, a model can learn the context.
If every novelty example is explained retrospectively, it can learn the
narrative form of success. If unrecognized ideas are absent from the
data, there is no direct feedback about them. In such a world, per‐
formance on a benchmark built from the same conventions may over‐
estimate usefulness for discovering exceptions.
The argument is not dismissed by merely adding verbal reasoning. A
system can produce an extensive justification of the same social label.
Nor does a large training corpus automatically solve the problem:
more examples of the same selection can reinforce the bias. Answer‐
ing the skepticism requires evidence that the evaluator responds to
controlled differences and can contradict a social cue when prior
work refutes it.
A second skeptical objection concerns transformational novelty. A
model trained to represent the world in a particular vocabulary may
struggle when the contribution changes that vocabulary. Even if the
architecture can express new combinations, the interpretation pro‐
77


cedure may normalize the new framework to the old one. This objec‐
tion does not prove impossibility, but identifies a region where stand‐
ard tests built from stable categories may be insufficient.
A third objection concerns the lack of independent ground truth. If
even experts cannot reliably establish novelty, how can we validate
the detector? The answer cannot be a promise of omniscience. We can
validate components in finite worlds, check concrete prior work and
evaluate conditional decisions. For open conceptual changes, uncer‐
tainty and longitudinal follow-up must be accepted. The absence of a
perfect standard does not mean every method is equally good; it
means performance claims must be limited to what was measured.
Skepticism becomes unproductive when it changes its criterion after
every success. If every recognized difference is retrospectively de‐
clared “not truly new,” the hypothesis can no longer be refuted. A sci‐
entific thesis must specify in advance what would count as recogni‐
tion. We can require generalization to unseen families, correct wit‐
nesses and robustness to reformulation. But we cannot require the
model to recognize something indefinable and then use the failure of
definition as evidence against it.
13.2. The Optimistic Case: Old Procedures Can
Detect New Objects
The strongest version of optimism is procedural. A system need not
have memorized a contribution to establish that it differs from prior
work. It can learn comparison operations, use new sources and deleg‐
ate checks to tools. Novelty of the object does not require novelty of
every cognitive operation used to evaluate it. A microscope built from
known principles can observe an unknown specimen; the analogy il‐
lustrates possibility rather than equating an LLM with a calibrated
measuring instrument.
Optimism has a strong formal counterargument to universal im‐
possibility: in a finite world, an exact rule can decide relative novelty,
and an LLM can participate in translating or controlling the proced‐
ure. If LLM use includes access to such tools, the class of possible sys‐
78


tems is broader than an isolated generator. Excluding tools by defini‐
tion may be legitimate for a model benchmark, but not for a conclu‐
sion about the usefulness of all LLM applications.
There is, however, an optimistic trap. The fact that the system can pro‐
duce a witness does not guarantee that the witness is correct. The fact
that it can cite a source does not guarantee that the source supports
the relation. The fact that it can formalize a claim does not guarantee
faithful formalization. A chain of approximate components does not
become verified merely because the final component uses exact logic.
If the translation is wrong, the verifier can impeccably certify a differ‐
ent problem.
The procedural argument must therefore be accompanied by con‐
tracts between stages. The model proposes an interpretation; the
source allows it to be checked. The model proposes a witness; an ex‐
ecutor tests it. The model proposes prior work; a comparator checks
the passages. Where no automated verifier exists, uncertainty re‐
mains explicit. This discipline is less spectacular than the promise of
an “autonomous researcher,” but offers a clear experimental path.
Another excessive optimism claims that scaling up models will inevit‐
ably solve the problem. The arguments presented provide no such
guarantee. More capacity may improve some operations and reduce
errors, but does not resolve missing information, incoherent criteria
or irreversible pipeline collisions. A larger final model may use the
original text better; it cannot reliably deduce inaccessible prior work
or a difference completely removed from its input.
Likewise, sampling diversity is not identical to interpretation di‐
versity. Raising temperature can produce more formulations and hy‐
potheses, but they may remain within the same conceptual family. An
exploration system must track operational differences between inter‐
pretations, not just lexical variation. If ten answers propose the same
mechanism under different names, we do not have ten research direc‐
tions.
The defensible optimistic position is therefore conditional: some com‐
ponents of novelty evaluation can be usefully automated, and their
limits can be measured and reduced through source access, better
79


representations and verifiers. It does not follow that an unaudited lin‐
guistic score can replace scientific judgment. Technical possibility be‐
gins a validation obligation rather than ending it.
13.3. An Intermediate Position That Is Not a
Comfortable Compromise
Supporting possibility and limits simultaneously does not mean avoid‐
ing a verdict. We can firmly reject the inference “learned from the
past, therefore incapable of new comparisons.” We can firmly accept
the limit “difference lost, therefore impossible to recover from the re‐
maining representation alone.” We can regard the existence of famili‐
arity bias in novelty evaluation as likely but still configuration-de‐
pendent. These positions do not contradict one another because they
concern different claims.
The most important conceptual shift replaces the question about the
model’s essence with the question about the observational contract.
What differences must the system distinguish? What information does
it receive? What transformations are allowed? What witnesses can be
checked? What type of error would count as failure? This approach is
closer to verifiable engineering than the dispute over whether the
model “truly understands.”
Nevertheless, the problem of understanding does not disappear. It is
partly operationalized. A system able to identify assumptions, produce
distinguishing consequences, resist reformulations and revise its ver‐
dict when prior work appears displays competence relevant to evalu‐
ation. This does not demonstrate a complete theory of understanding.
We have defined practically important, testable behaviors. This con‐
ceptual modesty is a methodological virtue, not an abandonment of
deep questions.
Novelty may sometimes require changing the question, not just the
answer. A detector fixed to one criterion can be correct within its
framework yet insufficient for research. Advanced systems should
therefore be able to signal that a contribution challenges the criterion
or that two interpretations produce different evaluations. But signal‐
ing this should not automatically be rewarded as originality. It must
be followed by clarification and testing of the consequences.
80


The most fruitful version of the initial intuition is thus a hypothesis
about preserving differences under interpretation. A system educated
by the past can be very good at making texts intelligible in the past’s
terms. Precisely this competence can become a vulnerability when the
task is to notice an exception requiring those terms to expand. Failure
is not inevitable. But we must not assume that a fluent interpretation
preserved everything that mattered.
This argument can ground a distinct research program: evaluating fi‐
delity to rare differences, not merely average fidelity to content. A
summary can be evaluated by how well it preserves the questions sep‐
arating a candidate from prior work. A retriever can be evaluated by
its ability to find sources refuting or confirming the difference, not
just thematic sources. An evaluator can be evaluated through wit‐
nesses and conditions, not merely agreement with global grades.
The book’s verdict is not that we should trust or distrust LLMs as a ho‐
mogeneous whole. Trust must be attached to operations and evidence.
We can trust a correctly executed witness, place less trust in an un‐
checked translation and very little in a claim of historical absence
without documented search. Such a distribution of trust is more de‐
manding and useful than a global label about model intelligence.

Chapter 14. The Research
Program and Criteria for Real
Progress
14.1. A Minimal but Decisive Experimental Core
A research program need not begin by building a very large system. It
should begin with an experiment capable of separating explanations.
The proposed core is a finite corpus of contributions with exact labels,
equivalent reformulations and minimal semantic differences. The
model is tested with prior work absent, supplied and retrieved, while
the full representation is compared with a compressed one. Each
factor corresponds to a distinct mechanism.
An illustrative pilot could use 160 independent cases, equally distrib‐
uted among eight construction families. For each case, four presenta‐
tions, three prior-work access conditions and two representation
paths would produce 3,840 evaluations for one model. Three repeti‐
tions of each evaluation would produce 11,520 calls. These numbers
are a planning example, not a power calculation or cost recommenda‐
tion. The 11,520 outputs do not represent 11,520 independent ideas.
The pilot first examines instrument integrity: labels are correct, vari‐
ants equivalent, witnesses work and logs are complete. Only then do
we estimate effect sizes and plan the confirmatory study. If the gener‐
ator has artifacts or the parser loses information, a large benchmark
may yield only a precise estimate of an incorrectly defined problem.
The primary outcome should be sensitivity to semantic change and in‐
variance to irrelevant change in form. These must be measured to‐
gether. A model that never changes its verdict is stable under para‐
phrase but does not detect differences. A model that always changes
its verdict is sensitive but not discriminative. Competence requires
change when the label changes and stability when it does not.
A hypothesis map can be maintained in a compact register.

14.2. From mechanism to architecture and utility
After identifying a mechanism, the next step is a remedial interven‐
tion. If the problem is the loss of quantifiers, we can add explicit ex‐
traction of them and access to passages. If the problem is thematic
search, we can add difference-oriented queries. If the problem is cal‐
ibration, we can separate the verdict from abstention and evaluate
probabilities on an independent set. The remedy must be chosen ac‐
cording to the diagnosis, not the popularity of a technique.
Each remedy must be tested through ablation. We compare the com‐
plete system with a version without the component in question, keep‐
ing budgets as comparable as possible. An improvement may come
83


from additional cost, not the claimed mechanism. A comparison with
a simple baseline is also useful, for example a full-text comparator or
an explicit rule in the finite domain. A sophisticated system that does
not outperform a modest baseline cannot justify its value through
complexity.
Real progress may also consist in reducing unjustified confidence. If
the new architecture better identifies cases where information is in‐
sufficient, raw accuracy may not increase much, but the risk of auto‐
mated decisions may fall. However, the cost of abstentions must be
measured. A system that escalates everything is not a useful evaluator,
even if it no longer makes mistakes on its own.
After the laboratory, transfer to real documents must be gradual. First,
short passages with validated differences. Then, complete papers with
adjudicated prior-work packages. Then, end-to-end search over ver‐
sioned corpora. Finally, prospective streams. Each stage adds a source
of uncertainty. If performance drops, the structure of the stages allows
the degradation to be located.
Utility must be evaluated in relation to human work. Does the system
save search time? Does it produce comparisons that experts can verify
faster? Does it reduce rediscoveries? Does it retain more rare contri‐
butions within the same budget? These questions are more concrete
than “can AI evaluate research?” A system can be valuable as a prior-
work assistant and unsuitable as a final filter. This boundary must ap‐
pear in the conclusions and the interface.
Replication must include different model families and conditions of
use, without assuming that a commercial name defines a stable con‐
figuration. Versions, data, tools and parameters must be documented.
If a service changes, historical results remain valid for the configura‐
tion tested, but do not automatically describe the current configura‐
tion. A mature program treats evaluation as a continuous process, not
permanent certification.

14.3. What would validate or invalidate the initial
intuition
The universal form of the intuition would be contradicted by robust
success on new objects, with exact ground truth, a fixed criterion and
conditions that prevent mere retrieval of a memorized label. The
model does not need to discover a fundamental law of nature to con‐
tradict a universal claim of impossibility. A nontrivial domain in
which comparison generalizes correctly to new instances is enough.
The behavioral form would be supported if familiar style or innova‐
tion rhetoric systematically changes the verdict for the same content
beyond a relevant threshold, and the effect replicates. It would be
weakened locally if adequately powered tests show invariance to
these manipulations and sensitivity to real semantic changes. No
single experiment closes the question for all domains, but it can delin‐
eate a zone of confidence.
The causal form concerning training would require interventions on
exposure. A convincing result would show that repeatedly associating
a pattern with the label “new” subsequently leads to overrating that
pattern, although ground-truth novelty is controlled. If the interven‐
tion has no relevant effect in well-tested regimes, the explanation
must be revised. Perhaps the problem comes mainly from the prompt,
the criterion or the representation, not the presumed frequency.
The informational form has a different status. For a demonstrated rel‐
evant collision, the impossibility of perfect classification from the re‐
maining representation follows mathematically. What must be tested
empirically is whether, and how often, real pipelines produce such
collisions or approximate losses. The theorem provides no estimate of
their frequency. The experiment must establish its practical relevance,
not prove the theorem again.
The institutional form would require observations of the flow of ideas
and feedback. If filters systematically reduce the diversity of verified
differences and an audit discovers relevant contributions among re‐
jections, there are reasons to change policy. But the impact must be
separated from resource shortages, idea quality and other filters. A
plausible story about homogenization does not replace measurement.
85


At the end of the research, we may reach a conclusion that is less dra‐
matic but more valuable than the initial hypothesis: models can detect
certain types of novelty, fail on others, and an important part of the
failure comes from losing differences in representations and evaluat‐
ing social or rhetorical proxies. This conclusion would lead directly to
better architectures, clearer criteria and audit policies.
The final thesis is this. The past does not make recognizing the new
impossible; it supplies the rules, comparisons and prejudices through
which we examine it. The decisive question is whether these tools al‐
low the difference to remain visible. A novelty evaluator becomes
credible not when it speaks convincingly about originality, but when
it can show what is different, compared with what, under which con‐
ditions, and through what evidence it would change its conclusion.

Appendix A. Detailed

examples
A.1. From frequencies to decisions: three
calculations that must not be confused
Consider a world with two classes, new and old. We want to under‐
stand the distribution of texts, learning a label and choosing an action
separately. These operations are often merged into a single intuition
about “what is probable,” although they answer different questions.
The following examples are didactic constructions, with numbers
chosen for clarity, not empirical findings about language models.
First, suppose a corpus contains nine hundred descriptions of idea A
and one hundred of idea B. An ideal model of idea identity would as‐
sign probabilities of 0.9 and 0.1. If we always choose the most prob‐
able idea, we will produce only A. If we sample correctly from the dis‐
tribution, we will also produce B, in its proportion. If we ask whether
B differs from A and provide their definitions, the frequencies do not
determine the answer on their own. We have moved from generation
to comparison.
Now we introduce an evaluative label. In the data, descriptions with a
certain rhetorical form S are called “innovative” in 80% of cases, and
those without S in 20%. A classifier using only S can perform well if
this correlation persists in the test. To find out whether it understands
the conceptual difference, we construct a test in which S is independ‐
ent of exact novelty. If performance falls, we have evidence that the
cue mattered. To demonstrate that those frequencies caused its use,
we must intervene on the data, not merely observe the correlation.
Finally, we choose an action. Even if the probability of novelty is es‐
timated correctly, the decision depends on costs. If checking a candid‐
ate is cheap and losing a discovery is costly, the selection threshold
can be low. If checking is very expensive, the threshold may rise. Two
87


systems with the same estimated probability can make different de‐
cisions without either being less capable of detecting novelty. The dif‐
ference belongs to policy.
We now derive the decomposition of logarithmic loss. For a finite set
and distributions P and Q, the average cost is the sum of P(x) multi‐
plied by minus log Q(x). We add and subtract minus log P(x). We ob‐
tain the entropy of P and the sum of P(x) log(P(x)/Q(x)), namely the KL
divergence. If Q assigns zero probability to an event with positive P,
the cost is infinite. Otherwise, the divergence is nonnegative, and the
ideal minimum is attained at Q=P.
Why is the divergence nonnegative? We can use the inequality log
u≤u−1 for positive u. Applied to the ratio Q(x)/P(x), it shows that the
sum of P(x) log(Q(x)/P(x)) is at most the sum of Q(x) minus the sum of
P(x), namely zero. Changing the sign gives nonnegativity. This ele‐
mentary proof explains why the ideal objective does not require elim‐
inating rare events. It requires assigning correct probabilities, includ‐
ing to them.
In a model with limited capacity, the family Q may not contain P. Op‐
timization then chooses the best approximation according to average
loss. If errors on a rare class cost little in the overall average, the mod‐
el may sacrifice that class for larger improvements in frequent re‐
gions. This is a possible tradeoff mechanism. It is not equivalent to the
claim that every rare class will be ignored. A compact rule can simul‐
taneously explain frequent and rare cases.
Returning to the prevalence example, for π=0.001, TPR=0.9 and
FPR=0.01, the probability that a selection is truly new is 0.0009 divided
by 0.01089, or approximately 0.08264. A detector with these character‐
istics recovers most novel contributions but produces many false
alarms. If we lower FPR to 0.001, hypothetically maintaining the same
sensitivity, precision becomes approximately 0.474. The example
shows how much the false alarm rate matters in a rare-event stream.
This analysis does not automatically justify raising the threshold. The
threshold may also reduce TPR. The complete curve and verification
capacity must be analyzed. If we choose only the safest candidates, we
88


may eliminate precisely the unusual contributions. A selection policy
can therefore retain a main channel with high precision and an audit
or exploration channel. Their proportions must be set in relation to
costs and evaluated, not chosen to produce a convenient score.
A.2. Bayes risk, collision and the possible benefit of
a summary
Let us have a discrete representation Z and a binary label Y. For each
value z, a classifier must choose zero or one. If it chooses zero, it errs
on the joint mass P(Y=1,Z=z). If it chooses one, it errs on the mass
P(Y=0,Z=z). The optimal choice takes the class with greater mass, and
the local cost is the smaller mass. The sum of these minima gives the
Bayes risk in Chapter 6.
This argument shows exactly where inevitable error arises. Not in
stylistic complexity or insufficient reasoning, but in the fact that the
same observation is compatible with different labels. A better al‐
gorithm can attain Bayes risk, but cannot improve on it without addi‐
tional information or changing the problem. In a finite world with de‐
terministic labels and complete observations, the risk can be zero.
After compression, it can become positive.
For equal priors, write p0(z)=P(Z=z|Y=0) and p1(z)=P(Z=z|Y=1). The
joint mass of each class is half its conditional distribution. We use the
identity min(a,b)=(a+b−|a−b|)/2. After summation, we obtain a risk
equal to one half minus one quarter of the sum of absolute differ‐
ences. Total variation is half that sum, which yields the formula in the
chapter.
If p0 and p1 are identical, TV is zero and the minimum error is one
half. If the distributions have disjoint supports, TV is one and the min‐
imum error is zero. Between these extremes lie degrees of separabil‐
ity. In a compression experiment, we can ask whether the transforma‐
tion makes the class distributions more similar with respect to useful
information. We do not need to observe an exact collision of real-val‐
ued vectors to have practical degradation; they may become closer
than the evaluator can resolve.
89


However, informational impossibility must be distinguished from dif‐
ficulty for an algorithm. Suppose X is a long document that explicitly
contains the correct label within a difficult logical relation. A sum‐
mary Z expresses it clearly. The actual evaluator may have a lower er‐
ror on Z. This does not contradict the fact that an optimal evaluator
with access to X could compute the same summary and decide at least
as well. The summary changes cost and accessibility; it does not create
additional truth about the world.
A constructed example uses documents with many irrelevant sen‐
tences and two premises: if A then B; A. The question is whether B fol‐
lows. A faithful summary can retain only the premises and reduce er‐
rors by a limited model. But if the question is whether the document
also asserts C, the same summary may be insufficient. Fidelity is task-
dependent. A good summary for conclusion B may be poor for invent‐
orying all contributions.
We can formulate a compression objective oriented toward a family
of questions Q. We want a low-cost representation such that answers
to relevant questions are preserved with an error below a threshold.
This is a design objective, not a complete method. The difficulty is that
questions about novelty may be unknown before reading. If the fam‐
ily Q is too narrow, contributions that change the questions may be
lost. If it is unlimited, the requirement approaches preserving all in‐
formation.
A system can manage this tension through two levels. The compact
representation serves frequent questions, while the original archive
remains accessible for new questions. Cost shifts from complete stor‐
age in every context to selective access. The practical guarantee de‐
pends on the system’s ability to recognize when it needs the source.
Detecting insufficient representation is therefore a separate task that
must be evaluated.
A subtle consequence is that the model’s explanation may be more in‐
formative than its score and still insufficient for the verdict. If the ex‐
planation preserves a difference but the score ignores it, the informa‐
tion exists in the output and can be recovered by another aggregator.
If the explanation and score were constructed after the same smooth‐
ing, both may miss the difference. Evaluation should inspect interme‐
diate objects, not only the final label.

A.3. Rare differences and selection models: exact
calculations
We construct two functions on eight bits. The first returns the XOR of
the first two bits. The second returns the same value, except on the in‐
put where all eight bits are one, where the value is inverted. The func‐
tions differ on exactly one input out of 256. If the library contains only
the first function, the second is new under the criterion of extensional
equivalence. This label does not depend on how frequently the distin‐
guishing input appears in use.
If inputs are uniform and we observe one hundred independent ex‐
amples with replacement, the probability of not seeing the difference
is (255/256) to the power of one hundred, approximately 0.676. With
one thousand examples, the probability is approximately 0.01996. To
obtain a miss probability of at most 0.05, we need at least 766 observa‐
tions in this regime. Enumerating all 256 inputs without replacement
is, of course, sufficient. The sampling regime matters.
If the distinguishing input has probability ε under the real distribu‐
tion, the general formula is (1−ε) to the power m. For small ε, the ex‐
ponential approximation exp(−mε) shows that the required budget
grows roughly inversely with ε. This is a reason not to equate the ab‐
sence of an observed difference with functional equivalence. A test
can be very long and still miss a rare case if it is not designed to seek
it.
A symbolic verifier can sometimes avoid sampling by analyzing struc‐
ture. For the functions above, it directly observes the condition that
inverts the output. But we must not generalize from this simple con‐
struction to every program. Finding a witness may be computationally
difficult. The difference between verifying a supplied witness and dis‐
covering one is central to the detector’s operational complexity.
Now consider compression. If we retain the outputs on the first 255 in‐
puts in lexicographic order and remove the last input, the two func‐
tions receive the same representation. The error comes from a de‐
terministic projection, not a language model. If we later add the final
row, the difference becomes identifiable. The example is an execut‐
able demonstration of how the verdict depends on the information re‐
tained.
91


For repeated selection, consider two classes with equal initial weights.
The first is accepted with probability 0.55, the second with 0.50, and
only accepted cases return. After t rounds, the weight ratio is 1.1 to
the power t. After twenty rounds, the ratio is approximately 6.73. We
have not modeled idea quality, the emergence of new contributions or
error correction. The calculation shows only how a persistent asym‐
metry can be amplified in a closed process.
If we introduce external ideas in each round and maintain auditing,
the dynamics change. Disadvantaged classes can be reintroduced, and
acceptance probabilities can change after errors are discovered. Thus,
the simple model is not an inevitable prediction of homogenization. It
is a tool for formulating the variables to be measured: external input,
selection probability, reevaluation rate and their dependence on fa‐
miliarity.
These calculations share a purpose. They show that an observed error
can have very different sources: the rarity of a distinguishing case, in‐
formation compression or a repeated selection policy. None should
automatically be attributed to the essence of an LLM. Instead, each
can be introduced into a controlled experiment and lead to a concrete
intervention.

Appendix B. Preregistration
protocol and evidence register
B.1. Study record and inferential contract
The protocol below is a conceptual template for a confirmatory study.
Concrete values must be set before data collection, following an inde‐
pendent pilot. It is not presented as an already registered protocol and
does not imply that an experiment has been performed. Its purpose is
to prevent retrospective changes to hypotheses, metrics and the popu‐
lation until the result becomes convenient.
The study title must name the mechanism and domain: for example,
the influence of stylistic familiarity on detecting functional novelty in
finite circuits. A formulation such as “can LLMs be creative?” is too
broad for such an experiment. The target population must specify ob‐
ject families and description languages. Conclusions will not automat‐
ically extend to open-ended scientific ideas.
The primary hypothesis must be formulated as a measurable contrast.
For example, the rate of correctly identifying the difference is changed
by style when semantics and prior work are held constant. The direc‐
tion can be preregistered, or the test can be two-sided if there are
reasons for effects in both directions. A threshold for the smallest rel‐
evant effect must be chosen, expressed in percentage points, score dif‐
ference or verification cost. The threshold must not be chosen after
seeing the results.
Experimental conditions must define exactly what the model receives.
An absent corpus, automated retrieval and a package of decisive prior
work are different treatments. Full text, a summary and a summary
with source access are other treatments. In a partial design, the reas‐
ons for omitting certain combinations must be declared. A reduced
design can be very good if it answers a precise question.
The main independent unit is the contribution or family, not the num‐
ber of generated responses. Repetitions estimate model variability in
the same situation; they do not multiply the number of ideas. The
93


clustering structure and method for calculating intervals must be spe‐
cified. If we use multiple models, we must declare whether inference
concerns individual models or a family of training procedures.

differently from what the researcher expected. A response in the
wrong format may be an instruction-following error and must be re‐

The analysis plan must distinguish confirmation from exploration. We
can later investigate why a model fails on a family, but the result gen‐

we have a new procedure and need a separate evaluation set. A
benchmark repeatedly used for tuning gradually becomes part of the
system’s training, even if the model weights do not change.

B.2. A case register and the path from text to
verdict
Each case must have a stable identifier and a version. The identifier
must not reveal the label. Data must separate the semantic object
from its presentation. For a circuit, the object can be the expression
tree and truth table. The presentation can be natural-language text.
For an article, the semantic object is not known exactly, so we retain
the extracted contribution together with passages and alternative in‐
terpretations.
The register must include the novelty criterion and exact library. If
the library changes, the label may change without modifying the can‐
didate. This is not an inconsistency but a consequence of the relative
definition. Versions must be retained so that the initial verdict can be
reconstructed. In a temporal study, the reference time is part of the
case, not merely decorative metadata.
Transformations must have provenance. Who or what produced the
paraphrase? Which rules guarantee equivalence? Was it checked by
an executor, an expert or only a model? For summaries, a record of
what information was omitted must be retained. If evaluation later
discovers a difference, we can determine whether it existed in the
model’s input or had been removed beforehand.
The evaluator’s output must be stored in full, before any normaliza‐
tion. A parser can extract the score and verdict, but the raw output re‐
mains necessary for auditing. Parser errors must be tested separately.
If the model says “I cannot decide” and the parser extracts a number
from an example, the analysis may produce a false result without the
model having issued that verdict.
Witnesses must be represented as verifiable objects. For a circuit, a
witness is an input and the two outputs. For prior work, it is the
source, version and passage supporting anticipation. For an empirical
hypothesis, it may be a protocol that would produce different results
under two mechanisms. A verified witness must be distinguished
from a proposed witness. An idea about how the difference could be
tested is not already proof of the difference.
95


B.3. Reporting the result and deciding whether to
continue
The final report must present the primary result even if it does not
support the hypothesis. It must show effect size, uncertainty, cost and
analyses by preregistered families. Selecting a few spectacular ex‐
amples is not enough. Examples are useful for the mechanism, but do
not replace the distribution of results. A dramatic failure may be rare;
a small bias may be systematic.
A null result must be interpreted in relation to the precision of the es‐
timate. If the interval is wide, the study may be inconclusive. If the in‐
terval excludes effects greater than the practical threshold, we can say
that evidence for a relevant effect has weakened in the configuration
tested. Lack of significance must not be converted into proof of ab‐
sence. Conversely, a significant difference does not automatically justi‐
fy a verdict about operational importance.
The decision to continue should depend on the information gained. If
the style effect is small but compression loss is large, the project can
shift its focus toward representations. If retrieval is the main bottle‐
neck, investment in a more expensive evaluator may be premature. If
no remedy outperforms the simple baseline at comparable cost, the
architecture must be simplified or the hypothesis revised.
The report must distinguish what the experimenter supplied, what
was learned, what the model generated and what was verified exactly.
This distinction is crucial for hybrid systems. Good performance may
come from a perfect prior-work package or a generator that makes
the task too easy. These advantages do not invalidate the experiment
if declared, but they limit the conclusions.
The report’s final section should state what future observation would
change the conclusion. A new domain, another transformation family
or an audit of rejections may reveal limits. A good scientific conclu‐
sion is not protected against revision; it is formulated so that revision
is possible and informative.

Appendix C. An executable
example of relative novelty
C.1. What the program verifies and what it does not
The program below is a complete Python example using only the
standard library. It was executed to verify the calculations and finite
properties presented here. It does not call an LLM, test a model’s cre‐
ativity or provide empirical evidence for familiarity bias. It independ‐
ently verifies several conditions that a future language benchmark
should satisfy.
A circuit is represented as a tree built from tuples. An integer denotes
a variable’s index. A tuple begins with the operator and continues
with the arguments. Evaluation is recursive and does not dynamically
execute code strings. The number of inputs is explicitly limited to
avoid accidentally very large enumerations. The program’s limit is a
practical safeguard, not a theoretical limit of the method.
The first test shows that a reformulation using De Morgan’s laws is
equivalent to AND and therefore old relative to the chosen library.
The second shows that XNOR is not in the library consisting of AND,
OR and XOR. This novelty is relative; adding XNOR to the library
would change the verdict. The program does not claim that XNOR is a
new invention in the history of logic.
The third test constructs two functions on eight bits that differ only
when all inputs are one. Verification enumerates all inputs and identi‐
fies the unique witness. It then removes the last row from the tables
and confirms the collision of the remaining representations. The ex‐
ample thus separates a real difference from the information available
to the evaluator. No classifier receiving only the two truncated tables
can reliably deduce the missing difference without additional assump‐
tions.
The final calculations use independent sampling with replacement
and uniform inputs. They describe neither exhaustive enumeration
nor natural input distributions. This qualification is necessary to in‐
97


terpret the number of observations. The program can be saved to a
file and run with a Python 3 interpreter; its deterministic output is re‐
produced after the code.
C.2. Complete implementation
"""Exemplu didactic; nu evalueaza niciun LLM."""
from itertools import product
from math import ceil, log
def value(node, bits):
    """Evalueaza un arbore validat, fara eval()."""
    if type(node) is int:
        if not 0 <= node < len(bits):
            raise ValueError("Indice de variabila invalid")
        return bits[node]
    if not isinstance(node, tuple) or not node:
        raise ValueError("Nod invalid")
    op, *args = node
    arity = {"not": 1, "and": 2, "or": 2, "xor": 2}
    if not isinstance(op, str) or op not in arity:
        raise ValueError("Operator invalid")
    if len(args) != arity[op]:
        raise ValueError("Operator sau aritate invalida")
    values = [value(arg, bits) for arg in args]
    if op == "not":
        return 1 - values[0]
    a, b = values
    if op == "and":
        return a & b
    if op == "or":
        return a | b
    return a ^ b
def table(node, n):
    if type(n) is not int or not 1 <= n <= 16:
        raise ValueError("n trebuie sa fie intre 1 si 16")
    return tuple(value(node, bits)
                 for bits in product((0, 1), repeat=n))
def compare(candidate, library, n):
    """Noutate functionala fata de o biblioteca finita."""
    target = table(candidate, n)
    matches = [name for name, node in library.items()
               if table(node, n) == target]
    return not matches, matches
def all_inputs_true(n):
    node = 0
    for index in range(1, n):
98


        node = ("and", node, index)
    return node
def main():
    library = {"AND": ("and", 0, 1),
               "OR": ("or", 0, 1),
               "XOR": ("xor", 0, 1)}
    old = ("not", ("or", ("not", 0), ("not", 1)))
    new = ("not", ("xor", 0, 1))
    assert compare(old, library, 2) == (False, ["AND"])
    assert compare(new, library, 2) == (True, [])
    print("Parafraza AND: veche")
    print("XNOR fata de biblioteca: noua")
    n = 8
    baseline = ("xor", 0, 1)
    candidate = ("xor", baseline, all_inputs_true(n))
    first, second = table(baseline, n), table(candidate, n)
    witnesses = [bits for bits, a, b in
                 zip(product((0, 1), repeat=n),
                     first, second) if a != b]
    assert witnesses == [(1,) * n]
    assert first[:-1] == second[:-1]
    assert first != second
    print("Martor unic:", witnesses[0])
    print("Coliziune dupa eliminarea martorului: True")
    miss = (1 - 2 ** (-n)) ** 100
    needed = ceil(log(0.05) / log(1 - 2 ** (-n)))
    print(f"Ratare dupa 100 observatii: {miss:.6f}")
    print("Observatii pentru ratare <= 0.05:", needed)
    print("Toate verificarile exacte au trecut.")

Appendix D. Technical
vocabulary and prerequisite
knowledge
D.1. Probability, information and statistics
Random variable. A function that assigns a numerical or categorical
outcome to each possible situation in a probabilistic model. In this
book, the novelty label, evaluator verdict and document representa‐
tion can be random variables. Randomness can describe variation
across cases, not necessarily a fundamentally nondeterministic phys‐
ical world.
Distribution. The rule assigning probabilities to a variable’s values.
The population it describes must be specified: training texts, ideas in a
stream, circuit inputs or model outputs. An unannounced shift from
one distribution to another is an important source of confusion in dis‐
cussions of rarity.
Conditional probability. The probability of an event after fixing
some information. An idea’s overall frequency is not the same as the
probability of the label “new” after comparing it with explicit prior
work. Conditioning can radically change the distribution relevant to
the decision.
Prior. The distribution used before observing the current information,
relative to a probabilistic model. A prior is not necessarily an unjusti‐
fied prejudice. It becomes problematic if it dominates relevant evid‐
ence or is confused with a verified conclusion about the individual
case.
Posterior. The distribution updated after observing data, under the
model’s assumptions. A posterior can be calculated impeccably in a
misspecified model. The resulting numerical confidence does not re‐
move the obligation to verify that variables and assumptions match
the novelty problem.
101


Likelihood. The value of the probability of the data viewed as a func‐
tion of a model’s parameters. It must not be confused with the probab‐
ility that a scientific hypothesis is true or with an idea’s novelty. A lin‐
guistically predictable text can contain an important conceptual dif‐
ference.
Informational surprisal. The negative logarithm of an outcome’s
probability. It measures improbability relative to a distribution, not
value, meaning or historical priority. Noise can be surprising, and an
important contribution can be expressed very simply.
Entropy. A variable’s average surprisal. It is low when the distribu‐
tion is concentrated and high when many outcomes are difficult to an‐
ticipate. A corpus’s entropy is not a direct measure of the originality of
its ideas.
Cross-entropy. The average logarithmic cost of predictions from a dis‐
tribution Q for data drawn from P. It decomposes into the entropy of P
and the KL divergence. In training, minimizing it encourages predic‐
tion of the distribution, not merely selection of the most frequent out‐
come.
Kullback–Leibler divergence. An asymmetric measure of the differ‐
ence between two distributions, relevant to prediction cost. It is not an
ordinary geometric distance and does not automatically measure the
semantic difference between two ideas. Choosing the variables is as
important as the formula.
Mutual information. The amount by which observing one variable
reduces uncertainty about another. In evaluating representations, we
are interested in information about the relevant label or question, not
only information about the document as a whole. A representation
can retain the topic and lose the difference.
Total variation. A measure of separation between two distributions.
In binary classification with equal priors, it determines the minimum
Bayes risk. If the observation distributions are identical in the two
classes, the observation cannot distinguish them better than the prior.
102


Bayes risk. The smallest possible expected error for the specified in‐
formation and loss. It is not the error of a particular model. An al‐
gorithm can be far worse than the informational limit, and a repres‐
entation can impose positive error even on an optimal algorithm.
Task-sufficient statistic. A representation that preserves everything
needed for the intended decision. In the book’s operational sense, suf‐
ficiency is relative to the criterion and questions. A representation
sufficient for topic classification may be insufficient for mechanism
novelty.
Distribution shift. The difference between the population used in
training or calibration and the population encountered later. In nov‐
elty research, the shift is not merely an accidental malfunction; it may
be the very phenomenon of interest. Guarantees obtained from the
past must be interpreted conditionally.
Prevalence. The proportion of positive cases in the evaluated popula‐
tion. Low prevalence can produce many false alarms even for a de‐
tector with good sensitivity. Selection precision cannot be interpreted
without knowing or estimating the relevant prevalence.
Precision. The proportion of positive selections that are correct. In an
idea filter, it answers how much verification is spent on false alarms.
It does not say how many new contributions were lost among rejec‐
tions.
Recall or sensitivity. The proportion of truly positive cases detected.
For discovery, it measures the ability to retain novel contributions. It
can be estimated only if we also have information about rejected
cases, not just selections.
False positive rate. The proportion of old cases classified as new. It
differs from the proportion of false alarms among selections. Confus‐
ing these two denominators is one of the most frequent errors in in‐
terpreting rare-event detectors.
Calibration. Agreement between stated probabilities and observed
frequencies of correctness. A system can be calibrated on average and
poorly calibrated on a rare class. Calibration does not certify the fidel‐
ity of explanations or completeness of the prior-work corpus.
103


Brier score. The mean squared difference between predicted probab‐
ilities and observed binary labels. It measures the quality of probabil‐
istic predictions. For novelty, labels must be defined beforehand; the
formula does not resolve ambiguity in the criterion.
Selective classification. A procedure allowing abstention on some
cases. Coverage, risk on decided cases and escalation cost must be
evaluated together. Abstention must neither be automatically penal‐
ized as an error nor excluded in a way that makes performance look
better.
Confidence interval. An interval produced by a procedure with a cer‐
tain coverage property across repetitions of the study, under its as‐
sumptions. It does not automatically mean the probability that a fixed
parameter lies in the particular observed interval. Interpretation re‐
quires attention to design and dependencies.
Effect size. The amount of difference between conditions, expressed
in a relevant unit. Statistical significance alone does not say whether
the effect matters for idea selection. A small effect can become import‐
ant at large scale, but this must be analyzed explicitly.
Statistical power. The probability that a test detects a specified effect
under a model of the data. It is not a universal property of a number
of examples. It depends on effect size, variation, correlations and the
analysis procedure.
Clustering and pseudoreplication. Observations from the same idea
or family may be dependent. Treating all paraphrases and repetitions
as independent can underestimate uncertainty. Analysis must reflect
the level at which real replications exist.
D.2. Language models, representations and
retrieval
Token. The discrete unit processed by a language model. It can be a
word, part of a word or a symbol. Comparing length and perplexity
across different tokenizations requires caution because the units do
not have the same granularity.
104


Embedding. A vector representation of an object, token or passage.
Vector proximity can be useful for retrieval, but is not identical to se‐
mantic equivalence. The training objective determines which similar‐
ities are favored and which differences may be ignored.
Attention. A mechanism for combining information across positions
in Transformer architectures. It enables context-dependent access to
other representations. By itself, it does not guarantee that the model
uses all premises relevant to a judgment.
Autoregressive. A mode of factorization and generation in which
each element is conditioned on preceding elements. It describes the
probabilistic organization of the output, not the full range of opera‐
tions that can be performed in computing that output.
Pretraining. A stage in which the model learns from a broad corpus,
usually through a general objective. Hypotheses about corpus fre‐
quencies must be separated from the effects of subsequent tuning, the
prompt and the tools available during use.
Post-training. Stages that modify model behavior after initial learn‐
ing, for example through demonstrations or preferences. They can
change style and instruction following. An effect observed in the final
model cannot automatically be attributed solely to pretraining.
SFT. Supervised fine-tuning on examples of inputs and desired out‐
puts. For novelty evaluation, example selection and labeling criteria
matter: the model may learn a comparison procedure or cues to the
desired verdicts.
RLHF. A family of learning procedures using human feedback that in‐
volve optimizing a policy. The reward used is a proxy for the intended
preferences or criteria. It must not be assumed that the reward coin‐
cides with the historical truth of a contribution.
DPO. Direct preference optimization through an objective based on
pairs of preferred and dispreferred responses. What matters in this
book is that preferences can change the final policy; we do not attrib‐
ute a universal effect on novelty to the method.
105


In-context learning. Changing behavior through examples and in‐
structions supplied in the prompt, without updating parameters dur‐
ing that interaction. It allows testing local worlds with new symbols,
but does not guarantee learning every rule from a few examples.
Decoding. The procedure that turns model distributions into a con‐
crete output. Greedy selection, sampling and other strategies can pro‐
duce different results. Changing decoding must not be confused with
changing competence in verifying prior work.
Temperature. A parameter that changes the concentration of the dis‐
tribution used for sampling. Higher temperature can increase textual
variation. It does not guarantee conceptual diversity, correctness or
historical originality.
Perplexity. The exponential of average logarithmic loss over the
chosen units. It is a text-prediction metric, dependent on the model,
context and tokenization. It can be a cue to familiarity, but not a direct
measure of novelty.
LLM-as-a-judge. Using a language model to evaluate other outputs or
documents. The criterion, format and sources must be specified.
Agreement with human evaluators on one task does not automatically
transfer to other types of judgment.
Familiarity bias. Sensitivity of evaluation to a match with familiar
patterns when this is not justified by the task criterion. Demonstrating
it requires controlling variables such as clarity and correctness, not
merely observing an association with style.
Learning shortcut. Using a cue correlated with the label instead of
the intended relation. It can produce good results on the usual distri‐
bution and failures when the cue is decoupled from truth. Factorial
experiments attempt to create precisely this decoupling.
Semantic smoothing. A working term for transformations that re‐
duce variation between representations. It is useful when it removes
irrelevant differences and dangerous when it erases decisive assump‐
tions, exceptions or relations. It is not treated as an inevitably negat‐
ive property.
106


RAG. Generation augmented by document retrieval. It adds informa‐
tion accessible at evaluation time, but does not guarantee corpus com‐
pleteness or correct comparison. Retrieval and judgment must be
measured separately.
Lexical retrieval. Search based on terms and surface matches. It can
be valuable for formulas, names and rare expressions. It may miss re‐
formulated prior work, but should not be discarded in favor of a se‐
mantic representation that loses exact details.
Semantic retrieval. Search based on representations of meaning or
context. It helps overcome vocabulary differences. Retrieved similar‐
ity is a lead for comparison, not proof that prior work anticipates the
contribution.
Cross-encoder. A model that processes two texts together to evaluate
their relationship. It can use detailed interactions, but has different
costs from independent indexing. The choice of technique must be
evaluated at the same operational budget.
Late interaction. A strategy in which representations of text parts are
calculated separately and later combined for scoring. It can preserve
more granularity than a single global vector. However, it does not
automatically certify preservation of novelty differences.
Top-k. The first k results according to a retrieval or evaluation score.
Decisive prior work may be absent from this subset even if it exists in
the index. Choosing k is a cost and coverage decision, not a guarantee
of completeness.
Contamination. A situation in which information from the test or
about its answer was available during preparation of the model or
procedure. It can also arise through repeated prompt tuning on the
test. Superficial anonymization does not automatically eliminate it.
D.3. Semantics, circuits, compilers and verification
Syntax. The form of an expression according to a language’s rules.
Two syntactically different texts can have the same behavior. A nov‐
elty detector that measures only changes in form will confuse para‐
phrases with contributions.
107


Semantics. The rule or family of rules through which expressions re‐
ceive an interpretation. For circuits, it can be the computed function;
for a proposal, it can include assumptions and guarantees. Not all nov‐
elty questions are required to use the same semantics.
Extensional semantics. Describing a program through the relation
between inputs and outputs. In its simple form, it ignores cost, intern‐
al structure and some observable effects. It is exact for certain ques‐
tions and insufficient for others.
Observational equivalence. The indistinguishability of two systems
relative to a declared class of observations. If we change the permitted
observations, for example by adding time or disclosed information,
equivalence may disappear. The observational criterion must be de‐
clared.
AST. An abstract syntax tree, representing an expression’s structure
without all graphical details. Tuples play this role in the executable ex‐
ample. The AST enables evaluation and transformations, but does not
by itself guarantee fidelity of translation from natural language.
Intermediate representation. An internal form used between read‐
ing the source and execution or analysis. It can facilitate comparison
and verification. If it loses a relevant difference, later stages cannot re‐
cover it from that representation alone.
Compiler. A procedure that translates between representations with
the aim of preserving specified properties. The analogy with extract‐
ing ideas is useful: what is preserved must be declared. A fluent trans‐
lation is not automatically semantically correct.
SSA. A representation form in which each variable is assigned only
once, useful for tracing dependencies. In a contribution-analysis
graph, a similar discipline can help version results. It does not, by it‐
self, confer truth on stored claims.
Boolean circuit. A finite graph of logical operations on binary inputs.
It has exact semantics over a finite domain and enables exhaustive
verification at small sizes. It is a controlled laboratory, not a complete
model of scientific creativity.
108


Truth table. Enumeration of a Boolean function’s outputs for all in‐
puts. Two circuits with the same table are extensionally equivalent on
that domain. The number of rows grows exponentially with the num‐
ber of inputs.
Witness. A concrete object supporting an existential claim, for ex‐
ample an input on which two functions differ. A witness proposed by
the model must be verified. The existence of a correct witness can be
more informative than an overall originality score.
Counterexample. A case that refutes a universal claim. If a system
claims two programs are equivalent, a single input with different out‐
puts is enough to refute it. The absence of a discovered counter‐
example does not automatically prove equivalence.
SAT. The problem of whether an assignment exists that makes a
Boolean formula true. It can be used to search for a witness of differ‐
ence between circuits. Using a solver requires a correct translation of
the problem; the solver does not verify the text author’s intention.
SMT. Satisfiability in the presence of theories such as arithmetic or ar‐
rays. It extends the types of claims that can be formalized. A proof
within a formalization does not guarantee that the formalization fully
matches an informal proposal.
Miter. A construction that combines two circuits so that its output in‐
dicates a difference between them. Searching for an input that activ‐
ates the output produces a witness of nonequivalence. If complete
verification proves this impossible, we have equivalence in the chosen
formal model.
Soundness, or logical correctness of conclusions. The property of a
procedure not certifying false claims within its formal model. It must
be separated from the model’s fidelity to the text or world being ana‐
lyzed. A sound verifier can receive incorrect premises.
Completeness. A procedure’s ability to identify all true cases in the
declared class. A system can be reliable when it certifies, yet abstain
on many cases. It must not be confused with coverage of a document
corpus.
109


Decidability. The existence of an algorithm that halts and answers
correctly for every instance in a class. It differs from practical feasibil‐
ity. A procedure can be decidable and too costly for large-scale use.
Undecidability. The absence of a total correct algorithm for all in‐
stances of a class. It does not mean that no instance can be solved or
that approximate methods are useless. The limit must be related to
the exact class of problems.
Complexity. The study of computational resources needed as a func‐
tion of input size. For novelty, the cost of searching for prior work,
translation and verification may dominate in different ways. An ac‐
curacy score without cost does not fully describe utility.
Datalog. A language of logical rules used for relations and inference,
often over finite databases. It can represent dependencies or con‐
sequences in a controlled laboratory. The choice of rules and negation
semantics must be explicit; it is not a universal novelty detector.
Logical closure. The set of consequences deducible from a base of
claims under specified rules. If we include all ideal consequences, a
new proof may not add a proposition that was not already implied.
Epistemic progress may concern the consequence’s effective accessib‐
ility.
Abstraction. Controlled removal of details to facilitate analysis. It is
useful only relative to the properties it must preserve. In novelty eval‐
uation, abstraction must be audited for loss of exceptions, assump‐
tions and resource differences.
Provenance. Information about the origin and transformations of an
object or verdict. It includes the source, version and dependencies. It
does not prove truth, but allows conclusions to be verified, repro‐
duced and revised.

D.4. Operational epistemology and experimental
design
Relative novelty. The absence of relevant prior work in a declared
reference corpus under a fixed criterion. It is the form of novelty most
easily made testable. It must not automatically be extended to abso‐
lute historical priority.
Epistemic novelty. A change in what an agent or community knows
or can justify. A new proof can play this role even if the result was im‐
plicit in the axioms. Resources and effective access matter.
Transformational novelty. A change to the rules, representation or
problem space, not merely an additional solution within the existing
framework. Identifying it requires examining the consequences of the
framework change, not automatically rewarding unusual vocabulary.
Anticipation. The relation through which prior work already con‐
tains the relevant elements of the contribution being evaluated. It can
be asymmetric and broader than textual identity. In this book it is an
operational concept, not a legal definition.
Residual difference. What remains unanticipated after explicit com‐
parison with prior work. It may be important, minor or uncertain.
The term requires the evaluator to describe the difference instead of
assigning only an impression-based score.
Construct. The theoretical property we want to measure, such as
mechanism novelty. Metrics are operational proxies for the construct.
Agreement with reviewers may measure community judgment
without fully coinciding with historical priority.
Construct validity. The degree to which a procedure measures the in‐
tended property rather than other variables. A benchmark may be re‐
liable yet primarily measure innovative style. Measurement consist‐
ency is not enough.
Identifiability. The possibility of determining the intended property
from available observations and assumptions. Two worlds with
identical observations and different historical verdicts make the prop‐
erty unidentifiable without additional information.
111


Causal confounding. A situation in which an association between
two variables is influenced by other causes. For example, perplexity
and score may be linked through clarity. A correlation alone does not
identify training frequency as the mechanism.
Intervention. A controlled change to a variable, used to study its ef‐
fect. Rewording text intervenes on presentation; retraining with con‐
trolled frequencies intervenes on exposure. The two do not answer
the same causal question.
Factorial design. Systematically combining levels of multiple factors.
It allows study of effects and interactions, for example whether style
matters only when prior work is absent. The number of conditions
must be balanced against budget and desired precision.
Interaction. A situation in which the effect of one factor depends on
another. An overall average can hide opposing effects under different
conditions. Important contrasts must therefore be defined before‐
hand, not selectively discovered after the results.
Experimental oracle. A condition in which the experimenter sup‐
plies the decisive information for comparison. It is not a claim of om‐
niscience. Its role is to separate access to prior work from the ability
to compare it.
Ablation. Removing a component to estimate its contribution to the
system. Total cost must be controlled as far as possible. A component
may appear useful only because it adds time or extra calls.
Preregistration. Fixing hypotheses, metrics and analysis rules before
the test. It does not forbid exploration, but separates it from confirma‐
tion. It reduces the freedom to retrospectively choose only procedures
favorable to the desired conclusion.
Audit of rejections. Checking a sample of cases eliminated by the fil‐
ter. It is necessary to estimate missed novel contributions. Without it,
we mainly observe false alarms among selections and may overestim‐
ate the system’s utility.
Prospective evaluation. Testing on contributions collected after the
procedure is fixed, before a stable public consensus. It reduces some
forms of contamination but requires follow-up over time and accept‐
ance of labels that remain undetermined.
112


Local falsifiability. The existence of observations that would weaken
a precise claim about a model, representation or domain. It is more
useful than a global thesis protected by continually redefining what
“truly new” means.
Correctability. The system’s ability to revise a verdict when new
sources, interpretations or witnesses appear. It is an architectural and
epistemic property. A credible evaluator must preserve the path
through which its conclusion can be refuted.

How the sources connect to the book’s argument
For foundations in probability and architecture, the entry point is
R02–R06. For the familiarity hypothesis and limits of automated evalu‐
ation, the core is R07–R12. R13–R16 are relevant to building a system
with explicit access to prior work. For measurement and uncertainty,
R17–R21 offer cautions and tools to adapt, not transfer mechanically.
For creativity, combinations and data dynamics, R01 and R22–R24
complete the perspective. R25 delineates a type of formal impossibil‐
ity that must not be confused with the practical difficulty of all tasks.
