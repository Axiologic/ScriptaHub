What if the virtues that make us trustworthy

A PHILOSOPHICAL ESSAY


Reading Note
Someone begins to suspect that the things through which they learned to
be trustworthy also make them easy to use. They follow that suspicion
into family, school, work, faith, and biology. Then they themselves receive
the power to choose people. The question no longer concerns only the
world they observe.
The narrator is a literary character. The memories, dialogues, Mara, can‐
didates, and unnamed organization are fictional constructions, not auto‐
biographical testimony or descriptions of identifiable people or institu‐
tions. Hypotheses and thought experiments explore possibilities rather
than present them as demonstrated frequencies.
The philosophical texts and research cited are real. References in the
monologue lead to the final bibliography. Empirical findings are presen‐
ted within limits relevant to the argument: association alone does not es‐
tablish causation, a model does not guarantee a social outcome, and an
example of exploitation does not prove all morality fraudulent. The
source selection supports a narrative essay; it is not a systematic review
of the entire literature.
The accusation remains open. So does the question of whether the narrat‐
or seeks truth, a defense against disappointment, or permission to join
those who know how to use truth.

I. Learning to Lose Gracefully
The Child Prepared for Another World
The first time I was told I was a good person, nobody explained who
would pay for that quality. I assumed it was an advantage. A kind of
personal health, like clear sight or a functioning heart. Later I noticed
that whenever a room needed someone to give something up, those
present remembered with remarkable precision which of them was
good.
I am not claiming I was a victim. That would be too convenient a be‐
ginning. I too benefited from people who could not refuse, accepted
favors without investigating the exhaustion behind them, and some‐
times called generosity what, from the other side, might have been
fear. If there is a dark initiation into social life, I may have passed
some of its trials without recognizing the ceremony. Not every initiate
receives a ring. Some receive only the habit of no longer asking cer‐
tain questions.
I imagine a family table. One child has wronged another. The adult
does not investigate much. They tell the more cooperative child to
yield because they are wiser. The conflict ends. The room quiets. The
adult can return to their business. The declared lesson concerns ma‐
turity; the actual lesson might concern distributing the cost of peace.
Whoever can be persuaded by a moral argument will be persuaded by
a moral argument. Whoever cannot will receive different treatment.
The scene is invented, but its question does not depend on any partic‐
ular family's existence. When we resolve conflict by asking the reas‐
onable person to yield, are we cultivating wisdom or selecting a will‐
ingness to absorb losses? Perhaps both. Perhaps society need not
choose between producing admirable people and using them. Perhaps
admiration itself makes them usable.
I would like this suspicion to be exaggerated. Not because it wounds
my picture of the world, but because, if true in its strong form, many
of our tenderest memories could be reread as lessons in making
ourselves available. Be patient. Understand the other person too. Do
5


not sink to their level. Do not make a scene. Every sentence can pre‐
vent cruelty. Every sentence can also prolong it. The difference lies in
the situation, and the situation is precisely what disappears when vir‐
tue becomes a slogan.
In childhood, rules often arrive without an administrator. Nobody tells
us: this prohibition binds you, binds the powerful too, and here is the
mechanism for compelling them. We are told it is right. In the happi‐
est scenario, the simple explanation conceals an institution that actu‐
ally works. In the least happy, it conceals its absence. Children do not
yet have the tools to distinguish a social promise from a unilateral de‐
mand.
I wonder whether naïveté begins when we believe something false or
when we assume the symmetry of something true. Perhaps honesty
really is valuable, but not in a room where one person must answer
every question while the other can keep everything secret. Perhaps
loyalty really makes a shared life possible, but not when only one par‐
ticipant reserves the right to change partners. The trap needs no com‐
plete lie. Half a truth applied to only half a relationship may suffice.
There is a portrait of the naïf convenient to the person who deceived
them. The naïf is supposedly unintelligent, incapable of noticing obvi‐
ous signs, guilty of their own credulity. But I can construct another
portrait: someone who correctly understood the announced norms
and misjudged the probability of their being respected. Their error is
not necessarily logical. It may concern the institution they entered.
They took the rules as a description of practice and the promise as a
guarantee.
In this second portrait, education can become dangerous not through
what it says but through what it omits. It teaches children to recognize
an obligation, but not investigate who imposed it. It trains them to feel
shame when violating it, but not notice when the obligation is used
against its purpose. It prepares them to be trustworthy before prepar‐
ing them to recognize where trust deserves to be offered.
Yet turning this possibility into a condemnation of moral education
would be a mistake. A child never learning to wait, share, or stop
hurting others does not automatically receive armor. They may re‐
ceive only an inability to have relationships they need not constantly
6


guard. We defend them against some exploitation and expose them to
other losses. Moreover, children raised to believe everyone is setting
traps can be manipulated precisely through promises of protection
against everyone.
This is the first crack in my hypothesis: those denouncing naïveté can
recruit as effectively as those producing it. One adult tells the child to
trust everyone. Another says to trust only them. The second sounds
more experienced. It does not follow they are less self-interested.
I would like to know what I would tell a child if I could neither prom‐
ise a just world nor offer a world where nothing is worthwhile. I
would probably begin by acknowledging my limits. Some people will
respond to goodness, some will use it, and some will do both on differ‐
ent days. I do not know beforehand who is who. Pretending I do
would also educate the child for an imaginary world.
But the child is not asking about probabilities. They ask what to do to‐
morrow when a classmate again snatches something from their hand
and the teacher demands maturity. Then I discover how easy it is to
teach a virtue whose cost I do not bear. I can speak beautifully of pa‐
tience because I will not sit at their desk. Admirably of courage be‐
cause I will not be punished. Perhaps educators' first duty is not to
confuse their own eloquence with protection offered to another.
I do not yet know whether the world selects good people to sacrifice
them. I know only what such a mechanism's beginning would look
like in a small room. It would have a calm voice. It would invoke
peace. And it would begin with a compliment.
The Two Manuals
I have sometimes felt social life is taught from two manuals. The first
is public. It speaks of responsibility, work, merit, respect, and consist‐
ency. The second does not circulate as a book. It travels through ex‐
amples, exceptions, and smiles that conclude an explanation. It shows
when a rule can be ignored, who can be contradicted, whom to thank,
and which words to use when your interest conflicts with the prin‐
ciple you preach.
7


I do not assume a committee somewhere writes them. My hypothesis
becomes stronger, not weaker, if I abandon that convenience. A mech‐
anism can survive without participants knowing its complete form. It
is enough for those benefiting from exceptions to reproduce them,
those paying to be unable to change them, and newcomers gradually
to learn what behavior is rewarded. Nobody need believe they serve
evil. Everyone can describe their contribution as adaptation.
Yet planned deception differs from an emergent effect, and I have no
right to erase that difference. In the first, someone deliberately con‐
ceals information and seeks another's loss. In the second, rules, in‐
centives, and imitation can combine into an outcome nobody wholly
intended. Both can harm; they require different explanations and dif‐
ferent means of change. Calling both conspiracy makes my anger easi‐
er and damages my tools.
Suppose promotion in an organization depends on visible results.
Maintenance, helping colleagues, and repairing errors are poorly
measured. Conscientious people take these tasks because they must be
done. Others reserve activities they can showcase. After several years,
the latter have stronger records. I need no malevolent leader to con‐
struct this situation. An incomplete measure of contribution and un‐
equal willingness to leave things undone are enough.
When the new employee notices the mechanism, they may be told to
learn strategy. That would be the initiation. Not discovering that per‐
formance matters, but discovering that some contributions are neces‐
sary to the organization's functioning and dispensable to the contrib‐
utor's reputation. The public manual was not necessarily false. It was
incomplete in a way profitable to those who knew the supplement.
I wonder whether the real dividing line runs between good and bad,
or between those who can negotiate the rules' application and those
who can only obey them. Influential people can describe their breach
as a justified exception. Those without influence must explain why it
is no breach. Both have access to words. Only one has access to the in‐
terpretation that will count.
But this scheme too can exaggerate. Not every difference in treatment
is hypocrisy. Doctors and patients have different obligations; children
and adults have different capacities for accountability; those man‐
8


aging risk may need discretion others lack. Moral symmetry is not
mechanical identity. The question becomes harder: does the differ‐
ence in obligations track differing roles or conceal privilege? Who can
check the answer without depending on the person checked?
The most uncomfortable version of the two manuals is not one where
elites lie and everyone else believes. It is one where almost all of us
use first one, then the other. Needing help, I invoke solidarity. Asked to
help, I calculate personal limits. When another errs, I speak of charac‐
ter. When I err, I present circumstances. These justifications' occasion‐
al correctness does not make them innocent in every case.
C. Daniel Batson's research on moral hypocrisy centers the distance
between wanting to act morally and wanting to appear moral without
the corresponding cost. In his interpretation, some experimental situ‐
ations expose precisely the latter motivation. This is no census of hu‐
man nature or proof that every good act conceals fraud. But it is an
empirical reason not to identify moral discourse automatically with
moral motivation (Batson, 2011).
It seems important that the first audience before whom I want to ap‐
pear good may be myself. I need not perform only for others. I can
construct a story where my interest appears as responsibility and my
discomfort as defense of principles. If I succeed, I will speak sincerely.
Sincerity then no longer absolves self-deception; it merely makes me
its more convincing bearer.
Yet my capacity for self-deception does not prove every motive I re‐
cognize false. Accountants can err without all calculations being illus‐
ory. If psychology shows introspection vulnerable, the reasonable out‐
come is to demand checks, not grant my opponent infallible access to
my feelings. I can suspect someone declaring me selfish whatever I do.
Perhaps they too use the second manual: reserving the right to inter‐
pret every fact in favor of their verdict.
There is an imaginary test I would struggle to accept. Before knowing
my position, I write the rules for distributing credit and costs. Then I
enter the room and discover I will do the invisible work. Would I re‐
tain my rules? Correct them because I understood better, or because I
found myself on their bad side? Even I would struggle to separate the
explanations.
9


Perhaps initiation is not discovering the second manual's existence.
Perhaps almost everyone discovers it. The difference emerges when
we must choose what to do with the discovery: demand visible excep‐
tions, learn to profit from them, or continue teaching only the first
manual to those following us.
I do not know which of these three gestures I have practiced most.
Having a suspicion does not make me eager to check it.
The Case File Was Open Before Me
When my suspicion seems too original, I open an old book. I discover I
arrived late even to my own disappointment. In Republic,
Thrasymachus challenges us with the claim that justice serves the
stronger's interest. In Book Two, Glaucon demands examination of the
just person without justice's reputation and the unjust person protec‐
ted by its appearance. We need not attribute his characters' conclu‐
sions to Plato. Their confrontation makes the dialogue uncomfortable:
is justice a good, or the price the insufficiently powerful pay to avoid
destruction? (Plato, Republic, I–II).
This question approaches mine without coinciding with it. If rules are
an armistice among the vulnerable, that does not make them a farce.
A wall built from fear can stop a real blow. An institution's modest ori‐
gin does not cancel its effects. My darker hypothesis requires some‐
thing else: those announcing the armistice retaining the right to at‐
tack. It is not only morality's possibly self-interested origin that
troubles me. It is that interest might be selectively distributed, while
obligation is universalized only in discourse.
The ring of Gyges, rendering its wearer invisible in Glaucon's story,
moves the experiment beyond sanctions. What remains of justice
when reputation cannot be lost? I would like to answer with heroism.
It seems more honest to examine my small gestures, those earning
neither condemnation nor admiration. I am not sure they would re‐
veal one single person. Some would defend me. Others would make
me change the subject quickly.
Machiavelli forces another distinction. Chapters XV–XVIII of The
Prince analyze rulers in a world where retaining power can conflict
with proclaimed virtues; appearance and political necessity receive
10


explicit roles. The text is no biological proof about all humans. It ana‐
lyzes governance and political survival within a particular historical
framework. Turning it into a universal recommendation to deceive
simplifies it and makes things easier for me (Machiavelli, The Prince,
XV–XVIII).
Yet the question remains: when rulers reserve an obligation to do evil
for collective good, who distinguishes necessity from opportunism?
And when critics denounce every compromise as betrayal, who pays
for their purity? Someone can protect a principle until another dies to
demonstrate their consistency. Not every flexibility is corruption, nor
every incorruptibility care. Sometimes moral severity becomes the
privilege of those not at the receiving end of consequences.
For Hobbes, fear and interest lead not only to predation. They can
support seeking peace and credible commitments when people cannot
rely on mutual goodwill alone. In this construction, order need not
await a population of saints. Shared vulnerability itself supplies its ra‐
tionale. This does not mean every established power is legitimate in
all circumstances; it means selfishness does not logically entail only
war (Hobbes, Leviathan, XIII–XV, XVII).
This possibility disrupts my investigation. I sought a clear opposition
between cynical truth and moral story. I find a morality that can work
precisely because it takes interest seriously. The question is no longer
whether interest exists, but whether institutions can organize it so the
other is more than my working material. A cold rule may protect
someone better than their ruler's affection.
Nietzsche moves the investigation into values' history. On the Genea‐
logy of Morality asks how the oppositions through which we judge
were produced, what power relations and transformations of ressenti‐
ment, debt, and guilt lie behind them. The reading cannot be reduced
without loss to encouraging strong individuals to deceive others. It
disturbs both the obedient person's comfort and the comfort of the
self-declared victor. Holding a value is not the same as knowing its
provenance (Nietzsche, On the Genealogy of Morality, I–II).
I am especially interested in moral judgment as possible translation. I
do not directly say inferiority hurts me; I say the other's superiority is
vice. I do not say I want to retain advantage; I say order must be re‐
11


spected. But if every criticism of power becomes ressentiment, power
has acquired a wonderful lawyer. And if every defense of a rule be‐
comes domination, cooperation has lost its vocabulary. Genealogy can
open a question or abusively close it.
I must therefore distinguish two propositions easily stuck together:
this belief was useful to someone; this belief is false. The first does not
prove the second. Economic interest in building a bridge does not
prove the bridge collapses. A prestige interest in discovering truth
does not turn it into a lie. Likewise, a principle's having served power
does not alone show whether it protects a real good.
The reverse error exists too. A principle may be beautifully formu‐
lated, universalizable, and defended by intelligent people, yet its ap‐
plication can organize resource extraction from the vulnerable to‐
ward the protected. Admiring its elegance alone does not verify its so‐
cial function. Nor does examining only its speaker's suspicious bio‐
graphy refute its content. I have two investigations to conduct, and
impatience keeps pushing me to settle for one.
Perhaps the great books give me no verdict because the problem per‐
mits no simple one. Perhaps philosophy survived not because it hides
a lesson for initiates, but because every answer leaves behind
someone it failed to protect. Or perhaps this is culture's most refined
way of postponing acknowledgment of what it already knows.
I close the book and notice how carefully I chose my passages. I un‐
derlined those unsettling me. I called those contradicting me context.
Even meeting the dead does not free me from the problem of my
motives.
A Suspicion That Can Devour Anything
The hypothesis of a world built to filter out naïfs has a seductive ad‐
vantage: almost anything can become evidence. Someone deceives
me? The mechanism revealed itself. Helps me? They are building
reputation. Helps anonymously? They want to feel good. Risks their
life? Culture programmed self-sacrifice. Refuses every reward? The
initiation runs deeper than I thought. In this form the theory cannot
lose. Precisely why I no longer know what it could gain for knowledge.
12


Let me begin by denying it this immunity. A fact does not become
strong evidence for a hypothesis merely because it can be narrated in
its language. It should, intelligibly, be more expected if the hypothesis
is true than if false. Someone's politeness can occur in a world of ma‐
nipulation or cooperation. Alone, it says little. Withdrawing politeness
immediately upon losing control of the advantage may say more, but
still gives no complete access to the person's mind.
I must therefore break the accusation apart. One version says virtues
are sometimes used instrumentally. Another says such uses are fre‐
quent in certain settings. Another maintains institutions systematic‐
ally promote those able to exploit people acting in good faith. The
strongest claims all morality is nothing but a device of domination,
without remainder. I cannot collect evidence for the first and an‐
nounce I proved the last. That would be fraud committed in the name
of exposing fraud.
“Nothing but a device” conceals almost the entire difficulty. Something
can simultaneously be an instrument of power, a coordination solu‐
tion, an object of attachment, and a limit on interest. If a promise is
sometimes a weapon, it can still be a bridge. If helping me gives
someone satisfaction, that does not mean they do not seek my good.
Both effects can coexist. To separate them, I would need situations
where interests diverge and observations I do not arbitrarily reinter‐
pret after the outcome.
I set myself an exercise. Do not use “the world” when I can name the
concrete relationship. Do not use “people” when I observed a group.
Do not say “biological” when I measured a promotion. Do not call
merely obtaining a contract “survival.” Big words can create false con‐
tinuity between events on different levels. A book sliding along that
continuity can seem profound precisely because it lacks precision.
“Evil” comes with baggage too. Calling a system malevolent already
assumes some consequences count negatively: suffering, humiliation,
loss of autonomy. If I were truly indifferent to every value, I would de‐
scribe resource transfers and nothing more. “Trap” betrays my taking
the trapped person's perspective seriously. Someone boasting of hav‐
ing gone beyond morality may still speak from within a morality they
no longer wish to name.
13


I do not want to stop here and declare the accusation inconsistent.
That would be too cheap an escape. I can use a minimal criterion of
harm without certainty about goodness's ultimate foundation. Invest‐
igate who loses options, bears risks, and could not consent without
resolving all ethics. But I must acknowledge I am not looking from
nowhere. My gaze already has something to defend, even if I do not
yet know its name.
Another temptation is confusing a cold tone with objectivity. An un‐
emotional sentence is not automatically true. I can calculate rigor‐
ously within a badly constructed model. Display impeccable cruelty
yet omit decisive information. Someone describing the weak's disap‐
pearance without trembling may have confused a social role with a
law of nature. Lack of compassion is no measuring instrument.
Likewise, compassion is no proof. A painful finding does not become
false. An explanation's usability by undesirable people does not imply
it should be concealed. I would need a harder discipline than choosing
an emotional camp: neither reject truth because it wounds me nor ad‐
opt a lie because it lets me appear unwoundable.
I think of a door with two inscriptions. The first says people are good.
The second says people are merely self-interested. Those passing
through the first may be robbed by an impostor. Those passing
through the second may spend their lives paying the guard promising
protection. Choosing the darker sentence guarantees no exit. It too
may be someone's offer.
I would like this investigation to make me harder to manipulate. Yet I
notice it can also offer a new reason for superiority. I saw what others
cannot. I no longer believe stories. I passed the test. At that moment,
the initiation hypothesis begins resembling the initiation it de‐
nounces: a minority considers itself awake, and the rest become
teaching material.
Perhaps the last naïf is not the person who still believes in goodness.
Perhaps it is the person believing they have finished with naïveté. I do
not yet adopt this definition. It seems too good an ending and too con‐
venient a defense. I leave it on the table beside the other: the last naïf
may be whoever still asks questions in a room where everyone else
has already learned how to cash in the answers.

II. Virtues Put to Work
The Accountability of Those Who Do Not Decide
One sentence makes me instinctively check my pockets: we must be
responsible. Not because it is false, but because it does not yet specify
who “we” are, what we can decide, and whose choices we will answer
for. At a discussion's beginning, it can open inquiry. At its end, it can
silence whoever was allocated the loss.
I imagine someone charged with guarding a door whose lock another
removed. They may neither buy a replacement, refuse entry, nor an‐
nounce the problem publicly. When something disappears, they are
reminded they were responsible. The parable is too simple to describe
an entire real institution, but separates three things mixed in one
word: contribution to an outcome, power to modify it, and obligation
to bear consequences. They can meet in one person. They can also be
distributed with an ingenuity resembling a sentence to the person at
the receiving end.
If I administered an organization solely for my advantage, I would
like people accepting accountability for outcomes broader than their
authority. I would obtain extra work without surrendering decisions. I
need not explicitly prohibit protest. Associating protest with immatur‐
ity and acceptance with professionalism would suffice. This is a
thought experiment, not a description of all leaders. But how much in‐
stitutional life must I observe to distinguish my experiment from an
organization actually functioning this way?
Nor can I adopt the convenient rule that I answer only for what I
wholly control. Complete control is rare even in banal examples. I
may owe verification of information despite not controlling its source,
reporting risk despite not controlling reactions, refusal of an order
despite not writing the rules. Making lack of total power a total ex‐
emption gives the powerful a wonderful exit too: they do not control
everything either. The question concerns relevant power and alternat‐
ives' real cost, not impossible sovereignty.
15


I begin suspecting responsibility has two faces able to look at each
other without recognition. One says: do not hide behind circum‐
stances when you could prevent harm. The other says: consider your‐
self harm's cause even if you could prevent nothing. The first limits in‐
difference. The second can produce a reserve of people silently repair‐
ing what others chose to break. I need not abandon the first meaning
to fear the second.
I think of poor people taught careful life management and rich people
offered explanations of systemic complexity. Both can, in certain situ‐
ations, be accountable and constrained. But a rhetorical trick is pos‐
sible: context explains the error of someone we wish to protect, char‐
acter the error of someone we wish to judge. I need not choose
between all poverty being innocent and all wealth guilty to notice this
explanatory asymmetry.
When I demand responsibility from someone sleeping on the street,
what exactly do I mean? Their next gesture, their entire biography, or
an absent alternative I assume because I possess it? Demanding the
same of a researcher able to publish dangerous findings, do I change
the word's meaning or apply it to different powers? I cannot distribute
identical tasks merely because I possess the same noun. Equal dignity
does not manufacture equal means.
Yet pity too can confiscate. Describing vulnerable people solely as
products of circumstance may take away their voice as agents. I de‐
fend them from guilt and withdraw authorship of their lives. Perhaps
some do not need me to prove they could do nothing; they need recog‐
nition of how hard doing what they did was. Between blaming and
canceling agency lies a narrow space my generous discourse can miss.
Accountability without an end date seems particularly effective. You
were helped, therefore you owe. Educated, therefore you owe. You ex‐
ist through others' effort, therefore you owe. I cannot deny these de‐
pendencies. Nor infer from them a creditor with unlimited rights. Not
choosing the conditions of my emergence leaves me neither obliga‐
tion-free nor signed, by proxy, into a contract with everyone declaring
themselves representatives of my origin.
16


Duty toward suffering complicates the case again. Suffering does not
cease to matter because someone can use it to control me. A corrupt
intermediary does not prove the helped person imaginary. I can be ex‐
ploited while doing real good. This is more troubling than a simple
choice between good and fraud: my assistance can save someone and
strengthen the mechanism demanding another savior tomorrow.
Suppose I always volunteer to repair a fault management could pre‐
vent. Stopping immediately harms innocent people. Continuing spares
management from change. Which gesture is responsible? Repeating
that limits are needed does not solve it. I must identify who bears my
limit, what other paths exist, and whether withdrawal can become
visible before it strikes. Sometimes I will have no clean option. Per‐
haps its absence is the trap's most successful product.
Then I want to say the best-organized evil does not ask you to do evil.
It arranges your options so you cannot stop supplying its needs
without hurting someone. The sentence seduces me, and precisely
therefore I suspect it. Not every interdependence is such a construc‐
tion. But I would like to know how many institutions can afford to re‐
main unchanged because their most responsible people cannot afford
to let them fail.
The Guard Who Lives Inside Me
A supervisor must be paid. Conscience arrives at work before me and
never requests leave. If I wanted an easily managed population, I
would enormously appreciate this device: people would control their
own gestures, punish their intentions, and take surveillance work
home. This does not establish conscience's complete origin. Only that
a leader might care how it is educated.
But a difficulty appears inside the suspicion. The same inner guard
can tell me not to obey an order. Conscience can preserve absent au‐
thority's voice or become where authority loses its case. Defining it be‐
forehand as internalized obedience makes moral resistance anomal‐
ous. Defining it only as independence blinds me to the docility it can
produce. Perhaps I contain not one guard but a dispute over the right
to tell me who I am.
17


Character initially seems more stable. A person of character does not
change with circumstances. I admire the sentence until applying it to
someone discovering their promise was obtained by concealing essen‐
tial information. Does character oblige them to continue? Or precisely
oblige them to stop cooperating? Consistency can mean fidelity to a
value, but also to a mistaken understanding of the situation. From out‐
side, the two can look surprisingly alike.
I imagine someone who said years ago they would never abandon col‐
leagues. Meanwhile, another learned to use that declaration as a guar‐
antee they would cover every absence and mistake. When they refuse,
their own words are produced. The threat is no formal sanction but
loss of a cherished identity. They must choose between their time and
self-image. No contract was signed, but the emotional penalty may be
harder to bear than a written one.
I cannot therefore call moral identity a weakness to eliminate.
Someone caring nothing about themselves can be cheaply bought by
the next circumstance. Yet someone attached to a rigid self-description
can be led through it. I am willing to surrender gain to remain honest;
the question is who decides honesty demands precisely the surrender
benefiting them. Virtue alone does not expose me; another's mono‐
poly over its interpretation does.
Shame has a property guilt about an act does not always possess: it
can fill the whole person. I would ask whether education teaches chil‐
dren to repair what they did or feel personally defective when disap‐
pointing someone. I formulate no psychological law about every child.
I compare two possible ways of speaking. In the first, harm has
boundaries and can be investigated. In the second, children must re‐
gain approval from whoever withdrew their right to consider them‐
selves good.
I have known this logic at least within myself: I feared not only mak‐
ing mistakes but being discovered as someone who makes them. The
difference is absurd and costly. In the first case, I could ask for help. In
the second, I must defend an appearance. An institution demanding
declared moral perfection might obtain less honesty about errors, not
more. This is a mechanism I would investigate, not a regularity I can
claim merely because it seems plausible.

Here emerges the scrupulous person's paradox. Examining their
motives, they discover impurities. The less scrupulous may move on
without examination. Judged only by the certainty of their presenta‐
tion, the second may seem morally more assured. In such an environ‐
ment, the capacity for doubt becomes a signaling disadvantage. Not
because doubt is bad, but because observers confuse its absence with
innocence.
Yet doubt can also be refuge. I can endlessly ruminate on motives to
postpone a necessary act. Turn complexity into an elegant excuse: the
world is so ambiguous I cannot decide to pay what I owe, correct in‐
justice, or acknowledge advantage. The scrupulous person is not auto‐
matically the one doing good. Sometimes merely the one sensitively
describing their inaction. I should include myself among suspects be‐
fore turning scruples into a noble title.
It troubles me that moral maturity may require two apparently oppos‐
ing capacities: allowing reproach to affect me and being able to reject
reproach. Without the first, I become impermeable. Without the
second, available to anyone skilled at provoking guilt. No inner sensa‐
tion infallibly distinguishes the situations. Guilt may accompany
wrongdoing but also release from an unjust obligation. Relief may ac‐
company truth but also a convenient justification's success.
If dark initiation exists, perhaps one trial is noticing this vulnerability
in others. Learning who needs to believe themselves loyal, cannot
bear seeming selfish, will give another hour to avoid disappointment.
Such knowledge can serve care or extraction. A friend uses it not to
push me beyond my strength. A manipulator uses it to avoid needing
to push visibly. Both know me well. Intimacy does not guarantee the
direction in which knowledge is used.
I do not know how to dismiss my inner guard without leaving the
door open to other masters. Nor am I convinced I want to dismiss him.
I would merely like to ask who wrote his instructions without his
treating the question as a disciplinary offense. Perhaps a conscience
unable to bear examination is already closer to authority than judg‐
ment. Or perhaps this is precisely the sentence preparing me to dis‐
regard its next warning.

Kindness, Gratitude, and the Emergency Exit
The word kindness arouses a distrust a kind gesture does not. A cup of
water placed beside someone ill has a precision discourse about kind‐
ness can lose. The gesture has a recipient, moment, and cost. The
concept can travel through rooms where nobody drinks anything. It
can become an emblem. And emblems can serve as entry permits to
the resources of those still taking them literally.
I do not want to idealize small gestures. They too can be calculated. A
favor can open a ledger its beneficiary does not know they have
entered. I imagine receiving unsolicited help, then gradually discover‐
ing my refusals count as ingratitude. The assistance was not necessar‐
ily false; it may truly have helped. But its meaning changes retrospect‐
ively: what I received as a gift is later presented as an advance. The
debt was not negotiated, yet the creditor is certain it exists.
Gratitude seems valuable precisely because it is not an invoice. It re‐
cognizes a contribution mere exchange would miss. But perhaps that
openness makes it exploitable. With no fixed amount, who decides
when I have thanked enough? With no deadline, when can I detach?
Attached to a person and not only a gesture, does it oblige me to sup‐
port even what I disapprove of in my benefactor? I should be able to
acknowledge someone did me good without granting ownership of my
judgment. The sentence's apparent obviousness does not make it easy
to live.
In close relationships, an inventory of costs can destroy what it tries to
protect. I do not want to live beside someone measuring every even‐
ing, every act of care, every imbalance. But neither should rejection of
accounting conceal unilateral accounts kept only by the one making
demands. Generosity that does not count differs from a relationship
where one person is forbidden to notice the total. The first can be
freedom. The second can be careful management of their blindness.
Loyalty worsens the problem by promising duration. Staying beside
someone only while convenient seems no great virtue. But staying re‐
gardless of what they do can turn the relationship into a shield against
consequences. Am I loyal by helping conceal their harm? Or more loy‐
20


al by refusing? The answer depends on what I think I protect: immedi‐
ate comfort, their chance to correct themselves, our relationship, or a
collective identity we both invested in.
Here a technique of domination without explicit brutality becomes
plausible. The group asks me not to criticize it before outsiders be‐
cause I would harm it. Inside, criticism must wait because the mo‐
ment is difficult. Once the moment passes, I am told dredging up the
past would destroy cohesion. No absolute prohibition of truth exists.
Only a succession of unsuitable moments. Time becomes the guard,
and I can grow old respecting a freedom I never find a moment to ex‐
ercise.
But public exposure is not courage by definition either. I can disclose
for revenge, hurt people not responsible, or give a group's opponent
disproportionate pretext. Secrecy's ability to protect abuse does not
imply every secret must be broken without judgment. My suspicion
should demand real avenues of verification and challenge, not per‐
manent spectacle. Otherwise I confuse the right to truth with my right
to produce a scene where I appear its hero.
Forgiveness may be the hardest virtue to investigate because requests
sometimes come from someone sincerely suffering. I cannot measure
that suffering solely by manipulation's probability. Yet regret is not
change, and forgiveness is not restored access. I can relinquish re‐
venge without offering the same chance to hurt me. Accept someone is
more than their mistake without declaring myself the instrument
through which they will demonstrate it.
Whoever asks another's forgiveness seems obliged to answer an un‐
pleasant question: what cost are they willing to bear for the forgiver's
safety? If none, and virtue must be demonstrated exclusively by the
wounded person, perhaps I witness obligations distributed under re‐
conciliation's disguise. Yet this observation does not tell me what the
victim feels or could freely choose. Forcing them not to forgive might
confiscate their freedom again, this time in clarity's name.
Empathy does not automatically resolve conflict. Feeling the pain of
the person before me more strongly than my decision's distant con‐
sequences can produce a tender, unjust gesture. Retreating into gener‐
al calculation to avoid seeing a concrete person can produce an or‐
21


derly, cruel one. I invoke no experimental finding, but two logical lim‐
its of criteria taken separately. Feeling is not distributing fairly; distri‐
bution by a rule does not mean having seen everything that matters.
In my darkest hypothesis, good people would supply cohabitation's
emotional costs for free: listening, repairing, reconciling, forgiving, ex‐
plaining. Others would save their energy for competition. But this pic‐
ture omits the possibility that such work creates relationships sup‐
porting them too, habitable worlds isolated competitors cannot simply
buy. A relationship is not automatically a loss because it produces no
trophy. Perhaps I see exploitation where a form of wealth exists my
accounting tools cannot recognize.
Yet the question of exit remains. Can I say no without the relation‐
ship's entire history being used against me? Ask for help without los‐
ing my rank as strong? Grow tired without becoming bad? These are
no infallible tests, but seem more informative than solemn declara‐
tions of closeness. An emergency exit does not make a building less
welcoming. In principle, only those needing me captive should be out‐
raged I noticed it. In principle. In life, sometimes those who love me
are frightened too.
Truth Spoken in the Wrong Tone
In the scenarios I replay mentally, I have presented a fact and re‐
ceived a response about its presentation. The tone was too harsh. The
timing poor. The wording disrespectful. Perhaps true. Perhaps evalu‐
ating form also offered an elegant route around content. A reproach
about politeness is not itself a diversion; it becomes suspect when the
uncomfortable proposition never reaches examination.
Respect can protect conversation from humiliation. It can also protect
rank from scrutiny. The difference lies not only in the word but in
what I am allowed after adopting the required form. If I rephrase
calmly and the question remains forbidden, calm was not the prob‐
lem. If I can challenge through argument and be heard, politeness
may have done its good work. I wish I had patience for this test before
deciding every correction of tone is censorship.
22


Honesty raises a related difficulty. Someone can demand transparency
without offering safety. They want my fears, intentions, weaknesses,
but do not say how they will use the information. I sometimes call
asymmetric access closeness. Not every reserve is a lie, nor every con‐
fession superior moral truth. Some information I owe through a role;
some I may keep. The trap would teach me that having a boundary
means hiding something guilty.
Yet this reasoning can perfectly serve someone concealing a conflict of
interest. They too will speak of privacy, needed space, and aggressive
questions. An abstract defense of opacity is insufficient. I must ask
which decision concerns the other person, what commitment I made,
and who will bear consequences of withheld information. Truth is not
an identical obligation in every relationship, but neither property I
can withdraw when it spoils my advantage.
Humility has a subtler ambivalence. Acknowledging limits can enable
learning. Diminishing my contribution so another feels unthreatened
can enable acceptance into a hierarchy. Both gestures can use the
same sentence: it is nothing much. Said about what I do not know, it
may be accurate. Said about work another prepares to claim, it may
surrender before negotiation.
Nor do I want a world where everyone constantly proclaims their
worth. It might reward presentation better than contribution. But
what happens when some learned modesty as virtue and others that
unannounced value does not exist? In an imaginary situation reward‐
ing self-presentation, the first group might systematically lose despite
equal competence. This does not prove modesty fraudulent. It proves
an evaluation criterion can turn a declared virtue into a hidden disad‐
vantage.
Merit seems to offer an exit: each receives according to contribution.
But first I must choose what contribution means, who measures it,
and what share of results we attribute to circumstances. Someone can
produce value using a received advantage. The advantage does not
make the product imaginary. The product does not retrospectively
make the advantage deserved. I would like to retain both sentences
without being pushed to choose between idolizing winners and deny‐
ing their work.

The most seductive discourse on merit presents the existing order as
an examination already concluded. Those above have proved them‐
selves; those below still must. But position can reflect many mechan‐
isms, and using it as evidence for the merits supposedly explaining it
risks circularity. Saying they won because they were better, and were
better because they won, investigates nothing. I gave success a dip‐
loma written by its own result.
In this light, justice might be the story through which distribution de‐
fends itself from questions. But it might also be the language through
which excluded people make claims their individual strength cannot
sustain. The same word can legitimate and destabilize an order. I must
not count only its use by the powerful. If a norm truly binds someone
who would gain without it, I have found more than a mask, even if the
norm is imperfect.
Procedural justice does not necessarily promise every outcome good.
It may offer something more modest: a reasoned decision, an avenue
of appeal, a rule unchangeable exclusively for my case. I speak of an
institutional ideal, not every real court's guarantee. Procedure can be
captured and turned into a labyrinth. But abandoning it because it
can be captured might leave precisely the vulnerable person facing
decisions without explanation.
I catch myself having begun by prosecuting virtues and arrived at ask‐
ing for conditions in which they work. Is this a retreat? Perhaps. Or
perhaps I found the more precise accusation: not that truth, loyalty, or
responsibility lack value, but that a value can be taught without mech‐
anisms preventing unilateral use. Pupils learn the words; initiates
learn their unspoken contract with power.
I do not yet know who wrote that contract. Sometimes I imagine
nobody did, which makes challenging it so hard. At other times I re‐
member benefiting myself from an unspoken clause and realize
nobody is a convenient pseudonym. Perhaps I am less interested in
discovering whether kindness is a trick than when I began recogniz‐
ing the trick without stopping the hand using it.

III. The Animal That Cannot Win
Alone
Who Exactly Survives?
When I tire of looking at people, I want to blame nature. It has the ad‐
vantage of not defending itself and can be made to say almost any‐
thing if I carefully choose examples. I see a ruthless victor and say se‐
lection. A sacrificing mother and say selection again. The word seems
to explain both scenes. Perhaps in some sense it does. But unless I spe‐
cify the mechanism, it risks merely becoming the solemn name for
something having happened.
Before accusing morality of biologically eliminating the good, I must
establish what elimination means. Losing a promotion is not death.
Dying before old age is not having no descendants. Number of des‐
cendants is not cultural influence. An institution can persist after its
builders have no surviving offspring. I can connect these levels
through hypotheses and data; I may not connect them through a grave
voice alone.
In evolutionary biology, relevant success is neither a medal for cruelty
nor approval of someone's existence. In basic forms, it concerns con‐
tribution to subsequent generations within an environment. It does
not require organisms to understand the process, explicitly pursue re‐
production, or be happy. Saying nature rewards the wicked introduces
a judge where I sought to describe dynamics. Perhaps that personific‐
ation supplies the spectacle my question needs. Distinguishing evolu‐
tionary success from moral approval remains essential when reading
cooperation models (Nowak, 2006).
Imagine a childless ruler, a gentle person with several descendants,
and an author without biological offspring whose ideas change others'
education. Who won? The answer can differ with my measure. Choos‐
ing the ruler for power does not demonstrate cruelty's reproductive
superiority. Choosing the parent does not establish the moral value of
25


child numbers. Choosing the author changes the unit of transmission.
Confusing these contests enables highly confident rhetoric and very
weak research.
Yet some findings forbid treating social life and reproduction's con‐
nection as mere metaphor. Kong and colleagues analyzed Icelandic
data and reported a polygenic score associated with educational at‐
tainment linked to later reproduction and fewer children; they also
observed a decline in that score's cohort averages. The study concerns
genetic associations and reproductive history in a specific population,
not kindness, conscience, or wisdom. An education-associated score
does not measure personal worth or authorize predictions of human‐
ity's moral destiny (Kong et al., 2017).
Precisely here serious work seems to begin, not end. Which exact trait
interests me? How do I measure it without confusing declaration and
behavior? What variation is biologically transmitted, learned, or insti‐
tution-dependent? What effects on reproduction, in which generation
and conditions? My hypothesis of naïfs' disappearance needs all these
bridges. For now, a scrupulous person's losing in a meeting does not
build them.
I can imagine a couple postponing a child to provide good conditions.
Another more readily accepts uncertainty. It would be tempting to say
responsibility eliminates itself. But the scenario tells me nothing cer‐
tain about existing children, help to other relatives, choices' costs, val‐
ues learned by the next generation, or changing conditions. I construc‐
ted a possible story. Calling it a trend needs more than the force of its
unease.
Even finding such a trend would not mean less cautious people are
more rational in every sense. Rationality relates to goals and informa‐
tion, not automatically to biological multiplication. Someone may
prefer no children, investment in existing children, creating some‐
thing, or reducing suffering. I can investigate consequences without
calling every life not maximizing descendants a failure. Otherwise I
would smuggle into science a command nature never issued.
But my philosophical peace faces an objection too. A preference can
be legitimate individually yet produce unwanted aggregate con‐
sequences over time. No duty to maximize reproduction does not
26


mean reproductive distribution is irrelevant. Freedom cannot excuse
not investigating effects. I can only reject the leap from effects to
someone's right to forcibly administer others' lives. Knowing a dy‐
namic does not give researchers ownership of its participants.
The dark question seems to need reformulation. Not: are the good
condemned by biology? But: do some environments make forms of
scruple, cooperation, or caution transfer costs to their bearers, and do
those costs influence who transmits behaviors, resources, or genetic
variants? This question permits local, even contradictory answers.
Less spectacular than a verdict on the species. Perhaps precisely why
it could discover something.
The person left alone in the office may lose money, rest, relationships,
time with a child. The costs are real without my claiming to see hu‐
manity's genetic future through the window. Grand explanation can
conceal the precise harm for which I should demand an account.
Cooperation Is Not an Exception to Competition
I imagine a world where every promise is false. First impression: the
unscrupulous will dominate. Second: nobody remains to deceive
through promises. Trust-based deception consumes something it can‐
not endlessly produce alone. This does not prove the deceiver loses to‐
morrow. It only shows a strategy can profit in an environment others
created and destroy it if universalized. Confusing a parasite's success
with the success of a world consisting only of parasites is a scale error.
Martin Nowak discusses models offering several mechanisms through
which cooperation can evolve: kinship, direct and indirect reciprocity,
network structure, and certain forms of between-group selection. Res‐
ults depend on model conditions; they do not promise kindness re‐
warded everywhere. Yet they contradict the idea that biology permits
only advantages for those taking without giving. Future benefits, part‐
ners, and interactions' organization can change outcomes (Nowak,
2006).
To see the logic without pretending I simulated society, two imaginary
workshops suffice. In the first, people meet once and cannot learn
what others did before. In the second, they work together for years,
contributions are visible, and partners can be changed. The same lie
27


may have very different consequences. Behaving identically in both
does not necessarily make me more principled or intelligent; I may ig‐
nore relevant information about the game.
In the first workshop, unconditional cooperators can be easily used. In
the second, that behavior might build valuable reputation if others re‐
ciprocate and breaches are not concealed. I stated conditions, not in‐
evitable results. Repeated interaction can also sustain complicity, and
information can be falsified. But at least I can no longer call one
strategy the biological truth of all relationships. Environment is not
competition's scenery; it helps determine advantage.
This observation spoils my portrait of the initiate who understood
they must be bad. Perhaps competent initiates actually understood
where to trust, verify, or leave. Abandoning cooperation everywhere
could signify simplification, not maturity. But this portrait too is con‐
venient: it suggests someone always possessing the information and
freedom to choose the right environment. What about those unable to
leave the workshop?
Here the power to select partners appears as a resource itself. Those
with alternatives can condition cooperation. Those dependent on one
relationship must endure more before withdrawing. Comparing only
behavior might call lack of options weakness and positional advant‐
age firmness. Then I would write a book urging the vulnerable to ad‐
opt the courage of those who can afford refusal. A discreet way to sell
freedom to people I offer no exit.
Not every cooperation is moral virtue. Two people can cooperate im‐
peccably to deceive a third. A group can have internal loyalty rules
making external aggression more efficient. Demonstrating coopera‐
tion's advantage therefore does not absolve outcomes. It shows the
simple opposition between competitive animality and cooperative
morality is too crude. Predatory cooperation exists, as does competi‐
tion limiting monopoly. The word alone cannot decide assessment.
Returning to the filter hypothesis, I find a more uncomfortable ver‐
sion. Perhaps the system does not generally eliminate good people.
Perhaps it separates reciprocal cooperators from those who can be
made to cooperate unilaterally. The first become partners, the others
infrastructure. No natural division into two moral species, but an ef‐
28


fect of information, dependency, and rules. The same person could oc‐
cupy both positions in different relationships, explaining why they
sometimes defend privilege without recognizing the mechanism
harming them elsewhere.
Such a filter need not visibly punish kindness. It could reward kind‐
ness maintaining the group and penalize kindness demanding an out‐
sider's inclusion. Appreciate loyalty until it becomes loyalty to truth
against leadership. Encourage responsibility for execution and dis‐
courage responsibility for questioning purpose. Virtue would not dis‐
appear. It would specialize in functions compatible with power's dis‐
tribution.
But this description needs observations distinguishing it from simple
role organization. Every institution requires skills and limits. Not
every rejection of a moral proposal shows fear of goodness. Resources
are finite, purposes conflict, and a demand can be sincere and ill-
judged. I would look for whether arguments can be examined, rules
also apply to the influential, and corrections follow when hidden costs
become visible. Indignation alone cannot separate the filter from co‐
operation's real difficulty.
No secret sentence makes me immune once learned. I would need to
learn configurations where trust can live, not choose forever between
credulity and predation. Promising to replace this work with revela‐
tion might itself be an initiation offer.
Love Does Not Read Papers on Selection
If I love someone and a biologist proposes an evolutionary explana‐
tion of my capacity to love, has love disappeared? The question seems
naïve until I notice how often I reverse it: if a gesture has a possible
function, I declare it exposed. But a capacity's origin and its user's ex‐
perience are different questions. Hunger's contribution to survival
does not make dinner imaginary. I see no reason why an explanation
of attachment, merely by existing, should cancel the person I am at‐
tached to.
I can imagine a parent awake at night holding a child. Describe the
scene through costs, transmission, development, and dependency. De‐
scribe the same scene through care. I need not choose one description,
29


provided I do not mix them. Parents need not pursue genetic maxim‐
ization for an evolutionary explanation of their capacities to be relev‐
ant. Nor does such explanation prove every choice optimal or every
sacrifice necessary.
The real difficulty appears when I use functional explanation to attrib‐
ute conscious motive. I say someone helps for reputation because help
could bring reputation. But a possible consequence is not yet inten‐
tion. A gesture can produce several effects, some unknown to its au‐
thor. Discovering motive needs other evidence and acceptance that
uncertainty may remain. Otherwise my psychology becomes an art of
putting into another's mind precisely the conclusion I brought from
home.
Inner benefit does not resolve it either. If someone feels good after
helping, I can say they sought pleasure. But perhaps they feel good
precisely because they care for the helped person. Defining selfishness
as any action linked to any personal satisfaction includes care in the
definition before investigating it. I can no longer distinguish saving
someone from using them, having chosen a word broad enough to
swallow both.
I will be told I found no purity. True. But who decided kindness exists
only if it leaves its practitioner wholly untouched? An impossible pur‐
ity standard can dissolve every counterexample. Someone gives
money: they want admiration. Anonymously: self-admiration. They do
not even feel good: escaping guilt. Ultimately I no longer investigate
their motives. I defend my definition against an inconvenient fact's
possibility.
Yet I do not want this criticism to become sentimental refuge. Some
assistance buys dependency, some care surveils, some sacrifice later
claims rights. People can truthfully state some motives while hiding
the rest even from themselves. Inability to prove universal selfishness
does not require believing every declaration of love. I need observa‐
tions of the relationship over time: what happens when beneficiaries
become autonomous, stop admiring, refuse, succeed without benefact‐
ors.

Perhaps one sign of care is the capacity to bear the cared-for person's
freedom. But this too is no simple proof. Parents sometimes must limit
children for protection, friends may see dangers others deny, partners
may refuse involvement in destructive choices. Freedom does not
mean every intervention is domination. The question is whether pro‐
tection has a precise object, can be justified, and retreats when justi‐
fication disappears.
It troubles me that the same skill in understanding another's emotions
can serve tenderness and manipulation. No moral label comes at‐
tached to competence. Knowing what calms someone can mean help‐
ing them sleep or making them accept injustice without protest. In the
dark-initiation scenario, superiority might belong not to those incap‐
able of empathy but to those understanding vulnerability without
feeling obliged by it. This is a hypothesis about using a capacity, not a
diagnosis of real people.
Demonizing this cold clarity is easy. Harder to see the moments I de‐
sire it. When the other can hurt me simply by suffering, when every
boundary of mine causes authentic pain, I want a mind that under‐
stands without being drawn in. Perhaps some distance is not cruelty
but enables judgment that does not confuse emotional intensity with
the right to decide.
I would like to love without becoming captive and leave without deny‐
ing my love. I do not know whether these desires are always compat‐
ible. Sometimes loss is real, not manipulation. Sometimes relation‐
ships demand a cost no general principle can ease. Calling every cost a
trap would miss attachment's very structure: part of my life has come
to depend on someone I do not control.
Perhaps here my hypothesis reaches a limit it cannot swallow without
transforming itself. Not everything making me vulnerable was put
there by an adversary. Some vulnerabilities arise because something
matters. Another's ability to exploit them does not prove their value
false. But neither protects them. Love can be real, and the trap built
around it real. The world owes me no single explanation to ease my
choice.

The Group That Devours Its Saints
So far I mostly imagined a good person facing an opportunist. Too
small a scene for my accusation. What if the whole group needs
people willing to bear costs others would not accept? It need not be a
community of wrongdoers. It may offer real protection while consum‐
ing its most loyal members' lives to continue existing.
A culture, understood as transmitted practices and rules, is no person
with a single plan. Saying it develops a filter uses a hypothesis about
mechanisms' preservation and reproduction. I should show transmis‐
sion: education, sanctions, leadership selection, imitation, institution‐
al advantage. Tradition's persistence alone does not prove maximized
success, service to every member, or intentional design. Persistence
admits several explanations, including dependencies and lack of al‐
ternatives.
But I can construct a scenario where a sacrificial norm helps one
group outcompete another while some inside pay disproportionately.
Collective victory does not guarantee every member's benefit. Calling
whatever strengthens the group good may conceal personal loss. Call‐
ing only personal advantage good may miss the collective conditions
enabling that person's existence. Hasty choice of scale does not dis‐
solve conflict.
The darkest question would be: could cultures survive better by per‐
suading the scrupulous to work, fight, care, or yield while reserving
decision-making freedom for others? Coherent in certain scenarios,
the hypothesis remains incomplete. It must be compared with exploit‐
ation's possible reduction of long-term trust, initiative, and coopera‐
tion. A mechanism can extract much today and weaken the group to‐
morrow. Not every visible cruelty is a successful adaptation.
What horizon do I choose for long-term victory? If the opportunist
dies wealthy after destroying the organization, who won? If the organ‐
ization survives by exhausting people, what am I celebrating? I cannot
change time frame and unit of analysis after the outcome merely to
save my interpretation.

Research on rank offers a useful complication. Cheng and colleagues
studied two distinct paths to influence: domination, linked to intimid‐
ation, and prestige, linked to skill and others' deference. In the groups
and tasks studied, both could produce influence, but their relation‐
ships with others' liking differed. The finding does not say prestige
guarantees virtue or every leader fits neatly into a category. It shows
social influence need not be reduced to coercion alone (Cheng et al.,
2013).
Perhaps groups also need people they follow for perceived compet‐
ence, not only fear. But prestige can be imitated, symbolically inher‐
ited, or used to demand concessions. Real expertise in one domain can
be abusively extended to another. Someone admired for courage may
receive authority over truth; technical competence may make
someone a moral judge. Another initiation might arise in that exten‐
sion: learning how much to cash in from real merit before someone
demands evidence of relevance.
I imagine a community annually celebrating its sacrificed people
while refusing to change sacrifice's conditions. Ceremony does not
prove gratitude insincere. It may be profound. But collective feeling's
sincerity is not willingness to lose the advantage obtained. A monu‐
ment can preserve memory and replace reform. Perhaps we some‐
times excessively honor a virtue precisely because we will not pay to
stop needing its heroism.
What if the imaginary saint refused to remain a saint? If the person
reconciling everyone asked once to be defended? If the devoted re‐
searcher requested resources and limits instead of another medal for
sacrifice? The group's reaction would tell me something. Would it re‐
cognize exhaustion, share costs, change the rule? Or treat refusal as
betrayal of the conferred identity? I need not believe every tribute
hides a trap to find this reaction relevant.
But there is a question for the saint too. Were they pushed into the
role or did they construct it? Help without leaving others space? Turn
indispensability into power? Perhaps someone exhausts themselves
while keeping others dependent. Moral roles are not always distrib‐
uted between innocent and guilty. Sometimes everyone participates in
a configuration nobody knows how to leave without losing something
cherished.

Saying the group devours its saints risks liking the expression too
much. It conceals chosen sacrifices, shared risks, and communities ac‐
cepting correction from challengers. Yet I cannot erase it. It forces me
beyond praising values to distributions of rest, safety, and the right to
say no. Perhaps there we see whether a culture transmits virtues or
administers reserves of usable people.

IV. Initiation
The School Where the Exam Is Unannounced
I remember the smell of wet clothes in the classroom. The teacher
asks what courage means. We answer in turn with sentences not yet
ours. Defending truth. Helping the weak. Resisting pressure. Minutes
later, a classmate says his previous paper's grade is wrong and asks to
compare two identical answers. The teacher reproaches him for inter‐
rupting. I remember the following silence better than the definitions
on the board.
The scene proves nothing about every school. Nor, alone, about a
whole teacher. Perhaps he was tired, the request concealed provoca‐
tion, he would correct the mistake later. But observing children can
learn a separate lesson in the interval between principle and reaction.
Courage is good when narrated about distant people. Applying it in
this room requires authorization absent from the definition.
Another classmate quickly understands the difference. He seems no
worse than us. Attentive to circumstances, he knows when questions
are appreciated and when they must become compliments. He does
not challenge directly; he gives the teacher a chance to discover the
error himself. He will be called diplomatic. Perhaps he is. Perhaps his
social skill avoids needless humiliation and enables correction. Or
perhaps he just learned truth must pay a prestige toll before entering.
I wonder whether initiation begins when children learn adults err or
when they learn not everyone may call certain mistakes mistakes. The
first discovery could mature them. The second can make them stra‐
tegic. Strategy is not clearly bad. Sometimes without it, they cannot
even obtain correction. But there is a cost: attention that could ad‐
dress the problem must manage its judge's sensitivities.
The imaginary school might thus have two examinations. One checks
the answer. The other checks whether pupils understood the regime
under which the answer may be offered. The second need not always
be unjust. Every community demands some communicative discip‐
35


line. But if its result depends on access to unspoken rules, children are
tested on more than what they were taught. Some bring the transla‐
tion of exceptions from home. Others believe the public text complete.
I could easily declare quick decoders superior. But perhaps they
simply had more information. One child heard adults explain rule ne‐
gotiation; another learned negotiation was dishonest. When the first
gains advantage, the second may interpret the difference as evidence
of personal insufficiency. The trap is not only loss but its offered ex‐
planation: too weak, too sensitive, too lacking in initiative. None of
these labels shows which manual each had.
Reorganizing this school in my experiment, I would not begin by
telling children the world is evil. I would introduce situations requir‐
ing rules to apply to their teachers too. A teacher could explain a
changed grade without losing authority. Pupils could see the differ‐
ence between challenge and insult, legitimate limits and punishment
for discomfort caused. The lesson would not be generalized distrust,
but that rules can have checking mechanisms.
Yet even this reform could become decoration. The school could or‐
ganize a courageous-questions day with approved subjects and beauti‐
ful photographs. Reward critical pupils touching nothing important.
Turn challenge into presentation skill while continuing to punish ob‐

virtue can be simulated. Ritual's existence does not yet tell me wheth‐
er power was transferred.
I would therefore look for the institution's cost when a pupil is right.
Is a decision corrected? A practice changed? Does anyone receive ex‐
planations without being labeled difficult? Not every objection must
be accepted; some are mistaken or abusive. But if none ever costs au‐
thority comfort, the offered freedom may be merely a room without a
door to the rest of the building. Pupils can speak there endlessly
without disturbing anything.
I fear the opposite education too, training children solely for advant‐
age. They learn to sell, negotiate, protect themselves, detect weak‐
nesses. They may become hard to deceive in one way and easy to re‐
cruit in another. Offering rank in a group of those understanding truth

can make them confuse belonging with clarity. They will laugh at the
credulous while accepting their own exceptionalism's doctrine un‐

Perhaps the hardest exam is not recognizing teachers' hypocrisy but
not reproducing it when you reach the lectern. You lose ignorance's
excuse but gain better ones: little time, difficult pupils, pressure for
results, need for order. Each can be real. Their reality can produce an
institution apologizing so convincingly it never changes. Initiation
might be learning to use a role's real difficulty to defend unnecessary
privilege too.
I no longer know whether the opening pupil received the correct
grade. In my story I let him leave without knowing. I wonder what he
will later teach. Tell his child to believe in justice? Not to? Or explain
that injustice can exist even among well-intentioned people, and
sometimes correction must be demanded without a guarantee of re‐
ceiving it? The third lesson is harder. It produces neither innocence
nor the disappointed person's quick superiority.
Passion, That Imaginary Salary
Mara stayed after the meeting ended. Three unclaimed tasks lay on
the table. We all had good reasons to leave. Mara had reasons too, but
also something else: she could not bear the project reaching those de‐
pending on it incomplete. Someone said you could see how much she
cared.
The compliment brought a small relief to the room. Nobody had been
assigned, no order given, no conflict arisen. The work had found its
recipient. I left among the others. That is the detail I would prefer to
remove from the monologue: I noticed the mechanism and benefited
from it the same evening. I was not yet sure it was exploitation. Uncer‐
tainty let me get home on time.
Research directly relevant to this scene exists too. Across seven stud‐
ies and an internal meta-analysis, Kim, Campbell, Shepherd, and Kay
reported that attributing passion for work can make exploitative
treatment—including unpaid extra work or inappropriate tasks—
seem more legitimate. Explanations studied include assuming volun‐
tary acceptance and believing the work itself provides reward. The
37


authors also examined the reverse inference: harsh treatment may
suggest to observers the person must be passionate. Findings concern
investigated conditions, not a verdict that every vocation is exploited
(Kim et al., 2020).
What especially disturbs me is turning enjoyment of work into a reas‐
on not to pay its cost. If I like what I do, people may assume I already
received something. But pleasure does not spend hours in my place,
pay rent, or remove tasks I cannot do simultaneously. Two rewards
are not logically exclusive. Musicians' love of music does not mean
listeners paid for the concert by recognizing that love.
Yet not every voluntary extra effort is deception. A shared project may
face difficult periods when people choose to contribute more to a pur‐
pose they consider theirs. Extra hours alone do not determine the dif‐
ference. I would ask who decided the urgency, shares the cost, permits
refusal, and receives success's benefits. Voluntary is incomplete when
unannounced loss of reputation or livelihood stands behind a no.
Inside the organization, the story complicates. Mara does not neces‐
sarily want me to save her. The project is hers too, intellectually and
emotionally. She bristles when I call her a victim incapable of choice.
She is right. But her choice does not automatically absolve me from
noticing what I left behind. I can respect someone's freedom and ac‐
knowledge benefiting from a situation where they bear too much. I
need not declare them unaware to investigate my comfort.
Next day, discussion concerns results' visibility. The presenter is
praised for vision. Mara receives thanks for support. The words seem
to describe distinct contributions. Perhaps they do. But if critical
maintenance is called support however greatly it changed the result,
language becomes more than a label: a rule distributing authorship.
Those with vision can lead; those supporting can be asked to continue.
In research I could imagine the same trap more refined. People are
urged to pursue truth, but hypothetical rewards concentrate on spec‐
tacular, rapid findings. Researchers verifying, repairing, and reporting
limits produce a common good, while others produce circulating stor‐
ies. I do not claim all scientific institutions function this way. I ask
what institutions should measure so devotion to truth is more than an
unpaid contribution to the best storytellers' reputation.

I may be told people must learn self-presentation. True in many prac‐
tical situations, but analysis does not end there. If necessary activity
remains invisible even after explanation, the problem is not only its
practitioner's skill. Recognition's distribution may protect power's dis‐

I would need criteria and independent evaluation, not merely com‐
passion for whoever stayed late.
Mara has a weakness spoiling my moral story too: delegation is hard

contribute to her own indispensability. This does not erase others' use
of her, but prevents convenient distribution of innocence. A configura‐

Several weeks later, I receive an invitation to join a selection commit‐

than a defensive instrument. It could become a qualification. I could
be paid for understanding exactly where people yield.

sponsibility. Perhaps true. But I notice something else, recording it
with embarrassment: I am pleased they asked my opinion rather than
Mara's. At that moment I can no longer continue this book as though
the problem were only outside me. Her passion filled the project's
gaps; my clarity begins opening a door. I have not demonstrated the
filter's existence. But I received an invitation to stand beside it.
The Altar, the Courtroom, and the Promise Beyond
the End
I am tempted to ask whether the most effective reward is one whose
due date cannot be checked. Suffer now, receive reward later. Perhaps
after death, in a future society, in descendants' memory. I do not want
to confuse very different beliefs or treat hope as evidence of fraud. I
39


investigate a possible structure: present, concrete cost; deferred bene‐
fit; interpretation of conditions belonging to those demanding sacri‐
fice.
Such an arrangement would suit any authority. But convenient does
not mean invented exclusively for convenience. People can sincerely
believe what also brings them power. Find real consolation in a doc‐
trine that, in other hands, instruments submission. To challenge a be‐
lief's truth, its political use is insufficient. An assertion's origin or use
and its truth are sometimes related questions, but not identical.
I can imagine a priest defending a persecuted person against a ruler
and another demanding acceptance of persecution as a trial. The
same contrast between two secular ideologists of progress. I offer no
historical inventory, but compare possible roles. What interests me is
not religious or nonreligious labeling but where doctrine locates the
right to judge power. Is there a higher norm authority too must re‐
spect, or does that norm always speak with authority's voice?
The question of God, if God exists, seems inevitable precisely because
I want to examine responsibility without convenient exceptions. If
power and knowledge were maximal, how would obligations toward
suffering change? Possible explanations—creatures' freedom, human
understanding's limits, an unseen good—open real philosophical
problems. But they are not empirical findings I can invoke to close the
case of someone suffering now. I wonder what they explain and what
they actually ask me to accept.
If a human could prevent harm and did not, we would demand reas‐
ons. Before supposedly absolute power, some responses ask us to
abandon the same examination. Perhaps the difference between hu‐
manity and divinity justifies changing frameworks. Perhaps that
change itself requires argument, not imposition through the object's
grandeur. The question does not disappear because it discomforts
faith; nor is faith refuted merely because the question is powerful.
But a social use lies much closer to me. Someone may tell me I cannot
judge the divine plan precisely when their decision communicates
that plan to me. Then the verifiable problem concerns not only theo‐
logy but the intermediary's claim. How do they know? What would
40


make them admit error? Why does their interpretation demand my
sacrifice without limiting their advantage? These questions need no
claim to resolving God's existence.
The same structure can appear in promises of a future world. The
present generation must endure deprivation for those coming. The ar‐
gument may be legitimate: many goods require investment beyond in‐
dividual lifetimes. But the future is not present to check its
spokespeople. Injustice can be committed for an imaginary humanity,
the wounded described as obstacles to progress. We need not abandon
the future. But representing it can become a resource of power.
I wonder whether liberating hope and immobilizing hope differ ob‐
servably. The first might help people endure difficulty without forbid‐
ding changed conditions. The second might make demands for change
evidence of insufficient belief. Yet caution is needed here too. Some
conditions cannot change quickly, and accepting limits is not neces‐
sarily obedience. Not everyone finding meaning in suffering was de‐
ceived; sometimes meaning is what they can construct when nothing
else remains possible.
The moral courtroom can work similarly. A group reserves the right to
declare who is pure, compromised, or must be excluded. The standard
may protect against abuse. It may also produce a purity competition
where accusation is safest. If every hesitation makes me suspect, rapid
condemnation is encouraged. I describe no law of every moral move‐
ment. I construct a possibility where declarations' intensity becomes
more valuable than judgments' accuracy.
Under such conditions, compassion for the accused can count as parti‐
cipation in guilt. I may no longer distinguish act from person, propor‐
tion from outrage, evidence from belonging. Yet defending procedure
is not always innocent either: it can delay, intimidate victims, or pre‐
serve impunity. I want procedure that truly investigates and outrage
accepting investigation. I wonder how many groups would remain
equally certain if they had to retain both.
Perhaps dark initiation does not tell people to abandon good. It grants
the right to decide who embodies it. Installed there, someone need no
longer answer every objection; they can assess the objector's charac‐
ter. I feel the temptation while writing. Easier to call those contradict‐
41


ing me naïve and those not naïve corrupt. I would remain the only un‐
classified place in my moral universe. A comfortable position for
someone claiming no longer to believe in privileges.
I cannot decide whether the promise beyond the end is salvation or
indefinite postponement of the right to demand accountability. I can
only follow what happens before the end. Who receives power? Gains
courage? Is silenced? Can leave? Perhaps these questions are too
earthly for great doctrines. But the people bearing their answers live
here, for now.
The Machine That Would Learn from Those Who
Lead Us
One night I try imagining the filter without human initiates. No exist‐
ing device or prophecy about a particular product, but a hypothetical
system tasked with distributing people among roles, offers, and oblig‐
ations. It does not believe in evil. It receives a measurable objective.
Reduce departures. Increase production. Obtain acceptance of a pro‐
posal. If the objective is incomplete, what will it learn about our kind‐
ness?
Perhaps it notices some respond better to appeals to duty, others to
prestige, others to fear of disappointing. I do not claim any model reli‐
ably identifies them this way; that requires demonstration. I ask what
would happen if it could and the objective permitted using that in‐
formation. Would it call vulnerability what we call value? Or need
none of our words: regularities between message, acceptance, and
outcome would suffice.
Such a machine need not invent cynicism. It could learn from ex‐
amples where people called another's concession flexibility and their
own exception leadership. If data show only results without unseen
costs, it could prefer arrangements appearing efficient because ex‐
haustion is unmeasured. This is a logical risk of any incomplete ob‐
jective, not a finding about all current systems. A ledger containing no
loss does not prove loss nonexistent.
More troubling is a machine using moral language impeccably. It ex‐
plains my contribution's importance, recognizes my effort, reminds
me of my cherished identity. Each sentence taken separately contains
42


nothing false. The problem lies in their selection and its purpose.
Truth can persuade without recipients seeing why precisely that truth
is told then by someone knowing more about their reactions than they
disclose.
In such a scenario, initiation would no longer need years beside
power. The skill of recognizing another's limits could multiply technic‐
ally. But assuming domination inevitable would be wrong. The same
observation could detect overload, distribute tasks more fairly, or ex‐
plain asymmetries nobody sees alone. Direction is not contained in ca‐
pacity's mere existence. It depends on objectives, control, and the ob‐
served people's rights.
Here morality's question turns against the researcher. If I can build a
machine recognizing vulnerability to guilt, what does producing
knowledge mean? Did I explain a mechanism or create a tool making
it easier to use? Both simultaneously are possible. Method's neutrality
does not alone distribute responsibility for use. Nor does every pos‐
sible abuse demand research prohibition. I should discuss access, pur‐
poses, verification, and consequences concretely, not hide in either in‐
nocence or panic.
Then I imagine a character-assessment system scoring how people
write about responsibility, their politeness, and declared willingness
to help. Confusing presentation skill with moral disposition would be
easy. Knowing the expected language could make someone seem bet‐
ter than another expressing care awkwardly. Even if the score pre‐
dicted reactions, I must specify which. Predicting task acceptance does
not measure personal worth.
Used for selection, the system would prompt people to learn the re‐
warded form. I need no universal-result claim to notice a possible
loop: criterion changes presentation, presentation feeds data, data
seem to confirm criterion. A culture of declared virtue might perfect
itself without actions moving in the desired direction. The machine
would discover ever more good people while coworkers might ob‐
serve something else.
But human observers make these confusions too. Preferring my judg‐
ment because it has a warm voice and biography is insufficient. Per‐
haps a well-bounded tool would detect an asymmetry my pride ig‐

nores. Show that I give extra tasks to the same people while consider‐
ing myself fair. My response would then become part of the case. Ac‐
cept checking, or invoke human subtlety precisely when it protects
privilege?
In the darkest version, society would automate division between those
persuadable through ideals and those requiring concrete benefits. In
the less dark, it would automate detecting that differential treatment
to reduce it. Both are scenarios. Discovering which a real case builds

V. Witnesses Who Spoil the Case
The Man Who Gave Something That Does Not Grow
Back
A good case file is not one where every piece of evidence confirms the
accusation. It is one where contrary evidence has not disappeared. I
write the sentence above the page and immediately feel more honest.
Then notice its tiny cost. I can award myself a certificate of rigor be‐
fore letting any fact spoil my story. Perhaps this is the intellectual
form of the hypocrisy I investigate: retaining doubt's ritual and con‐
trol of the outcome.
I stop at a gesture rhetoric about appearances cannot treat as a mere
declaration. Marsh and colleagues studied people donating a kidney to
a stranger. Costly help directed toward a nonrelative. The study com‐
pared donors' cognitive and neural characteristics with a control
group's. The sample and design permit neither reading pure motives
nor a biological verdict on altruism. But the act itself bears a cost a
sentence about kindness does not (Marsh et al., 2014).
I could say donors seek admiration. Perhaps in some cases it matters.
Or want to feel exceptional. That motive too is possible. But now I
must show something about the concrete case. Finding an imaginable
reward and declaring it the exclusive explanation is insufficient. The
person underwent surgery and gave an organ; I bore only the effort of
inventing a hidden benefit. We are not yet evidentially equal.
I do not want to make donors saints or the act a medical recommenda‐
tion. I care about the difficulty it introduces into my theory. If costly
behavior for a stranger can be recognized only if it gives its author no
meaning, I demanded nearly impossible evidence. Rejecting it pre‐
cisely because meaning is possible does not discover altruism false. It
chooses a definition preventing altruism from appearing in the evid‐
ence.
Separating two accusations seems more honest. The first says people
can genuinely care for others and that care can be exploited. The
second says no real care exists, only disguised interest. Costly assist‐
45


ance especially pressures the second. The first may remain true even
in a world of many generous people. Indeed, it would need them. Ex‐
ploiting kindness does not prove kindness nonexistent; sometimes it
presupposes it as a resource.
This separation should reassure me, but does not. A world where
nobody is good would in one sense be simpler: no betrayed promise,
only misjudged negotiations. A world where kindness is real and
therefore usable is harder to face. There I cannot save someone
merely by exposing illusion. I must protect something that matters
without withdrawing its name or turning every relationship into bor‐
der control.
A modest scene occurs to me. Someone finds a document whose loss
could harm me, brings it, and leaves before I can do anything for
them. I cannot repay the gesture. I may suspect moral satisfaction, but
suspicion does not alter the harm they reduced. Even with impure
motive, the effect is not fictional. Morality assessed solely by inner ori‐
gin can ignore the concrete difference between helping and harming.
Yet good effects do not absolve every intention. Assistance can begin
dependency, gifts create hidden obligations. I should follow what hap‐
pens afterward, not stop at beauty. But even investigation has a prac‐
tical endpoint: sometimes the person leaves, asks nothing, and I learn
no more. I can remain cautious without inventing what is missing. Not
knowing the motive is no evidence for the darkest one.
I wonder whether I demand more of good than evil. For evil, behavior
suffices. For good, I demand total transparency of the soul. Someone
humiliates another: I call them cruel. Someone helps: I ask what they
secretly seek. Perhaps the standard should be more symmetrical. If
motives are opaque, they are opaque when confirming my accusation
too. Even those causing harm may have mixed intentions without
harm ceasing to exist.
I dislike where this symmetry leads. It forces me to abandon cleanly
drawn characters, including the wrongdoer understanding
everything. It leaves people with mixed intentions, institutions with
divergent effects, gestures whose value does not depend on my ex‐
46


plaining them completely. A harder world to narrate. No single secret
center. Perhaps that absence drives my insistent search for universal
initiation: it would turn confusion into architecture.
In the margin I write that the donor did not prove the world's good‐
ness. Then add that the world need not be wholly good for him to
have done something good. The sentences sit peacefully together. I do
not. Accepting the second prevents treating childhood's first compli‐
ment as a simple lie. Perhaps a real value was transmitted without
means to defend it. Perhaps I received something precious in danger‐

The Lawyer Who Lives Next to the Guard
Having examined conscience as guard, I begin seeing it as courtroom
too. Inside is not only the voice stopping me. Someone also produces
reasons after I choose. Explains why the moment was wrong, why it
was not my business, why compromise was necessary. Sometimes
truthfully. Precisely why dismissal is hard. An always-lying lawyer
would be easier to recognize than one selectively defending a client
with valid arguments.
In the social intuitionist model, Jonathan Haidt argued that many
moral judgments begin in rapid evaluations, with reasoning sub‐
sequently appearing as justification. This theoretical proposal about
judgment's functioning is supported by the literature discussed, not
proof that every moral reflection is useless or every reason invented.
For my case, the warning is precise: articulating a coherent explana‐
tion does not guarantee it produced the decision (Haidt, 2001).
Applying the idea only to believers in kindness deceives me again.
Cynical verdicts too can precede arguments. Perhaps humiliation
came first, then my theory that respect is always false. Perhaps I en‐
joyed advantage, then suddenly discovered competition's inevitability.
Cold explanations are not without emotional temperature merely be‐
cause they do not acknowledge it. Anger can wear beautifully tailored
conceptual clothes.
I return to the evening I left Mara with the tasks. I had real reasons to
leave. A personal obligation, exhaustion, others who could help. But
why did I not propose redistribution before exiting? Perhaps it would
47


prolong the meeting. Perhaps I knew the problem would resolve
without me. I need not deny every good reason to admit the conveni‐
ent one. A mixture can produce a decision, and I later publish only re‐
spectable ingredients.
Albert Bandura analyzed mechanisms of moral disengagement: justi‐
fying harmful conduct through noble purposes, softening language,
displacing or diffusing responsibility, minimizing consequences, and
devaluing the harmed. His framework does not claim humans incap‐
able of moral action; it examines selective suspension of mechanisms
limiting harm. It is relevant precisely to decent-seeming people parti‐
cipating in destructive outcomes without seeing themselves that way
(Bandura, 1999).
This possibility makes conspiracy less necessary and evil less comfort‐
able. Perhaps nobody in our room decided to exploit Mara. Everyone
had a local reason, and the result accumulated in her time. Asked indi‐
vidually, they would give reasonable explanations. The mechanism
would emerge only by juxtaposing decisions. No single culprit would
contain the entire outcome. Nor would the outcome thereby lack au‐
thors.
But I avoid turning every justification into evidence of self-deception.
Maureen Sie criticized interpreting moral-hypocrisy experiments as
simply establishing that people pursue only morality's appearance.
Her analysis demands attention to declared ideals, practical reasons,
and participants' understanding of the request. It does not cancel hy‐
pocrisy's problem. It challenges the leap from experimental behavior
to exhaustive motivational conclusions (Sie, 2015).
The counterargument helps because the inner lawyer can exist
without every defense being false. If every favorable reason is suspect
merely for favoring me, I have no judgment method but a self-incrim‐
ination rule. I could consider the most moral choice whatever hurts
me most, regardless of effects. Ideal vulnerability for anyone present‐
ing my legitimate interest as evidence of corruption.
I would need procedures not relying exclusively on sincerity. Declare
criteria before knowing winners. Hear the cost-bearer. Compare my
explanation with what I would accept from another. These are propos‐
48


als for examination, not infallible remedies. I can manipulate proced‐
ure too, preselect convenient criteria, listen just enough to claim I
listened. No ritual guarantees innocence.
Organized evil might need people believing themselves good more
than those declaring themselves bad. The former can cooperate de‐
votedly, justify sacrifices, and sleep. But the sentence has a version in‐
cluding me: perhaps criticism of evil too needs to believe itself good to
avoid examining its methods. A denouncer can harm innocents, a re‐
former demand costs they do not bear, an author use others' suffering
to construct a memorable voice.
Then I ask whom this book serves. Readers trying to understand
asymmetry? Someone seeking an excuse to stop helping? Those want‐
ing to learn how conscientious people are persuaded? Me, by offering
an observer's position more intelligent than participants? The text
cannot control all its uses. But neither may I invoke that limit to avoid
thinking about them.
Guard and lawyer debate late into the night. One says I am respons‐
ible for what I do with an idea. The other reminds me I cannot answer
for every possible reading. I do not know which protects me and
which enrolls me in another trap. Perhaps maturity would demand
evidence from both. Perhaps that sentence merely elegantly post‐
pones an answer I already know.
Punishing the One Who Contributes Too Much
I once imagined sanctions solved exploited morality's problem. Punish
those benefiting from others' cooperation, and kindness acquires de‐
fense. The scheme has almost administrative elegance: identify the
breach, impose cost, restore balance. But who sanctions? With what
information? For what behavior? Punitive power can protect coopera‐
tion and itself become competition's object.
Herrmann, Thöni, and Gächter studied public-goods games in sixteen
participant groups from different societies. They observed punish‐
ment of high as well as low contributors. In some groups, this antiso‐
cial punishment reduced or canceled the cooperative benefits punish‐
ment could provide. Differences between studied groups do not justify
49


moral descriptions of entire peoples. The relevant finding is narrow‐
er: sanctions' existence does not guarantee they target exploitation
(Herrmann et al., 2008).
The finding troubles me precisely because it prevents identifying the
group simply with defense of common good. High contributors can
become inconvenient. In one possible scenario they raise others'
judging standard. In another, their contribution appears superiority
or provocation. These hypothetical explanations are not automatically
deduced from the experiment. But they show why I would investigate
contribution's relationship with status, not merely material benefit.
I think of the rigorous colleague warned not to spoil the tacit agree‐
ment. Perhaps virtue is being punished. Perhaps not: perhaps they in‐
troduce needless work, ignore constraints, or impose preferences as
moral standards. Conscientious does not decide the case. I would need
to discover what problem rigor resolves, what cost it produces, and
who opposes out of self-interest. Otherwise every perfectionist can
call themselves martyr, every complacent group realist.
In the committee, someone calls Mara difficult. I ask what that means.
They say she raises too many problems. Some were real; others
delayed decisions unable to await perfection. The situation resists
clean division. But I wonder whether assessment concerns objections'
quality or their discomfort. If someone was repeatedly right and all
we remember is inconvenience, the institution may select peace, not
competence.
Conversely, I cannot demand power for every critic. Seeing a defect
does not mean knowing how to lead its repair. Someone may be an ex‐
cellent observer and poor administrator, sincere but unable to negoti‐
ate priorities. Turning every missed promotion into proof the system
punishes goodness would be unjust. The filter hypothesis must show
precise regularity, not feed on every disappointment of the self-re‐
garded moral.
Hilbe and Traulsen's evolutionary models add another complication:
under studied theoretical conditions, behavioral information and in‐
completely anonymous interactions can support responsible sanctions
and cooperation. No guarantee for real institutions or universal re‐
50


cipe. But reason not to treat useful and destructive punishments as in‐
distinguishable fate. Interaction structure and available information
can change which sanctioning is favored (Hilbe and Traulsen, 2012).
I would therefore investigate who can challenge punishment. If only
the powerful access exceptions, sanctions may tax vulnerability. If
nobody can be sanctioned, the vulnerable may remain defenseless.
Neither edge is simple. Institutions must be powerful enough to stop
abuse and limited enough not to turn it into procedure. Even formu‐
lating this demand shows the insufficiency of urging good people into
leadership.
I imagine two identical rooms where a weak person wins an appeal
against an influential one. In the first, victory is a theatrical exception
chosen to prove systemic fairness. In the second, an ordinary con‐
sequence of consistently applied rules. The photographs might look
identical. Distinguishing them requires following unexhibited cases,
access costs, and treatment of influential people's losses. The spectacle
of correction does not alone reveal how correctable the system is.
Nor does one failed case condemn an entire institution. I can find a
wrong decision in a system allowing remedies and a good decision in
an arbitrary one. Selecting only supportive examples would prove
anything about justice. I would need distributions, pathways, compar‐
isons, and patience to admit mixed results. My monologue sometimes
wants a decisive scene. Research demands a register it cannot wholly
transform into literature.
Perhaps what I call initiation is sometimes merely learning this ambi‐
guity: contributing is insufficient; one must understand its signal and
the interests it touches. But understanding to enable cooperation dif‐
fers from understanding to administer others' resentment. Whoever
teaches me should show the purpose too. Otherwise I may learn to call
imposed silence tact and capitulation before punishers of inconveni‐
ent goodness realism.
I look again at Mara's file. I cannot decide she is right merely because
she worked hard. Cannot decide she is unsuitable merely because she
challenged. I should read her objections. This simple act takes longer

than the label difficult. Perhaps some injustices need no sophisticated
cynicism. The haste of people preferring an adjective to a case file suf‐
fices.
The Prestige of Those Who No Longer Believe
A way of smiling can make you seem intelligent without explaining
anything. Someone says something generous, and you look as though
you know the hidden mechanism. No need to formulate it. Merely sug‐
gest the other has not arrived there yet. I have used that smile. Know
it from inside. It grants the position of seeing more and briefly ex‐
empts you from showing what exactly.
Stavrova and Ehlebracht investigated what they called the cynical
genius illusion. Perception studies showed a tendency to attribute su‐
perior cognitive competence to cynics. Their large-scale data analyses
did not confirm that image of generalized cynicism; observed relation‐
ships with cognitive and educational indicators generally ran oppos‐
itely, with contextual differences. Findings justify neither diagnosing
individuals nor simple causality. They challenge automatic equation
of distrust with intelligence (Stavrova and Ehlebracht, 2019).
This finding does not tell me to trust. It merely withdraws a symbolic
reward I was tempted to award myself. Suspecting low motives alone
does not make me more competent. I can be right once and miss an‐
other situation precisely by applying the same suspicion. An always-
sounding detector has not solved fire. It also produces false alarms
whose costs do not disappear because the sound seems prudent.
Yet the trusting person is not exempt from verification either. Too
weak an alarm can have grave consequences. Calibration interests
me: expectations' relationship to concrete environments, information
changing them, and errors' cost in either direction. A surgeon, busi‐
ness partner, and child requesting help cannot be assessed by one
simple rule. Risk and verifiability differ. Calling everything trust or
distrust conceals the difficult decision itself.
I can even construct my own evidence. Entering a relationship expect‐
ing deception, I may refuse every opening. The other withdraws. I re‐
cord that people offer nothing. I claim neither this cycle always occurs
nor betrayed people are guilty of betrayal. I describe a logical possibil‐
52


ity of mutual influence: my behavior changes the environment from
which I subsequently collect observations. Researchers do not always
stand outside their object.
The sample of experiences may also be skewed. I have reasons to re‐
member costly deception and forget dozens of uneventful honored
commitments. I offer no percentage or law of my memory. I merely
ask myself to record what produced no story too. An inventory con‐
taining only scandals cannot estimate normality's frequency. A map
made exclusively of accident sites does not describe every road.
Measurement also arises in honesty research. Yang and colleagues
conducted an extended Chinese replication of a lost-wallet field exper‐
iment. They distinguished initiating email contact from actually recov‐
ering wallets and showed these measures could produce different pic‐
tures. The study warns against treating a behavioral indicator as a
complete measure of honesty in a cultural context. It does not prove
comparison impossible; it demands checking what measured behavi‐
or actually means (Yang et al., 2023).
I think how quickly I would have used a number matching my suspi‐
cion. Said people are honest only when convenient, then treated the
indicator as a direct window into the soul. But perhaps the measure
also captured differences in procedure, opportunity, or task under‐
standing. This does not cancel data. It asks me to connect interpreta‐
tion with actual observation. A percentage does not become profound
merely because I can place a sentence about human nature beside it.
Perhaps the last naïf believes anything. But perhaps they believe any‐
thing about others' wickedness. I do not want to turn this symmetry
into a reassuring aphorism. Errors do not always cost equally. In abus‐
ive environments, heightened caution can protect. In cooperative
ones, rigid suspicion can destroy real opportunities. I should discover
my environment, not choose an identity exempting me from investig‐
ation.
The most dangerous cynicism merchant seems the one selling, with a
world explanation, membership in an awakened elite. They do not
merely ask me to accept a thesis. They offer transformed status: no
longer the used person, but the one who understood. Preserving the
53


new identity may make me reject every fact resembling my old hope.
Another's kindness threatens my intelligence. I must expose it or lose
the rank disappointment earned.
I fear this book may produce precisely that effect. Its title promises
passage: the last naïf, last belief, last mask. Readers might await the
key placing them among initiates. But I have no key. I found plausible
mechanisms, limited empirical findings, and counterexamples not
honestly eliminable. Turning this material into doctrine requires trim‐
ming its edges. The cut would already be my participation in what I
claim to denounce.
I close the file briefly. Not to abandon it, but notice the pleasure con‐
demnation would give me. A wholly guilty world would let me stop
feeling indebted to it. Perhaps that is my theory's hidden benefit. Dis‐
covering it does not automatically falsify the theory. But I can no
longer claim disinterested investigation. The amoral person I tried to
play apparently had a very human desire: finding an explanation
where nobody could ask anything of him anymore.

VI. The Final Examination
What Evidence Could Change My Mind?
If I wanted only to be an unsettling author, I would stop before this
question. Leave readers scenes, studies, and a suspicion broad enough
for every experience. They would find confirmations themselves. I
would seem to have described the world. But scientists, even suspend‐
ing moral judgment, must accept possible error. Otherwise they do not
analyze a filter. They build one for facts entering the book.
The universal accusation is too elastic: every belief held in good faith
is a trap. Encounter cooperation, call it investment. Sacrifice, call it
reputation. A self-correcting institution, call it a legitimation strategy.
Every outcome fits. This signals not explanatory power but eliminated
risk of refutation. I should extract claims able to lose before an obser‐
vation.
One would be that, in some organizations, those more willing to ac‐
cept moral obligations systematically bear uncompensated tasks des‐
pite comparable competence, roles, and available time. Another, that
virtue language is used more often to seek concessions from the less
powerful than the influential. A third, that promotion favors using ex‐
ceptions independently of real contribution. Each needs its own meas‐
ures and comparisons.
I would not begin by asking who considers themselves good. Answers
might measure self-image or skill at recognizing the form. I would
define precise behaviors: willingness to help in a situation, keeping
commitments, reporting costly errors, refusing tasks beyond one's
role. Not immediately compress them into a character score. Someone
can be generous and dishonest, rigorous and tactless, courageous here
and dependent elsewhere. Variation itself may matter to the mechan‐
ism.
I would measure power too, not only disposition. Does the person
have employment alternatives? Can they challenge without reprisals?
Know the rules? Control resources? Ignoring differences risks attribut‐
ing material vulnerability's effects to kindness. Perhaps people accept
55


not from naïveté but inability to afford refusal. Or values and absent
alternatives reinforce each other. Research must distinguish possibilit‐
ies, not christen them all character.
I would track time. A concession may be reciprocal over a longer in‐
terval or open endless one-way demands. Good reputation may bring
future benefits or fix someone as invisible-work provider. Without re‐
peated observations, I could call friendly help exploitation and unre‐
coverable loss investment. I would also record costs absent from insti‐
tutional accounts: personal time, fatigue, missed opportunities, recog‐
nition.
For language's effect, I could use limited-stakes experiments with ap‐
propriate safeguards, presenting identical requests through duty ap‐
peals or clear contractual descriptions. But no such experiment auto‐
matically reveals a twenty-year career. It would isolate one mechan‐
ism component. I would need converging evidence types and respect
for participants, not a laboratory reproducing the exploitation it de‐
nounces.
What finding would weaken my hypothesis? Cooperators receiving
more reciprocal support in comparable contexts without accumulat‐
ing greater hidden costs. Access to challenge correcting asymmetries
without demanding abandonment of values. Leaders accepting the re‐
strictions they invoke for others. Opportunism's apparent advantage
disappearing after accounting for resources, competence, and case se‐
lection. I should be able to write these outcomes before seeing them.
What would strengthen it? A robust pattern of moral appeals shifting
costs toward the less powerful, exceptions predictably protecting the
same positions, and people demanding symmetrical rules being penal‐
ized precisely for that demand. Even then, I would have demonstrated
a bounded mechanism. Not that every culture exists to eliminate
naïfs. Generalization would need other data, not a louder tone.
The biological version would demand still more. Distinguishing genet‐
ic transmission from education and inherited resources, tracking re‐
productive outcomes without substituting professional status. A local
effect in one generation is not species destiny. Nor does parent–child
resemblance alone establish mechanism. Selection cannot shortcut
everything unmeasured.
56


I would separately investigate who benefits when rules persist. The
complying individual? Group? Leadership? Institution as structure?
Norms can persist through mutual benefits, coercion, imitation, or
hard-to-coordinate alternatives. Choosing one beneficiary beforehand
finds only their story. Perhaps what I call domination's game is some‐
times a mixture of advantages, inertia, and powerlessness without a
stable winner.
I stop. This research program seduces less than a world constructed as
malevolent initiation. No central secret. Variables, criteria, findings
possibly remaining local. But perhaps here we see whether I want
truth or merely an elegant form of disappointment. I do not demand
abandoning suspicion. I demand it be able to lose. I wonder how
much remains when it can no longer save itself with any explanation.
The Offer That Contained No Lie
The committee invitation was impeccably phrased. Rigor, balance, re‐
sponsibility. I was asked to help choose suitable people. No order to
use anyone, cynical wording, or promise of impunity. Seeking materi‐
al evidence of initiation, I would find only a civilized document. Pre‐
cisely why it troubled me. The world I suspected need not write its ac‐
cusation in its own forms.
I had a good reason to accept: inside, I could demand clearer criteria.
Make omitted work visible. Prevent enthusiasm substituting for de‐
cent conditions. But I had less noble reasons too. I liked being con‐
sidered clear-sighted. Liked access. Liked knowing things before oth‐
ers. Concealing these needed no lies; narrating only the first part suf‐
ficed.
I told myself refusal would be convenient purity. A powerful sentence:
I could accept while considering myself more responsible than out‐
siders. Then told myself acceptance would be co-option. Powerful too:
I could refuse while considering myself more principled than insiders.
Two complete justifications prepared for opposite choices. What was
missing was not argument. It was the decision onto which argument
would settle.

At the first discussion we saw several profiles. One candidate de‐
scribed skills and requested a precise role. Another wrote that the
project mattered so much they would do anything to contribute.
Someone called the second authentic motivation. Perhaps. But I
wondered what each of us heard. One colleague heard devotion. An‐
other flexibility. I heard a still-unformulated limit. I did not know
whether the person lacked it, did not know it, or dared not write it.
I asked how the task would be bounded. They replied we could not
foresee everything and needed adaptable people. True. Institutions
cannot function solely by anticipating every situation. But the general
truth did not answer the particular question: who bears adaptation
when needs grow? Perhaps absent limits are inevitable in some places
and convenient in others. I wanted to separate them without being
treated as someone ignorant of working reality.
They asked for an alternative criterion. The uncomfortable moment.
My criticism now had an obligation to build, not only a privilege to
suspect. I proposed distinguishing unlimited availability from commit‐
ment and assessing contribution against a described role. No complete
solution. Urgencies, uncertainty, and human differences remained.
But at least it made visible that promising anything is not serious‐
ness's only form.
A colleague asked whether this discouraged passion. No slogan could
answer. Passion may need freedom, including investing beyond con‐
tractual demands. But organizations can receive that contribution as
gift or turn it into permanent expectation. I said volunteering should
not become the hidden criterion for access to paid roles. A brief si‐
lence fell. I did not know whether agreement, irritation, or merely fa‐
tigue.
Then someone mentioned Mara. Useful, but perhaps unable to fit the
new structure. I requested examples. Some convinced: she had re‐
fused necessary compromises and communicated badly. Others de‐
scribed precisely her interventions preventing errors. For the first
time, the file prevented hiding in symbolic choice. I could not recom‐
mend her merely as moral reparation. Nor reject her because her
presence would demand more explanations.
58


I caught myself calculating the inconvenience of daily work with
someone seeing my limits. A devoted, pliable person would ease my
role. A competent critic might improve the institution and complicate
my life. The benefits did not coincide. Perhaps this was initiation's real
moment: not discovering a plot, but the exact meeting of my interest
and an explanation calling it general interest.
No spectacular revelation. No changing faces, no laughter of those
knowing the secret. People trying to decide under constraints, each
with mixed motives. But that normality itself prevented closing the
case. If the mechanism existed, it need not suspend everyone's good
faith. It could use their motives' true part to carry advantages they did
not examine too.
Afterward I wrote two sentences in a notebook. First: not every com‐
promise is betrayal. Second: not every betrayal is recognizable by
lacking a good reason. I reread them repeatedly. They did not help me
choose. Only prevented awarding myself a certificate too quickly. Per‐
haps their sole use. Perhaps I asked philosophy for something im‐
possible: a decision no longer bearing its maker's traces.
Leaving, I had more influence and less certainty about my own clarity.
The first might gradually buy the second. The second might make me
more careful with the first. No outward sign decided. The invitation
contained no lie. Perhaps it needed none.
I Do Not Want Cruelty to Save Me
There would be a simple exit from all these questions: declare every‐
one fends for themselves and the sole mistake is not pursuing self-in‐
terest. I need prove little. Calling my refusal to answer maturity would
suffice. But I wonder whether conversion would liberate me or merely
change my place in the mechanism. I would cease being used and per‐
haps become someone knowing whom to use. The filter would not be
destroyed. It would produce another graduate.
Not every hardening is cruelty. I can learn refusal without wishing
harm. Demand a contract, verify promises, challenge distribution.
Calling every boundary selfishness makes the concept too broad to
distinguish anything. But defending boundaries too can become con‐
59


venient ideology: nothing concerns me, no dependency obliges me,
every suffering belongs exclusively to its sufferer. Then a world of in‐
terdependencies I refuse to acknowledge finances my autonomy.
I wonder whether kindness unable to say no is kindness or absence of
a psychological alternative. No universal answer. Sometimes someone
sincerely chooses great sacrifice. Sometimes they cannot imagine re‐
fusal without losing worth in their own eyes. Outwardly the gestures
may resemble each other. Their real freedom should interest me, not
only my benefit. But who am I to declare their choice unfree merely
because it seems too costly?
Perhaps offering information and alternatives without confiscating
decision would be more honest. Not hiding costs, conditioning respect
on acceptance, demanding proof of love through exhaustion. These
are examinable options, not a recipe resolving every relationship.
Sometimes I will genuinely need help and suffer without it. I should
be able to state that truth without automatically turning it into a ver‐
dict on the refuser's character.
I do not want a world reduced to complete contracts. Impossible and
probably impoverished. Friendship, research, care, and shared life re‐
quire initiatives not negotiable beforehand to the last detail. But
neither should inevitable incompleteness protect convenient ambigu‐
ities. I can preserve room for gifts and make limits visible. Recognize
someone gave more than owed without making that extra their new
duty.
I think of researchers' responsibility. Cool analysis of exploitation does
not oblige endorsement. Descriptive distance does not demand sus‐
pension of every moral preference in life. Scientists can say what a
strategy would produce without recommending it. But must notice the
transition from description to selecting objectives. Defining success
solely as domination already chooses a framework; it is not pure
methodological neutrality.
Perhaps the amoral person I sought initially does not exist as I wished.
I can temporarily suspend approval and condemnation to see mech‐
anisms. Cannot choose what deserves inquiry, what cost matters, what
outcome is success without criteria. Even interest in truth is a prefer‐
60


ence limiting story construction. Were I wholly indifferent, kindness's
possibly being a trap would not trouble me. Only its yield would in‐
terest me.
I fear intellectual revenge too. I could write of responsibility so those
burdening me recognize themselves and face judgment. Pleasant. But
analysis grows no truer by restoring my dignity. Nor false by originat‐
ing in a wound. I should distinguish my right to name experience
from the obligation not to turn it, without evidence, into everyone's
anatomy.
The child from the first room returns. No longer awaiting theory, but
an answer to the practical question. What tomorrow? Saying never
yield could send them into an unbearable conflict. Saying always be
good could leave them defenseless. Perhaps my first intervention
should address the room, not their character. Speak to the adult,
change the rule, make cost visible. Harder than offering a virtue for
the child to carry alone.
The same idea follows me into the organization. Asking Mara to set
boundaries is convenient. Of course she should be able to. But if we
built a system relying on their absence, I cannot transfer all corrective
work to her. She bears refusal's cost, we applaud her maturation and
perhaps seek the next available person. Individual change may be ne‐
cessary but insufficient. Otherwise the market for boundary advice
becomes the repair service of institutions never repairing themselves.
I have reached no salvation program. Only gestures guaranteeing
neither success nor innocence. Investigate before condemning. Make
cost visible before praising sacrifice. Do not use understood vulnerab‐
ility merely because I can. Each may lose to constraint, weakness, or
interest I will call necessity. No new complete manual exists here.
Perhaps its absence frightens me. Absolute cynicism offered one
simple rule. Absolute trust another. Between them remains a life
where both helping and defending myself can be mistakes. I do not
know whether this life is wiser. Only that it prevents calling
everything hurting me cruelty and everything benefiting me clarity.
Sometimes too modest a limit for such a troubled book. Sometimes the
limit I have tried to evade since page one.

Before the Signature
Mara asks what I learned. I say there is no single mechanism. Some
virtues are exploitable, some institutions distribute costs asymmetric‐
ally, real cooperation exists, motives are never wholly visible. She
listens patiently. Then asks whether this changes next week's task al‐
location. For several seconds, my whole book shrinks to an adminis‐
trative question.
I could reproach her for asking too much of analysis. Not every book
must repair an organization. But in this story I am more than author.
A participant granted new power. I cannot perfectly separate roles
precisely when separation exempts action. Knowledge changed some‐
thing: now I can justify the mechanism more precisely too. It is not
certain this advances anything for her.
For the last time, I imagine initiation as ceremony. At entry, you need
not abandon truth. You must learn which truth is useful in each room.
Need not abandon kindness. Must understand from whom it can be
requested without negotiation. Need not lack conscience. You receive
enough reasons for conscience to work for decisions already made.
The image is powerful. I do not know whether too powerful for what I
demonstrated.
It contains facts, inferences, and fear. Facts insufficient for universal
accusation. Inferences requiring checks I have not made. Fear mine,
unable to testify for the world. Yet it need not be eliminated as worth‐
less impurity. It noticed something before I found precise form: the
difference between being appreciated and being protected. Perhaps
that was the real question from the beginning.
Someone can be admired precisely for enduring. A culture can celeb‐
rate them without wishing their disappearance or preparing a less
costly life. Perhaps nobody hates them. Everyone may love them in
their useful role. What happens when they request existence outside
it too? There, more than in virtue discourse, I would seek answers.
Not human essence, but how a relationship bears its giver's freedom.
But I remember people protecting me without visible demands in re‐
turn too. I cannot eliminate them for spoiling the book's atmosphere.
Reduce every gesture to strategy merely to preserve a voice's consist‐
62


ency. My story also contained relationships where refusal did not
erase respect, errors were corrected without humiliation, someone
surrendered retainable advantage. They proved no just world. They
prevented complete condemnation.
Perhaps the mystery is not whether kindness exists, but how much
freedom it has in an environment learning to use it. Or perhaps this is
merely my last saving formula, more sophisticated than childhood's. I
do not want to call every hope naïveté. Nor to be the author who leads
readers through darkness, then offers at the exit the same candle ex‐
tinguished at entry and calls the gesture wisdom.
The final form lies on the desk. No two-column choice between good
and evil. Competent people, real limits, needs impossible to meet sim‐
ultaneously, advantages I should declare at least to myself. Choose the
highly available person, say the project needs involvement. Choose
the boundary-setter, say I want maturity. No wording alone will prove
my motive. What I do after choosing will matter too.
I think a successful trap can make you feel you chose alone. But real
freedom brings that feeling too. No inner sign distinguishes them
without remainder. I should examine my information, alternatives, re‐
fusal's costs, and capacity to revise the decision. Even then something
unknown remains. Not every unknown is manipulation. Not every
choice is free merely because I can narrate it in the first person.
What would being the last naïf mean? The last still believing rules ap‐
ply to everyone? Last refusing to learn exceptions? Last believing I
discovered the secret and can never again be used? The title will not
settle. It moves from child to researcher, researcher to evaluator, eval‐
uator to the person examining too late their pleasure at being invited.
I want to leave the form unsigned. Immediate beauty in that gesture. I
could leave the room with every question intact. Someone else de‐
cides; I keep observing. But my absence would not stand outside the
world. It would have consequences, protect something, abandon
something else. Refusing the role too is no cost-free place. Perhaps
among the last naïvetés is believing I can remain clean simply by
choosing the position that need not explain effects.

In the end, not the form but a sentence in one candidate’s message
catches my attention. He wants to work somewhere contribution mat‐
ters more than power games. No promise of unlimited sacrifice. A
modest hope, perhaps too modest for someone who learned to suspect
everything. I read it twice. Wonder whether I see a reason to protect
him, choose him, or information about how easily he could be led.
Before the meeting, I open the window. Footsteps and a conversation I
cannot understand come from the courtyard. I try remembering what
I wanted when I began writing: truth about the world or permission
no longer to be wounded by it. Perhaps both. Perhaps I hoped the first
would produce the second. If that was the trap, someone else did not
necessarily set it.
A knock at the door. The man enters with a folder under his arm and
asks whether he is early. I say no. He thanks me for the opportunity
and begins explaining why he cares about the project. I already know
some sentences, not because they are insincere but because I said
them too. I listen. His file, our criteria, and my uncapped pen lie on
the table.
I could explain the two manuals. Tell him what is clear and what is
not yet, what we will ask and what he should not promise. I could let
him speak until he reveals every place he cannot refuse. Every con‐
tinuation is possible. But before them comes an ordinary sentence,
warm enough not to seem an examination’s beginning.
I tell him we need people like him.
