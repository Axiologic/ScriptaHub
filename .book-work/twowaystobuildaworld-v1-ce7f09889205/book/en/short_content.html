Two Ways to Build a World

Ten-minute edition

The book revisits Riane Eisler’s partnership–domination distinction without relying on a peaceful prehistoric golden age. It treats the two patterns as ways to coordinate people: one can make refusal costly, while the other makes contribution, reciprocal rules, and contestable authority worthwhile. Neither is pure, and neither belongs to one period or ideology. The question is which material, social, institutional, and technical conditions make each pattern more likely.

1. The idea that refuses to die

Eisler’s historical account remains contested, yet her analytical contrast can still be useful. The book separates an empirical question about migration and prehistoric violence from a more general question: why does one social order reward coercion while another rewards contribution?

2. The past is not a moral exhibit

Archaeology neither establishes a uniformly peaceful Old Europe nor makes domination inevitable. Ancient DNA, graves, settlement patterns, and inequality measures support different inferences with different limits. The useful questions concern control of resources, violence, exit, office, and surplus.

3. From binary to coordinate system

Partnership and domination describe dimensions of relationships, not whole civilizations. Examine who can leave, speak, appeal, observe decisions, impose sanctions, and convert one advantage into many forms of control. A hierarchy can be necessary and still remain bounded and contestable.

4. Two roads to status

Influence can grow through dominance, backed by fear and the capacity to impose costs, or through prestige, granted because others value competence. Prestige can later be converted into coercive office. The crucial design problem is preventing one kind of status from governing unrelated domains.

5. Equality is something people do

Egalitarian orders require active counter-power rather than an absence of ambitious people. Coalitions, criticism, courts, labor organization, replication, exit, and protected voice can restrain would-be monopolists. Formal consent says little when refusing destroys a person’s livelihood and membership at once.

6. Cooperation is not innocence

Durable cooperation needs boundaries, memory, monitoring, and proportionate sanctions. Shared resources cannot be governed by goodwill alone, yet enforcement need not become unchecked central command. The institutional task is to make rule-breaking answerable without giving enforcers permanent arbitrary power.

7. The tribal trap

Human solidarity can coexist with exclusion of outsiders. In-group cooperation is not automatically partnership across group boundaries. The book asks whether rules protecting members also constrain how they treat strangers, rivals, and vulnerable people beyond the group.

8. Surplus, storage, and chokepoints

Stored resources and narrow gates can make power durable. Whoever controls grain, land, payment rails, cloud infrastructure, or discovery can command more than their visible holdings suggest. Technology may move chokepoints rather than abolish them.

9. Why threat loves hierarchy

Emergencies can justify concentrated authority for a limited task. The danger comes when emergency powers outlive the threat, leaders benefit from keeping fear salient, and dissent loses protection. Reversal, expiry, and independent review matter before the crisis arrives.

10. Scale changes the game

Personal trust weakens as strangers and specialized roles multiply. Large-scale cooperation needs procedures, records, rights, and institutions that replace knowledge of each individual. Scale does not force domination, but it raises the cost of keeping authority visible and contestable.

11. Every age has its power technology

Horses, writing, guns, printing, networks, and models change who can coordinate, monitor, persuade, and resist. The same invention can strengthen concentrated command and distributed cooperation. Political outcomes depend on the surrounding rules, not the tool alone.

12. Partnership needs teeth

The book draws on commons governance to show that cooperation requires clear boundaries, locally appropriate rules, monitoring, graduated sanctions, appeal, and nested institutions. Partnership is neither unlimited trust nor the absence of enforcement.

13. Hierarchy without domination

The objective is not flatness. Task-specific authority can be legitimate when its purpose, scope, duration, review, and exit are clear. Hidden hierarchies can be harder to contest than declared ones. Expertise in one domain must not silently become power over every domain.

14. A diagnostic for real institutions

A practical scorecard examines coercion, exit, voice, information, sanctions, status conversion, time, and boundaries. The point is to expose mixed patterns and surprising weaknesses rather than sort favored institutions into moral boxes.

15. How partnership fails

Cooperative orders can drift toward oligarchy, procedural overload, charismatic capture, moral purity, or free-riding. Success creates assets worth capturing. Repair requires institutions that keep their own leaders, experts, and guardians open to challenge.

16. AI can become an empire machine

AI can lower the cost of prediction, persuasion, surveillance, classification, and administrative action. Combined with concentrated data and infrastructure, these capabilities may turn many chokepoints into one computational layer. The book treats this as an institutional risk, not an unavoidable destiny.

17. AI can lower the cost of partnership

The same tools can help people read rules, inspect contracts, translate, verify claims, deliberate, and coordinate locally. Their value for partnership depends on traceability, access, portability, and the ability to contest machine-assisted decisions.

18. Designing a non-utopian future

A serious design starts with human ambition, tribalism, error, crisis, and exploitation. Make domination expensive, visible, narrow, and reversible; make cooperation easier to sustain at scale. The closing claim is architectural: institutions can change which form of coordination is cheaper.
