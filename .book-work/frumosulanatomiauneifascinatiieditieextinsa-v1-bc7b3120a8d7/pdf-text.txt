PROLOGUE
Three Rooms
An impeccable proof. A voice you cannot forget. A crowd moving like
a single body. Do we admire them for the same reason?
Imagine three rooms. In the first, someone finishes a proof and, for a
few seconds, a problem that seemed to offer no way out becomes
transparent. In the second, someone looks at their sleeping partner;
what moves them would not necessarily score highly in an experi‐
ment using photographs. In the third, on a huge screen, an authorit‐
arian power unfolds its spectacle: rhythm, order, scale, precision. The
viewer knows its cruelty. And yet, for a moment, is fascinated.
It would be reassuring to say that the first experience is true beauty,
the second is love, and the third merely manipulation. But this verbal
tidiness would spare us precisely the interesting problem. Why can
something we reject possess aesthetic force? Why do some truths
leave us cold, while a false explanation can seem revelatory? And why
can a beautiful thing, encountered too often, cease to reveal itself to
us?
Beauty promises, often without words, that something deserves atten‐
tion. Yet it does not always specify what: form, competence, vitality,
story, power, familiarity, or the possibility of a different world. Faced
with an elegant construction, we may feel we have understood before
we have checked. Faced with a person, we may feel we have recog‐
nized a character before we have come to know a life. Faced with a
work of art, we may receive an intensity that cannot be translated into
either usefulness or a moral lesson.
In an imaginary scenario, two people attend the same interview. They
have performed equally well on the practical test. One has an appear‐
ance the assessors find attractive. The other does not. The written re‐
cord says nothing about beauty. It mentions “presence,” “potential,”
“confidence.” Nobody feels they have rigged the competition. This is
precisely where the problem begins.
4


The question can be investigated. A meta-analysis found average ad‐
vantages of attractiveness in experimental professional evaluations
and outcomes. This does not mean that appearance explains an entire
career, but that merit can be assessed through a filter that does not de‐
clare itself a filter. [1]
Now we change the scene. A person alone listens to music. They are
not trying to impress anyone, buying status, or choosing a partner. A
passage makes them stop. They have gained nothing measurable. Yet
their evening has become richer. A theory that sees only a reproduct‐
ive strategy or a commercial trick here misses something. A theory
that sees only an innocent preference in the first interview misses
something else.
A book about beauty that stopped at prejudice would miss the reason
we accept the risk of prejudice: there is something to gain. A success‐
ful form can open perception, compress a proof, make a loss bearable,
create a language between people who had none. Sometimes it serves
nothing beyond itself. And demanding that it justify its existence
through productivity or virtue might already be a form of impoverish‐
ment.
But the case for autonomy does not free us from the difficulty either.
Do we admire the form of an object or the form of life that object pre‐
supposes? Can we isolate a palace’s beauty from the social order that
made it possible? If we can, how much of the experience’s truth do we
lose? If we cannot, is there any work we can look at without a com‐
plete dossier on the world?
We will encounter bodies, animal ornaments, equations, voices,
rituals, buildings, algorithms, gestures, and ruins. Not to squeeze them
into a single definition, but to see where analogy works and where it
begins to lie. Grace is not the same as perfection. Elegance is not syn‐
onymous with simplicity. The sublime is not merely a larger beauty.
And intelligence can be seductive even when it works against us.
The final question will not be only who gains from beauty. It will also
be this: what are we willing to lose for it? Some money, of course.
Sometimes time, comfort, safety, or truth. Harder to acknowledge is
that a world without such losses might be more reasonable and, for
some of us, harder to desire.

PART I / THE PROMISES OF BEAUTY
CHAPTER 1
The Word That Praises
Incompatible Things
A face, an equation, a forest, a sentence, and a generous gesture can
receive the same praise: “beautiful.” Suppose we asked the person
saying it to specify what they all have in common. Color cannot be the
answer: an equation does not depend on the ink we write it in. Sym‐
metry is not enough: a generous gesture has no two halves that mirror
one another. Pleasure is too broad: we enjoy drinking water when
thirsty without calling every sip beautiful. Nor is morality enough: an
evil intention can be expressed in a remarkable sentence.
The word gathers a family of experiences, not necessarily an ingredi‐
ent present in the same dose in all of them. The history of aesthetics
can be read as a dispute over the center of this family: the object’s or‐
der, perfection, the viewer’s pleasure, love, form, or how something
fulfills its purpose. No short definition has settled the dispute. [2]
We can nevertheless introduce a working definition. I call an experi‐
ence of beauty that appreciation in which the way something presents
itself, is organized, or becomes intelligible acquires a felt value, and
attention wishes to remain with it or return. This definition is pro‐
posed to orient the book, not as scientific consensus. It includes under‐
standing, not just sight. It does not claim beauty must be soothing. And
it allows that some important aesthetic encounters will remain at its
margins.
We must first distinguish beauty from attraction. Attraction directs me
toward someone or something: I want to approach, possess, taste, con‐
tinue. Aesthetic appreciation can exist without this intention. I can
consider a face beautiful without erotic desire. I can be attracted to
someone I would not choose as an example in a beauty contest. I can
admire an object I would not wish to own. When we confuse these ex‐
periences, we reduce a person’s beauty to their availability for
someone else’s desire.
6


There is also the difference between beauty and cuteness. A cute ob‐
ject may invite protection, closeness, play; monumental architecture
may produce respect or distance. In the Kantian tradition, “sublime”
names an experience different from the beauty of forms: the en‐
counter with a magnitude or power the imagination cannot comfort‐
ably encompass. A night sky and a flower need not be forced to pro‐
duce the same emotion for both to matter aesthetically. [3]
Elegance is another neighbor. An elegant proof achieves much with
few means, makes a connection visible, avoids steps that seem added
merely to salvage the construction. But concision is not enough: a
false proposition can be very short. Moral beauty is another exten‐
sion: we appreciate the manner of an action, not the pigment of the
body performing it. Precisely this extension becomes dangerous when
the direction is reversed and we assume that a body pleasing to the
eye conceals, for that reason, a good character.
These distinctions have practical consequences. An art school can
value a work that unsettles, although it is not decorative. An employer
can appreciate a clear presentation without awarding points to the
face of its presenter. A partner can be sincere in affection without is‐
suing a universal ranking. Thinking precisely about beauty does not
mean cooling it down. It means not asking the same emotion to do all
the mind’s jobs.
A further distinction is decisive: beauty may belong to how something
presents itself, how it is organized, or how we come to understand it.
A drawing can be immediately delightful. A proof may become beauti‐
ful only after effort. A gesture may acquire beauty only when we
learn what risk it involved. In the last case, a change in knowledge
changes the experience, without necessarily changing the visible ob‐
ject.
This is why the idea that beauty is a mere surface is insufficient. The
surface can be a gateway to a structure, and the structure may exist
only through a history. A mark on wood may look like carelessness
until we learn it is the trace of a gesture repeated throughout a life‐
time. Information does not automatically add value: a banal story can
be used to sell a banal object at a high price. Yet the fact that a story
can deceive does not prove that all experiences dependent on history
are false.

Beautiful, Elegant, Interesting, Impressive
Elegance has a more precise profile than the general praise “beauti‐
ful.” It suggests a fit between means and difficulty: the problem is
solved without unnecessary gestures, relationships become legible,
tension is controlled. A tangled forest can be beautiful without being
elegant. An administrative solution can be elegant without moving us.
A highly elaborate dress can possess elegance through the organiza‐
tion of abundance; austerity has no monopoly on the term.
The interesting calls for exploration but does not guarantee admira‐
tion. An unusual error is interesting. A cruel situation can be interest‐
ing to a researcher and unbearable to someone caught in it. The im‐
pressive calls, rather, for recognition of an intensity or capacity. A
huge building can impress us without seeming beautiful. These dis‐
tinctions matter because the same bodily reaction of pausing or won‐
der can receive very different interpretations.
Expressiveness should not be confused with beauty either. A broken
voice can express pain more convincingly than an impeccable one.
We can call it beautiful in an expressive sense, but we can also refuse
the word while preserving its value. We are not obliged to rename
every achievement beauty. A rich aesthetic culture also needs terms
such as exact, disturbing, sober, tender, monumental, strange, comic,
or terrible.
At least five levels meet in an aesthetic judgment. There are the thing’s
properties: rhythm, material, form, expression, relationships. There is
the person’s experience: what they feel and notice. There is the judg‐
ment expressed: “it is beautiful,” “it is banal,” “it is too much.” There
are the community’s rules that make some judgments prestigious. And
there are the consequences: who is looked at, bought, heard, pro‐
moted, or ignored. Separating these levels is the book’s analytical
framework, not the discovery of a single study.
Consider a hypothetical chair. Its lines may be appreciated in a photo‐
graph. When we sit down, we find it uncomfortable. A museum may
continue to consider it important to the history of design. A shop may
sell it at a high price. Someone living with pain may consider it a hos‐
tile object. Nobody necessarily has to be lying: the participants judge
8


different aspects. The error arises when the photograph gains jurisdic‐
tion over the entire experience and the user’s discomfort is declared a
lack of taste.
Natural and artistic beauty introduce another difference. In a forest,
we need not assume an author is communicating something to us. In a
poem, expressive choices, possible intentions, and reading traditions
may matter. Functional beauty emerges when we understand how
something works; expressive beauty, when we feel a form says or em‐
bodies something. Relational beauty may belong to a shared memory.
An apparently modest object becomes irreplaceable not because the
market has valued it incorrectly, but because its history cannot be re‐
placed by an identical object.
Someone may object that we have broadened the concept too far. If
anything meaningful can be beautiful, what use is the word? The ob‐
jection is sound. Not every meaning produces beauty. A bill makes
sense and may be enormously important without inviting appreciat‐
ive attention to its form. Nor is every intense emotion aesthetic. Fear
of immediate danger does not become sublime merely because the
heart beats fast. Context and the kind of attention are part of the phe‐
nomenon, not subsequent details.
This plurality does not mean that anything can be declared beautiful
without reason. It means the reason must be identified before com‐
parison. The question “which is more beautiful, a theorem or a song?”
is not impossible, but requires us first to build a bridge between the
experiences. If that bridge is merely emotional intensity, we have
eliminated precisely the difference between what moves us.
A simple test for our vocabulary is substitution. What is lost if we re‐
place “beautiful” with “efficient,” “pleasant,” “expensive,” or “good”?
If nothing is lost, perhaps we were really talking about something
else. If something is lost, that remainder deserves pursuit. This book
begins with that remainder: the experience of a value we recognize
more quickly than we manage to define.

CHAPTER 2
From the Order of the World to
the Justification of Existence
Suppose beauty were a reliable sign of truth. Learning to look well
would be enough to choose correct ideas, good people, and just insti‐
tutions. Aesthetics would become a shortcut to wisdom. The attraction
of this promise is enormous. So is the risk: the world can offer convin‐
cing appearances without offering what they seem to guarantee.
In Plato’s Symposium, Diotima’s speech describes an ascent of love:
from the beauty of one body to that of several bodies, then toward
souls, practices, knowledge, and ultimately Beauty itself. Beauty here
is neither merely a pleasant sensation nor the sum of a market’s pref‐
erences. It is a stable, intelligible goal toward which particular experi‐
ences can direct us. This is one of the most influential proposals for
turning fascination into an education of desire. [4]
The idea’s power lies in refusing to stop at the first surface. If some‐
thing captivates us, we might ask what precisely we admire and
whether there is a deeper realization of that value. Desire can become
inquiry. Yet not every passage from the sensible to the abstract is an
advance. If the beloved person becomes a rung abandoned after
serving our purpose, we have lost precisely the relationship’s singu‐
larity. A philosophy of beauty can elevate attention and simultan‐
eously draw it away from the actual human being.
Another difficulty concerns authority. Someone who knows they have
reached true beauty may consider others’ preferences mere errors.
The high standard offers the possibility of criticizing fashion, but also
of domination in the name of an order inaccessible to its challengers.
It does not follow that every objective ideal is authoritarian. It follows
that the link between claimed access to truth and the power to discip‐
line others must be justified separately.
In the Poetics, Aristotle shifts attention toward the work’s organiza‐
tion. A whole has parts arranged in an order, and its size must allow it
to be grasped. In discussing tragedy, he treats the construction of the
10


action and the relationships between events as essential to success. He
also observes that representing unpleasant things can offer pleasure
through recognition and learning. The represented object and the rep‐
resentation’s value are therefore not the same thing. [5]
We can derive a fruitful question from this without turning the an‐
cient text into a contemporary manual: do we admire something be‐
cause it is intelligibly composed? A novel with many characters does
not succeed merely because it is complicated. A simple one does not
succeed merely because it can be summarized. The relationship
between the parts matters. If we remove a scene and nothing import‐
ant changes, we have reason to discuss it. If a deviation prepares a
later understanding, the apparent flaw may belong to the construc‐
tion.
Aristotle thus offers a criterion that does not require every element to
be agreeable: the success of the relationship between parts. But this
criterion leaves the nature of the whole open. A whole can be reassur‐
ing, violent, comic, or harrowing. The beauty of organization does not
yet tell us whether the organized world is one we would wish to in‐
habit. Two hopes diverge here: that beauty might confirm the order of
existence, or that it might allow us to bear the absence of such an or‐
der.
Beauty as a Sign and Beauty as a Refuge
In a passage from the Summa theologiae, Thomas Aquinas lists integ‐
rity or perfection, due proportion, and clarity among beauty’s require‐
ments. The passage occurs in a theological argument, not an inde‐
pendent treatise on design. Read in its own context, it connects beauty
to an intelligible order of being and the way it manifests itself. It
should not be converted without explanation into the recipe “three in‐
gredients for anything beautiful.” [6]
The attraction of such a perspective is obvious: beauty would not be a
pleasant accident in an indifferent universe, but a sign that reality has
meaning. An illuminated window, a harmonious proportion, a voice
can seem more than sensory events. For a believer, they may particip‐
11


ate in an experience of transcendence. This is a possible metaphysical
interpretation, not an experimental conclusion binding on everyone
who feels the emotion.
The problem arises when the beautiful world is invoked as sufficient
evidence of its order’s goodness. A landscape can be magnificent and
contain hunger, disease, fear. Viewed from a distance, its aggregate
form can delight us while the condition of its living creatures remains
appalling. Seeing a harmony does not mean having verified its justice.
Beauty can be a reason for gratitude; alone, it is not an answer to suf‐
fering.
Imagine a painter creating a panorama of a city. From the chosen
viewpoint, poor neighborhoods form picturesque textures. Their in‐
habitants would prefer clean water and safe housing to this contribu‐
tion to the composition. The painter can be sincere, and the painting
can have aesthetic qualities. But the painter’s perspective does not
represent the city’s whole truth. This is the risk of any view from very
high above: what appears harmonious as a totality may be intolerable
from within.
The philosophical history of beauty is therefore not a sequence in
which old ideas are naive and new ones have solved the problem.
Older theories preserve questions that strictly psychological explana‐
tions may sidestep: why cultivate certain pleasures? What kind of at‐
tention deserves education? Can beauty draw us out of our obsession
with immediate usefulness? Modern perspectives, in turn, oblige us to
investigate who presents a particular ideal as universal order.
Schopenhauer proposes a radical shift. In the third book of The World
as Will and Representation, aesthetic contemplation temporarily sus‐
pends a relation to things dominated by desire and personal advant‐
age. We no longer look exclusively at what we can obtain from things.
For a moment, the will’s permanent pressure ceases. This is a meta‐
physical interpretation of experience, not a clinical finding about art’s
guaranteed effect. [7]
Its phenomenological possibility nevertheless deserves serious consid‐
eration. Suppose you look at the sea without calculating a profit, a
photograph, or an achievement to publish. You have not received a
new object; the regime of attention has changed. On this reading,
12


beauty would be an interruption of the obligation to turn everything
into a means. Precisely for that reason, it would have value even if it
did not make us more productive after the break.
In Nietzsche’s The Birth of Tragedy, the relationship between the Apol‐
lonian and the Dionysian complicates the ideal of a purely serene
beauty. Form, dream, and measure meet the intensity that dissolves
the individual’s boundaries. Tragic art should not be read as merely
embellishing an already reasonable world; it participates in affirming
an existence that includes suffering and destruction. This is a philo‐
sophical construction about art and life, not a neutral and definitive
history of Greek theater. [8]
The difference is profound. On one reading, art briefly takes us out of
desire. On another, it can intensify the power to say yes to existence.
In one case, beauty is liberation from turmoil; in the other, form given
to turmoil. We do not have to choose a philosopher by vote. We can
ask whether these theories describe different experiences we actually
have: the stillness of a still life and the energy of a tragic scene do not
do the same thing to us.
A World That Needs Beauty
Imagine two universes. In the first, everything is harmonious, and suf‐
fering is a small, reparable error. In the second, there is no promise of
final harmony, but extraordinary local forms appear: a gesture, a son‐
ata, a proof. In which does beauty matter more? The first makes it pre‐
dictable, almost administrative. The second gives it fragility and risk.
This comparison proves nothing about the real universe, but shows
how different the metaphysical functions assigned to the same word
can be.
From this perspective, the question addressed to the past is not “were
they wrong to believe in beauty?” but “what did they hope to receive
from it?” Sometimes they hoped for evidence that the world was not
meaningless. At other times, a model for shaping character or an or‐
der against arbitrariness. These hopes remain comprehensible. What
we cannot do is confuse them with evidence that beauty keeps all its
promises.
13


The history of aesthetics can therefore also be read as a history of the
needs beauty is called upon to meet. When we demand it as a guaran‐
tee of truth, we turn it into a judge. When we demand it as consola‐
tion, we turn it into shelter. When we demand it as an intensification
of life, we accept that it may not soothe us at all. None of these de‐
mands is absurd. But beauty may answer one by refusing another.

CHAPTER 3
Taste: Freedom, Education, or
Discipline?
In an imaginary concert hall, a listener is bored. Their neighbor is
moved to tears. On leaving, the first says “I did not like it,” and the
second hears “it had no value.” The dispute begins with a distorted
translation. But if we stop every discussion with “there is no arguing
about taste,” we lose the possibility of learning. Perhaps the first
missed something. Perhaps the second is swayed by reputation. How
do we find out without turning the conversation into a test of superi‐
ority?
In his essay Of the Standard of Taste, published in 1757, David Hume
recognizes the diversity of preferences but does not accept that all
critical judgments are equally well formed. Practice, comparison,
sensitivity to detail, good sense, and diminished prejudice define the
trustworthy critic. Feeling intensely is not enough; one must perceive
and compare well. Hume even acknowledges the difficulty of identify‐
ing such judges. [9]
The idea can be translated into an everyday situation. Someone who
has repaired musical instruments may hear differences we cannot.
Someone who has read a great deal of poetry may notice a change of
rhythm that escapes us. We are not obliged to prefer what the expert
prefers, but it would be rash to declare their observation mere pre‐
tense. Education can enlarge what becomes accessible to experience,
not just the vocabulary used to describe it.
Yet there is a possible circularity. We recognize good critics because
they appreciate good works, and good works because good critics ap‐
preciate them. How do we escape? A modest solution is to demand
verifiable intermediate results: what relationship did they notice,
what comparison do they propose, what variation would alter the ef‐
fect? We do not thereby obtain a unit for measuring beauty, but we
can distinguish rich attention from a pompous verdict. A critic who
can show nothing beyond their prestige demands submission rather
than cultivating taste.
15


There is also the difficulty of survival. A work long admired deserves
examination, but the duration of a reputation is not an experiment
free from intervention. Transmission involves copying, teaching,
translation, preservation, access. As an argument, a work’s survival
through historical selections may support its value, but does not allow
us to establish the value of works denied the same chance. A voice’s
absence from the archive is not a vote cast against it by all humanity.
Let us give the expert the strongest defense. They do not necessarily
claim their pleasure is worth more because it is theirs. They may dis‐
tinguish things the beginner cannot yet hear: a modified reprise, a de‐
ferred tension, a sentence discreetly preparing the ending. Compet‐
ence is sometimes access to real properties. Reducing it in principle to
snobbery means prohibiting the very possibility of education.
But the defense has an equally strong opponent. Experts may learn a
milieu’s conventions so thoroughly that they become unable to notice
achievements that contradict them. A capacity for discrimination can
also become a discipline of exclusion. The problem is not simply “ex‐
pert or amateur?” but what kind of attention has been trained, what
results it produces, and what it can no longer notice.
Why Do We Ask Others to Agree?
In the Critique of Judgment of 1790, Kant characterizes aesthetic satis‐
faction through disinterestedness: not the absence of emotion, but
pure judgment’s independence from the desire to possess the object or
from its usefulness. At the same time, a judgment of taste requests
others’ agreement without relying on a concept that would demon‐
strate beauty. This claim to universality is not a survey finding that
everyone already agrees. It is a problem of justifying the way we
judge. [3]
The philosophical gain is that the viewer no longer has to be reduced
to a buyer. We can appreciate without accumulating. We can contem‐
plate a tree without immediately calculating the land’s price. In a soci‐
ety demanding a return from every experience, this possibility mat‐
ters. But the social question does not disappear: who has the time,
safety, and peace for such attention? The freedom to suspend self-in‐
terest may be easier to exercise when urgent needs are already met.
16


The objection does not invalidate Kant. The fact that a practice has
material conditions does not mean its value is false. Reading requires
time and literacy without thereby being merely a class masquerade.
The better question is how to extend the conditions of the practice, not
how to declare it useless. We can defend contemplation while refusing
to treat difficulty accessing it as personal inferiority.
In his letters on aesthetic education, Friedrich Schiller explores the
possibility that aesthetic experience and play might mediate between
sensory impulses and the demands of form. Human beings should be
shaped neither as mere executors of constraints nor as prisoners of
impulses. His project assigns aesthetics an emancipatory function.
This is a philosophical thesis about the formation of freedom, not ex‐
perimental proof that museum visits automatically produce just cit‐
izens. [10]
Here we should be both generous and demanding. Art can offer a
space in which we imagine other lives and try perspectives without
immediately turning them into orders to execute. But the same capa‐
city to organize emotions can serve propaganda, the cult of prestige,
or exclusion. Not every complex emotion shapes a good character.
Someone who weeps at a performance may remain indifferent to the
person cleaning the hall. Aesthetic competence and moral responsive‐
ness can meet; there is no logical identity between them.
In 1896, George Santayana proposed the formula of beauty as objecti‐
fied pleasure: a positive feeling is experienced as a value of the thing.
The emphasis falls on how experience is projected into the object,
without the object thereby becoming unimportant. [11] The formula
helps us understand why we say “the evening is beautiful,” rather
than merely “at this moment I am in a pleasant state.” The world does
not seem an interchangeable pretext for emotion; it seems the place
where emotion finds its justification.
There is a difference here between requesting agreement and impos‐
ing it. When someone shows us a sentence and says “listen to how it
closes,” they are not merely communicating a preference. They invite
us to recreate an experience. An aesthetic judgment thus has a public
dimension without being a measurement. We can argue through ex‐
ample, comparison, highlighting a detail, even if the outcome is not
guaranteed.
17


Suppose two highly competent people remain in disagreement. One
admires a work’s economy; the other believes that economy has elim‐
inated what would have made it alive. The disagreement does not im‐
mediately prove that one is incompetent. It may reveal a conflict
between two real values: formal necessity and generosity of detail.
Sometimes understanding more does not mean arriving at the same
preference, but discovering more precisely what each choice sacri‐
fices.
Nor is our own taste entirely stable within us. We may want a severe
form in the morning and an exuberant one in the evening, without
either self being the other’s impostor. Situation, fatigue, purpose, and
memory change how we become receptive to an object. This is a reas‐
on to describe the conditions of experience, not to abandon judgment.
A successful education of taste does not necessarily produce a stricter
tribunal. It can increase the number of pleasures available to us and
the number of distinctions we can defend. But there is a price: once
you begin hearing false resolutions, some old satisfactions disappear.
Learning to see does not mean only gaining. It can also mean losing a
pleasure’s innocence. We have no guarantee that every refinement
will make us happier.
This is the question pedagogical optimism avoids: is becoming more
demanding worthwhile if the world will satisfy us less often? The an‐
swer cannot simply be “yes, because discernment is superior.” We
must show what form of freedom, intensity, or understanding makes
the loss bearable. Otherwise, aesthetic education risks producing
people who know how to refuse everything and no longer know how
to receive anything.

CHAPTER 4
Cultures That Teach Us to See
Differently
An irregular cup, a mourning song, and a perfectly symmetrical
facade can become examples of aesthetic achievement. It does not fol‐
low that every form pleases everyone. It follows that objects enter dif‐
ferent practices, demanding different kinds of attention. The question
“which is more beautiful?” may be as poorly framed as asking wheth‐
er a conversation is better than a map. First we must discover what
each does and how it is encountered.
In Japanese aesthetic traditions, terms such as mono no aware, wabi,
and sabi indicate distinct emphases: sensitivity to transience, austere
simplicity, the traces of time. They are neither perfect synonyms nor a
declaration that every Japanese person prefers any imperfect object.
Different philosophers, practitioners, and periods have developed dif‐
ferent relationships with these concepts. [12] The comparative lesson
is not to replace one universal recipe with another, but to notice that
one experience can center transience where another celebrates per‐
manence.
Let us return to the hypothetical cup. If the question is “how precisely
does it reproduce an industrial template?” the deviation is a flaw. If
the question is “how does it make the encounter between material,
gesture, and time perceptible?” the same deviation may be relevant.
Not every crack becomes valuable: one may ruin the object, another
may belong to its history. Changing the criterion does not abolish judg‐
ment. It makes judgment more attentive to the practice from which
the object receives meaning.
In the Sanskrit tradition, debates about rasa concern the taste or savor
of artistic experience, especially in relation to dramatic and poetic
emotion. The anthology of primary texts edited by Sheldon Pollock re‐
veals a history of disputes, not a single Indian doctrine: what does the
spectator experience, how is the represented emotion transformed,
19


and why can receiving a tragedy involve pleasure? Rasa should not
automatically be translated as beauty, as though it answered the same
question in another language. [13]
The question is extraordinarily concrete. Why would we choose to
watch a sad story after an already difficult day? The answer “we like
sadness” is too simple. In an artistic experience, emotion can be or‐
ganized, shared, understood, held at a certain distance. These are in‐
terpretive possibilities, not a single explanation for all spectators. For
one person, the performance may bring clarity; for another, it may be
intolerable. Biographical context is not noise to be eliminated in order
to find true aesthetics.
Comparing traditions should thus be a method for producing better
questions. We need neither pretend all cultures ultimately say the
same thing nor that they are completely incommunicable worlds. We
can observe similarities of problem and differences of emphasis. Sens‐
itivity to form, the experience of loss, play, recognition, and prestige
may appear in many places, but their combinations are not identical.
Good translation also preserves what does not immediately fit.
A culture offers not only objects to appreciate but also instructions on
how they should be encountered. Some things require silence, others
participation; some require distance, others touch; some can be re‐
peated at any time, others acquire meaning on a particular date. If we
transport an object but remove the time and practice of the encounter,
we may preserve the material and lose the form of the experience.
This does not make translation impossible. It only means that a dis‐
play case is not a universal substitute for a world. We can learn some‐
thing from a foreign object without claiming to have recovered its cre‐
ators’ entire experience. It would be equally wrong to assume cultural
belonging guarantees understanding: within the same tradition there
are very different experiences, competencies, and conflicts.
Ritual: The Form of a Relationship
In the Analects, the connection between music, ritual, and human cul‐
tivation does not prevent distinctions. In III.25, Confucius appreciates
Shao as perfectly beautiful and good, and Wu as perfectly beautiful
but not perfectly good. In III.3, he asks what ritual and music mean
20


for someone lacking humanity. The texts offer no simple equation
between beauty and virtue: their very proximity makes it possible to
observe their disagreement. [14]
We can build a contemporary question from this without turning a
tradition into a recipe. Is a ceremony beautiful solely through move‐
ments, clothing, and sounds, or also through the relationships it en‐
acts? A greeting can be exact, restrained, full of consideration. The
same inclination of the head can be fear, irony, or submission. Visible
form does not exhaust the gesture; the relationship completes it.
At the same time, ritual is not false merely because it is learned. A fu‐
neral improvised in the name of authenticity can leave people without
a shared language of loss. An inherited form can carry what the indi‐
vidual cannot yet formulate. The danger arises when form demands a
feeling participants are obliged to simulate. The same framework can
support experience or replace it.
Shared Biology, Different Experiences
A study by Josh McDermott and his colleagues, published in 2016,
compared responses to sounds among Tsimane’ participants from the
Bolivian Amazon with those of other groups in Bolivia and the United
States. The Tsimane’ participants did not show the usual Western pref‐
erence for consonant over dissonant chords, although they could dis‐
criminate relevant acoustic differences and responded to acoustic
roughness. The result challenges the automatic equation between a
perceptible physical distinction and a universal hierarchy of pleasure.
It does not show that their music lacks aesthetic criteria. [15]
What we cannot infer matters. A single study does not demonstrate
that every musical preference is entirely cultural. Nor do differences
between environments prove the existence of genetic types of taste. A
comparison of populations simultaneously varies experiences, prac‐
tices, instruments, familiarity with the task, and many other condi‐
tions. Research improves when it separates these, not when it immedi‐
ately turns a result into an essential portrait of a civilization.
Let us construct another thought experiment. One child grows up in a
home where sonic improvisation is part of daily life. Another encoun‐
ters music mainly as a discreet background. In adulthood, a dense
21


passage may be an invitation to the first and clutter to the second. The
story has not demonstrated that this is exactly the mechanism of the
actual difference. It has shown why equality of sensory organs does
not compel identity of experiences. Biology makes learning possible;
learning changes what biology can do for us.
Moreover, no culture is a single observer. A city contains generations,
professions, religious communities, subcultures, and individual his‐
tories. Saying “Westerners love symmetry” or “Easterners love imper‐
fection” can become an elegant way of no longer looking at people.
Such large categories sometimes have comparative usefulness, but
must be accompanied by the question: which participants, which task,
which objects, at what moment?
The history of aesthetic standards also raises the problem of circula‐
tion. When a style spreads, its spread alone does not tell us whether it
was freely chosen, institutionally imposed, associated with prosperity,
or encountered more often. These mechanisms can coexist. Visible
uniformity is not sufficient evidence of biological universality, just as
visible diversity is not sufficient evidence that biology does not matter.
Confusing one conclusion with the other means using data as decora‐
tion for a prior conviction.
What we call cultural beauty can also be the fit between several prac‐
tices: how people speak at the table, how an object is cared for, how a
guest is welcomed, the rhythm of a celebration. We then admire a
world of relationships, not a collection of settings. This coherence can
have real value without making the tradition immune to criticism.
The difficult experiment is to imagine a highly coherent culture that
leaves no room for those who do not fit. Its coherence could be truly
beautiful and yet costly. The pluralist alternative could be freer but
more fragmented. We do not know in advance that freedom will al‐
ways produce a more harmonious composition. The question is
whether we can cultivate shared forms that do not require every life
to become a variation on the same theme.
Encountering other aesthetics does not mean collecting exotica or de‐
claring that all judgments have equal weight. It means discovering
that some supposed laws of sensibility were local habits, while some
22


supposed oddities become intelligible through practice. Beauty can be
what a culture teaches us to recognize; it can also be what makes us
discover the limits of the culture that educated us.

CHAPTER 5
The Pleasure of Seeing and the
Pleasure of Understanding
A melody can go from irritating to indispensable without a single note
changing. A poem can remain opaque until someone shows us how
the sentence breathes. The change matters philosophically: if beauty
were only in the object, where would this difference come from? But
if it were only an arbitrary invention of the mind, why can we not de‐
cide to like anything, immediately and equally?
An influential explanation formulated by Rolf Reber, Norbert
Schwarz, and Piotr Winkielman centers on processing fluency: the
ease with which the mind organizes what it encounters. Clarity, fa‐
miliarity, or an easily grasped structure can contribute to pleasure,
and this pleasure can participate in aesthetic judgment. The theory
also discusses the role of expectations and how we interpret the
source of ease. It does not claim that all simple things are beautiful or
that a work’s value reduces to how quickly it is recognized. [16]
Consider two situations, not as experimental evidence but as tests for
intuition. In the first, a poster becomes more pleasing when a clutter
of lettering is removed. In the second, a novel becomes more interest‐
ing when we discover that an apparently useless detail reorganizes
everything we have read. The first gains through immediate ease; the
second through a difficulty that comes to make sense. In both cases
there is a relationship between form and the capacity to grasp it, but
the experiences are not identical.
From this we can construct a more general hypothesis: some aesthetic
pleasure might come not only from order but from discovering an or‐
der we did not yet possess. This is an explanatory proposal, not a law
demonstrated for all the arts. It should be tested by separating famili‐
arity, surprise, understanding, and pleasure. Otherwise, any result
could retrospectively be renamed “discovered order,” and the theory
would no longer risk anything.
24


Aesthetic experience does not always require surprise. Sometimes we
want precisely the reliable return of a familiar form. At other times,
familiarity may become saturation for a particular listener. This sug‐
gests why the question “what is the optimal complexity?” needs quali‐
fications: for whom, after how much exposure, and in an experience
of what duration? An object’s difficulty is not only its own property; it
is also the distance between its organization and the observer’s com‐
petence.
An ambiguous image offers a more precise case than speculation
about any masterpiece. In Claudia Muth and Claus-Christian Carbon’s
experiments, the emergence of an “aha” insight concerning ambigu‐
ous images was associated with increased appreciation of them. The
result supports the role of discovery in certain aesthetic experiences;
it does not prove that every work must hide a puzzle or that every
solved puzzle becomes beautiful. [17]
We can feel the difference between two satisfactions here. The first
says “it is easy to receive.” The second says “now I see why it seemed
difficult.” The second may be more intense precisely because it in‐
cludes the memory of resistance. A novel without difficulties may
keep us pleasant company. A novel that retrospectively reorganizes
what we have read can change our experience of the whole. But diffi‐
culty is fruitful only when it produces something; confusion without
gain does not become depth through persistence.
In Paul Silvia’s research, interest is linked to appraisals of novelty or
complexity and the feeling that we can understand what we en‐
counter. The experiments distinguish interest from simple pleasure: a
configuration can invite exploration without being the most agree‐
able. This distinction is useful when we say a work captivates us al‐
though we do not like it in the ordinary sense. [18]
Is Beauty Successful Compression?
Jürgen Schmidhuber proposed an algorithmic model in which regu‐
larities can be described more compactly, and progress in compres‐
sion fuels interest and curiosity. From this perspective, what seemed
random becomes intelligible to a learning observer. The model is a
25


fruitful theoretical hypothesis about agents and learning, not an ex‐
perimental law that has reduced every form of beauty to the length of
a program. [19]
Let us see what it explains and where it begins to creak. The sequence
2, 4, 6, 8, 10 can be compressed by the rule “positive even numbers, in
order.” Once understood, it offers little surprise. A sequence without
detectable regularity may be very difficult to compress, but does not
necessarily demand admiration. Between banality and noise, the pos‐
sibility of discovery appears: a structure we did not see but that al‐
lows itself to be seen.
The problem is that beauty does not always seem to disappear after
discovery. We can return to the same music without learning a new
rule each time. We can admire a water surface whose general prin‐
ciples are familiar to us. Compression theory might reply that there
are new details, new levels, or benefits of recognition. But if every
pleasure is retrospectively reinterpreted as a type of compression, the
theory becomes difficult to refute. Its strength depends on predictions
that can fail.
One possible test would compare objects with similar regularity but
different emotional histories. Another would separate progress in un‐
derstanding from the reward of familiar recognition. We might also
ask whether an explanation offered before the encounter increases
appreciation or destroys the very pleasure of discovery. We do not
present these comparisons as results already obtained. They show
what would need to be measured to move from an elegant metaphor
to a stronger explanation.
What Does a Brain See When It Says “Beautiful”?
Tomohiro Ishizu and Semir Zeki investigated experiences of visual
and musical beauty through brain imaging. In their study’s tasks,
beauty ratings were associated with activity in regions including the
medial orbitofrontal cortex. The result suggests shared evaluative
components in experiences produced through different sensory mod‐
alities. It does not demonstrate that the researchers found a cerebral
apparatus that reads the world’s objective beauty. [20]
26


A brain signal recorded during an aesthetic evaluation describes the
observer’s processes in that task. It must be distinguished from re‐
sponses to reward, attention, and other states. Otherwise we risk a
circle: we identify a region using people’s ratings, then invoke it to
demonstrate that those ratings are correct.
Localization does not explain why this sentence moves us and another
does not. Form, memory, and interpretation must also be investigated.
Brain analysis and analysis of meaning can describe different levels of
the same event.
There is, however, a powerful cultural temptation to consider what
appears in a technical image more real than what is described in
words. With beauty, this temptation produces an interesting error: we
tried to explain the power of appearances and ended up convinced by
the scientific appearance of a colored map. The image deserves trust
through its method, not its visual solemnity.
The important question is not whether beauty “is in the brain” or “is
in the world,” as though experience had to live in a single drawer.
Without the object we do not have this encounter; without the observ‐
er’s capacities we do not have this way of experiencing it. A serious
explanation will have to connect the two without making either the
object or the person disappear.
At the end of the neural explanation, a problem of description also re‐
mains. Two experiences can involve shared evaluative mechanisms
without having the same content. Saying that a system participates in
appreciating an equation and a melody does not mean the equation
has become music or that we have identified beauty’s metaphysical
essence. A shared mechanism may be the site of convergence, not the
origin of all differences.
But we should not use this caution to isolate beauty from science.
Questions about learning, attention, memory, and reward are real.
They can help us understand why an object becomes accessible, why a
preference changes, and why a form captures the eye so quickly. What
they cannot decide alone is whether that way of seeing deserves cul‐
tivation. Here, investigation of the mechanism meets judgment about
the life we want to live.

CHAPTER 6
Darwin: A World Also Shaped by
What Pleases
Viewed solely as a survival machine, a creature should be economical.
Why carry a bulky ornament, consume energy in a display, or become
more conspicuous? The question changes when we observe that stay‐
ing alive and having offspring are not the same problem. An advant‐
age at one stage can come with a cost at another.
In The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex, Darwin de‐
veloped the explanation of sexual selection: traits may be favored
through success in competition for mates or through the choices those
mates exercise. Beauty thus enters a history of preferences, not just a
geometry of forms. Darwin’s book remains a fundamental source, but
its passages about humans also contain contemporary prejudices re‐
garding sex and hierarchies among populations. The theory must be
examined separately from these historical assessments. [21]
A famous experiment by Malte Andersson, published in 1982, offers
something stronger than a plausible story. In males of a bird species,
Euplectes progne, the researcher experimentally altered tail length.
Males with elongated tails had greater mating success than those in
the comparison groups. The result supports the role of female choice
in maintaining the ornament. Alone, it demonstrates neither how the
preference arose nor that human preferences work identically. [22]
A testable evolutionary explanation specifies what result would sup‐
port the proposed mechanism and what would challenge it. The con‐
venient explanation observes the ornament and retrospectively de‐
clares it useful. If any result can be described as useful, we have not
explained selection: we have renamed the trait’s persistence.
But why would an organism prefer a costly ornament? There are sev‐
eral families of answers. The preference may relate to information
about the bearer; it may exploit perceptual sensitivities developed in
other contexts; or it may evolve in interaction with the preferred trait.
Mark Kirkpatrick’s formal model shows how mate choice and orna‐
28


ment can evolve together under certain conditions. Such a model es‐
tablishes a mechanism’s possibility under explicit assumptions, not
that it explains every case in nature. [23]
The distinction is crucial. A preference need not be a perfect test of
health to have evolutionary effects. Nor does an ornament become an
honest signal merely because it looks expensive. We must investigate
who can produce it, under what conditions, and whether the observer
obtains useful information from it. Cost alone does not certify truth.
Waste can also be just waste.
The importance of this change of perspective is hard to exaggerate: a
creature’s environment includes other creatures’ preferences. There
are not only temperature, food, predators, and obstacles. There is also
a biological audience. What attracts that audience can alter reproduct‐
ive prospects, even if the ornament is costly in other respects. “Useful‐
ness” cannot be judged solely from the standpoint of individual sur‐
vival.
Nevertheless, “biological audience” is an analogy, not proof that an
animal has the human experience of beauty. We can observe choices,
preferences, and reproductive effects. Access to subjective experience
is a separate problem. Calling an ornament beautiful to us and finding
it preferred by a mate of the same species are not equivalent claims.
Preference Can Create What Later Seems
Necessary
To understand the logic of models of the coevolution of preference
and ornament, imagine a population in which a certain trait becomes
preferred. Preferred individuals have more reproductive opportunit‐
ies under the conditions assumed by the model. If the trait and relev‐
ant predispositions are transmitted in a way that sustains their associ‐
ation, the process can amplify itself. Kirkpatrick’s model formalizes
conditions of this kind; it does not claim that every actual preference
follows the same history. [23]
The conceptual consequence matters. A trait can become advantage‐
ous because it is preferred, without the preference always being a
transparent instrument for detecting an independent quality. The his‐
tory of choices can construct the selective environment. A parallel
29


with fashion appears here, but a parallel is not an identity: cultural
transmission, deliberation, and human institutions add mechanisms
that cannot be tacitly inserted into a genetic model.
Other possibilities also exist: preference may use sensitivities de‐
veloped in other contexts; the ornament may provide information
about its bearer’s condition; choice may depend on learning or ecolo‐
gical constraints. These families of explanations must be compared
through different predictions. Discovering a costly ornament and an‐
nouncing that its very cost proves its honesty is not enough. Cost may
be a condition within a model, not a universal certificate for the sig‐
nal. [21, 23]
The least interesting Darwinism is the kind that can explain any ima‐
ginable outcome after the fact. If people prefer symmetry, it would be
for health; if they prefer a distinctive feature, for diversity; if they
prefer the familiar, for safety; if they prefer the unknown, for explora‐
tion. Each hypothesis can make sense. Together, without distinguish‐
ing conditions, they become a collection of stories that no longer risk
anything.
Beyond Reproduction, Without Leaving Nature
In ordinary language, “selected” sounds almost like “approved.” Here
arises one of the most dangerous translations from biology into mor‐
ality. Natural selection is not a jury rewarding merit. Reproductive
success does not mean an organism is more worthy, happier, or better.
It is a relationship between traits, environment, and their transmis‐
sion across generations. Turning this description into a rule about
who deserves resources means adding a moral judgment the mechan‐
ism does not contain.
Moreover, the evolutionary explanation of a capacity does not exhaust
its uses. Even if some of our sensitivity to forms had a history connec‐
ted to mate choice, it would not follow that every sonata is a disguised
mating request. That reduction would require additional evidence. A
capacity can be repurposed, combined with others, and transformed
through culture. Origin does not exhaustively dictate present mean‐
ing.
30


An imaginary sculptor may begin working for prestige and end up
pursuing a problem that brings neither money nor mates. An evolu‐
tionary hypothesis does not offer privileged access to every individual
motivation.
Three questions must also be separated: why a sensitivity arose, how
it produces pleasure today, and what social role that pleasure re‐
ceives. The first is evolutionary, the second concerns current mechan‐
isms, and the third concerns institutions and relationships. The an‐
swers can support one another but cannot substitute for one another.
The existence of a biological mechanism does not explain why a par‐
ticular institution decides to reward it.
A reductionist argument says that art, science, and the elegance of
ideas are merely refined versions of displaying quality. The counterar‐
gument need not deny that performance can bring prestige or mates.
It need only observe that a possible reproductive consequence does
not exhaust an activity’s content. A proof can be valid independently
of its maker’s motivation. A melody can create an experience the
listener seeks without an audience and without any intention of dis‐
play.
Here we must distinguish a capacity’s origin, its current mechanism,
and the objects it comes to pursue. The capacity to learn regularities
can have an evolutionary history and later be used for domains that
did not exist in ancestral environments. We do not need a genetic pro‐
gram for each sonata or architectural style. A flexible sensitivity can
generate historical consequences selection did not anticipate, because
selection does not anticipate.
The provocative question is therefore not whether beauty takes us out
of biology. Nothing in human experience must be removed from
nature to matter. The question is how much autonomy a system of
preferences can acquire once it begins modifying its own environ‐
ment. We can construct ideals so costly that they make our lives
harder. We can prefer a work that does not soothe us or a truth that
does not benefit us. The origin of the capacity to prefer does not pre‐
determine everything we will prefer.
31


In this sense, beauty can be a product of selection, a factor that
changes selection, and an experience whose value need not be calcu‐
lated in offspring. Maintaining all this is not contradictory. A contra‐
diction would arise only if we confused the biological explanation of a
capacity with an obligation for every use of it to maximize the same
quantity.

The Body: Between Pattern and
Presence
A face does not come with a medical report, a moral certificate, and
an estimate of intelligence. Yet the impression it produces can seem
surprisingly rich. We feel not only that we see the person but that we
understand them. The problem is not noticing an expression or a
form. It begins when we extend the impression beyond what it can
support.
The meta-analysis by Judith Langlois and her colleagues found agree‐
ment among raters about attractiveness, including cross-cultural com‐
parisons, and tendencies to evaluate and treat people considered at‐
tractive more favorably. The existence of agreement challenges the
idea that taste in faces is completely random. It proves neither a single
canon independent of experience nor the moral superiority of those
receiving favorable ratings. [24]
Statistical agreement must be read carefully. Two groups can have
similar average ratings while individuals within each group differ
greatly. Moreover, agreement about selected photographs is not
identical to agreement about all real people. A photograph fixes an
angle, an expression, a light, and a stage of life. Interaction adds voice,
movement, humor, smell, behavior, and shared history. Extrapolating
without qualification from one context to the other means changing
the question while answering it.
Research on faces also often assumes a link between symmetry, devel‐
opmental quality, and health. A large study by Nicholas Pound and
colleagues did not find the expected association between fluctuating
facial asymmetry and childhood illnesses in the British cohort ex‐
amined. The finding weakens that specific link. It does not show that
appearance can offer no clue about health, but neither does it permit
symmetry to serve as a general certificate of superior biology. [25]
33


There are two distinct leaps here. The first moves from a visible trait
to a hidden biological characteristic. The second moves from that
characteristic to the person’s worth. Even if the first were justified in a
precise situation, the second would not follow automatically. An ill
person has no fewer rights because the illness is visible. Medicine may
have reasons to observe a sign; this does not give an institution the
right to turn a face into a ranking of dignity.
What It Means for Taste to Be Formed
Laura Germine and her colleagues studied individual differences in
preferences for faces, including comparisons between twins. In their
model, variation in individual preferences was largely explained by
environmental influences specific to the person rather than genetic
differences. The result does not deny the biological basis of vision and
attraction. It concerns the explanation of variation among individuals
in a particular population and task, and the environmental compon‐
ent in such models may also include measurement error. [26]
Such a finding opens an interesting space between two simplifica‐
tions. We are neither identical machines reading the same formula
nor beings who choose taste by decree. Experiences can help form a
preference without the person being able to reconstruct its exact
genealogy. “That is what I like” can be sincere and, at the same time,
insufficient to explain how that taste arose.
What we call bodily beauty is not merely a geometry of the face, how‐
ever. There is beauty of posture, movement, voice, how someone occu‐
pies or frees up space. To notice the difference, imagine two record‐
ings of the same person. In the first, they monitor every movement. In
the second, they attentively carry out a task and briefly forget the
camera. We can respond differently without their bodily proportions
having changed.
The example does not demonstrate that spontaneity is always more
attractive. Sometimes deliberate precision is exactly what we admire.
It shows that presence is a temporal phenomenon: it includes expecta‐
tions, adjustments, relationships, and meaning. A photograph cap‐
tures something real, but not everything that can become aesthetically
significant in a body.

Exemplary Beauty and Distinctive Beauty
A face may please because it seems close to a recognized pattern. An‐
other may become memorable through what does not perfectly fit the
pattern. We should not turn this observation into a law that imperfec‐
tion is always more beautiful. The distinctive can be fascinating,
agreeable, disturbing, or simply unpleasant. The point is that a fea‐
ture’s value also depends on the configuration in which it appears.
The same peculiarity can seem a flaw in an isolated photograph and
organize a moving person’s expressiveness. In a painted portrait, the
artist may accentuate precisely what a homogenizing application
would correct. We then have two aesthetic projects: approaching a
canon and constructing a perceptible identity. They can support one
another, but can also contradict one another.
Imagine a technology that could instantly modify any face toward a
group’s average preferences. The first results might seem more agree‐
able. But if all faces approached the same configuration, rarity and in‐
dividual recognition would change. It does not follow that attraction
would disappear. It follows that locally optimizing a score does not tell
us what experience will look like in a population transformed by that
same optimization.
There is another difficulty: beauty can become an impossible standard
to inhabit when detached from concrete existence. An ideal photo‐
graph does not tire, age while being viewed, change its mood, or de‐
mand reciprocity. If we judge a person through competition with a
fixed, selected image, we are not comparing two equivalent speci‐
mens. We are comparing a life with a presentation.
This observation does not require contempt for beautiful photo‐
graphy, makeup, or style. Presentation is also a space for expression.
The problem arises when we forget what we selected and begin de‐
manding that life behave permanently like the result of that selection.
A face must remain recognizable as belonging to someone, not merely
as a surface performing for an evaluator.
The body has another peculiarity that the analogy with an object con‐
ceals: it can respond to being looked at. A painting does not learn that
we are evaluating it. A person can learn this and change their gesture,
35


confidence, vulnerability, or receptiveness. Interpersonal beauty can
thus develop in a loop. Admiring does not mean merely recording a
property; it can mean changing the situation in which that property
appears.
This loop is not inevitably manipulative. Being viewed with admira‐
tion can make room for expressiveness that would otherwise remain
hidden. But it can also create a prison: the person feels they must re‐
main the form that was admired. Beauty sometimes offers the free‐
dom to enter a room and, simultaneously, the fear of never escaping
the role for which you were admitted.
Here the question “is it real or constructed?” reveals its limit. A pres‐
ence can be real precisely because it is constructed in relationship. Its
dependence on light, movement, history, and attention does not inval‐
idate it. But neither does it turn it into a permanent essence someone
should carry uninterruptedly, like a deed of ownership.

CHAPTER 8
Desire, Intimacy, and Singular
Beauty
A profile receives numerous likes. The person finds a partner. The re‐
lationship lasts. The couple has children. These sentences may de‐
scribe the same life, but none logically follows from the preceding
one. They are four different outcomes, produced in different contexts.
Compressing them into “biological success” can make an explanation
seem more complete than it is.
Immediate attraction is a reaction. Forming a couple involves mutual
choice and favorable circumstances. A relationship’s continuity re‐
quires, among other things, practical compatibility. Reproduction adds
its own intentions, possibilities, and constraints. And the number of
children born does not exhaust every question about the transmission
of traits across generations. A study must specify what outcome it
measured rather than allowing the reader to slide from one meaning
to another.
Markus Jokela’s study of attractiveness and reproductive success in
the United States addresses precisely the connection between appear‐
ance and reproduction, not merely appearance and others’ evalu‐
ations. Yet the question’s importance does not justify turning an obser‐
vational analysis from one historical context into a law about the spe‐
cies’ future. Participant selection criteria, period, attractiveness meas‐
urement, and family variables can change the interpretation. [27]
To see the difficulty, we can construct two imaginary societies. In the
first, people with aesthetic prestige have more opportunities for part‐
nerships and larger families. In the second, those same opportunities
are associated with career paths that delay starting a family, and pref‐
erences about children differ. The same initial attraction can have dis‐
tinct relationships with the number of descendants. We have not
proved that the real world follows either story. We have shown why a
simple causal arrow cannot be assumed.
37


Equally, a correlation between an aesthetic rating and the number of
children does not automatically tell us how much of the observed dif‐
ference will be genetically transmitted. The rating may include groom‐
ing, expression, material conditions, and observers’ norms. A claim
about evolutionary change requires more than two correlated
columns in a table. The traits, their transmission, and their relation‐
ship with reproduction in the studied environment must be defined.
Why Do We Not Love a Ranking?
Suppose you could identify the person who would obtain the highest
attractiveness score for you in a very well-designed test. Would it fol‐
low that you should want a life alongside them? Not necessarily. The
test might measure an important dimension without measuring a re‐
lationship’s rhythm, trust, conversation, humor, or mutual availability.
An intense preference can be a beginning rather than a destiny.
There is, however, a symmetrical mistake: describing physical attrac‐
tion as superficial to protect an elevated image of love. The body is not
accidental packaging for the person. Voice, closeness, movement,
smell, and touch can participate in how someone becomes present to
someone else. The philosophical problem is not solved by devaluing
one dimension, but by understanding how they combine.
Love also adds a difficulty for simple theories of beauty. In a thought
experiment, someone discovers after years that their partner’s expres‐
sion seems beautiful precisely because they recognize care, irony, or a
shared history in it. An outside evaluator of the photograph does not
see the same relationship. We need not immediately choose between
“one is right” and “the other is deluded.” The two are not necessarily
responding to the same object in its full sense: one sees a configura‐
tion, the other encounters a person they know.
Can this singular beauty be a form of knowledge? Sometimes, yes: we
notice expressions and relationships inaccessible to a hurried viewer.
But it can also be idealization. Intimacy is not immune to error. It can
reveal a person and manufacture a version of them that we need. The
fact that an experience is deeply personal does not automatically tell
us whether it is truer.
38


Consider an imaginary case. After years of closeness, a scar becomes
beautiful to someone who knows its story. Not because suffering is
good, but because the mark has become part of a beloved presence. A
stranger sees only the irregularity. Neither simply sees “the object
without interpretation.” The first may have access to a real meaning;
the second may perceive a formal property better. There is no obliga‐
tion for all forms of attention to produce the same verdict.
Being Desired and Becoming Visible
Attraction is asymmetrical. My admiration’s sincerity does not create
the other person’s duty to confirm it. But the absence of a duty does
not cancel the pain. A lucid analysis can hold together the freedom of
desire and the reality of exclusion without turning either into a denial
of the other. A world where every choice is free can produce distribu‐
tions of attention that some experience as a sentence of condemna‐
tion.
From this arises the temptation of accounting: who receives more,
who remains invisible, who can choose, who accepts? It captures
something important but misses mutual transformation. Relationships
are not always transactions between fixed evaluations. Sometimes the
other becomes attractive during a shared activity. At other times, an
initial beauty fades with the relationship. Neither change proves that
the first impression was dishonest; the experiences had different ob‐
jects and conditions.
There is also the beauty of non-erotic intimacy: gestures through
which two friends no longer need to explain everything, how a parent
recognizes a child’s rhythm, a silence that need not be filled. Not every
such relationship is beautiful, and familiarity can also become suffoc‐
ating. But when we appreciate the fit between freedom, trust, and
rhythm, using the word does not seem a completely arbitrary meta‐
phor.
Singular beauty challenges the economics of substitution. A better-
performing object can replace another. A person ranked higher by one
criterion does not automatically replace a relationship. Value lies not
only in comparable properties but also in the fact that a certain his‐
39


tory happened between certain beings. This does not make every
bond irrevocable. It does, however, make intelligible why some losses
cannot be compensated by a “better” specimen.
The remaining question is uncomfortable: does love teach us to see
beauty others miss, or make us call beautiful what we cannot bear to
lose? Sometimes one, sometimes the other, perhaps often both. A suffi‐
ciently profound theory must allow this mixture without declaring
either love infallible or its beauty a mere sentimental error.

Why Can a Proof Be Beautiful?
Sometimes the solution does not seem to have defeated the problem.
It seems to have found the place from which the problem ceases to
resist.
Add the numbers from 1 to 100. You can begin with 1 + 2, then add 3,
then 4, and continue. Or you can notice that the first and last make
101, the second and penultimate also make 101, and the process pro‐
duces 50 pairs. The result is 5.050. Both methods are correct, but they
do not offer the same experience. The first advances through opera‐
tions; the second changes the task’s form.
We need not attribute the anecdote to a child genius to feel its eleg‐
ance. The mathematical facts suffice. For a positive integer n, we can
write the sum forwards and backwards. Each of the n columns con‐
tains n + 1. We have counted the sum twice, so:
1 + 2 + … + n = n(n + 1)/2
The beauty lies not merely in shortening the calculation. It lies in the
emergence of a relationship that makes the result inevitable. Once
seen, the method seems natural. But this very impression can conceal
the important act: someone changed the representation. Intellectual
elegance is often the power to find the representation in which a diffi‐
culty becomes transparent.
Infinity in a Finite Step
Now suppose someone claims to have a list of all prime numbers. A
prime number is an integer greater than 1 with only two positive di‐
visors, 1 and itself. The supposedly complete list contains p₁, p₂,
through pₙ. We construct a new number: their product, plus 1.
N = p₁ × p₂ × … × pₙ + 1
41


N is not exactly divisible by any prime on the list, because each divi‐
sion leaves a remainder of 1. If N is prime, we have a missing prime. If
N is not prime, it has a prime divisor, and that divisor cannot be on
the list. In either case, the list was not complete. Therefore, no finite
list of all prime numbers exists.
This is the core of the classical proof of the infinitude of primes. No‐
tice the precision preserving its beauty: the constructed number need
not itself be prime. For example, 2 × 3 × 5 × 7 × 11 × 13 + 1 = 30.031,
and 30.031 = 59 × 509. Saying the method always produces a new
prime would turn an elegant proof into a false claim. The argument’s
quality depends precisely on the distinction an overly hasty account
might eliminate.
Why admiration, rather than just satisfaction at a correct result? Be‐
cause a finite operation rules out all purported finite lists. The argu‐
ment does not hunt down the first primes, then the next ones, end‐
lessly. It changes levels: it shows why any attempt to close the list can
be opened again. Generality was obtained not through enumeration
but through a construction applicable to any supposedly complete list.
Several sources of mathematical beauty can be distinguished here:
economy of means, the idea’s surprise, the power of generalization,
the transparency of necessity, and connections between domains.
They need not appear together. A very general proof can be difficult to
follow. A visual one can be immediately convincing but limited. A
short one can hide an enormous theory in a single technical word.
Simplicity on the page is not the same as simplicity of the knowledge
required.
When a Formula Begins to Mean Something
Semir Zeki, John Romaya, Dionigi Benincasa, and Michael Atiyah stud‐
ied the experience of mathematical beauty among participants with
relevant training. Ratings of the beauty of formulas were associated
with activity in the medial orbitofrontal cortex. The study shows that
appreciating abstract objects can have evaluative correlates compar‐
able in certain respects to other aesthetic experiences. It demonstrates
neither that more beautiful formulas are true nor that brain activity
replaces proof. [28]
42


In mathematics we can distinguish at least a proposition’s truth with‐
in the given framework, the proof’s correctness, and the elegance of
the way it is proved. A long, cumbersome proof can be correct. A se‐
ductive one can conceal an unjustified step. Elegance is a good reason
to investigate; it is not permission to skip verification.
The cited study’s data must be read at their scale: the imaging analysis
included 15 mathematicians after one participant’s exclusion. We do
not have a referendum on mathematical beauty or proof of a special
organ for truth. We have an association between competent people’s
ratings and brain activity under the conditions defined by the experi‐
ment. [28]
A formula such as Euler’s identity can impress through the relation‐
ship between notions that seemed separate. But to someone unfamili‐
ar with complex numbers, the exponential function, and the role of π,
it may be only compact calligraphy. This does not mean the uniniti‐
ated person has less sensitivity. It means the relevant object of experi‐
ence is not merely the visible string of signs. It is the structure the
signs make accessible.
Conversely, familiarity can dull surprise. An identity that once in‐
spired wonder can become a tool used automatically. The mathem‐
atician has not thereby demonstrated that the original beauty was
false; their relationship with the object has changed. Sometimes teach‐
ing restores its beauty by requiring them to see again the distance
between beginning and understanding.
There is also local elegance within a globally cumbersome proof. A
single step can be extraordinary although the rest of the argument re‐
quires many checks. Demanding a solution of a few lines for every
theorem is an aesthetic prejudice, not a condition of truth. Some struc‐
tures resist compression accessible to us. The absence of a pleasing
form is not proof that the problem has been poorly understood.
Beauty as a Tool for Searching
Before proof, elegance can guide the search: a symmetry suggests a
generalization, two similar cases invite a common formulation. After
proof, it can aid memory and transmission. Between these moments,
43


however, lies a decisive break. What suggests does not guarantee. An
argument can seem inevitable because we missed precisely the case
contradicting it.
We can devise an exercise for any idea we have fallen in love with:
seek the smallest change in assumptions under which it stops work‐
ing. An elegant argument about sorting numbers does not automatic‐
ally transfer to objects for which comparison is not well defined. A
property proved for a finite number of steps may not carry over to in‐
finity without an additional argument. Beauty becomes more interest‐
ing when we know exactly what limits it holds together.
Verification does not necessarily destroy the pleasure of proof. Some‐
times we discover that the argument withstands scrutiny better than
expected. Admiration then becomes more than seduction: it has
passed through the possibility of failure.

CHAPTER 10
Scientific Elegance and the
Indifference of the Universe
A theory can be so beautiful that we feel ashamed to demand evid‐
ence. That is exactly when we should demand it.
An equation uniting many phenomena promises extraordinary eco‐
nomy. Instead of a collection of local rules, a common principle ap‐
pears. But science is not the mathematics of worlds chosen solely by
us. A model can be impeccable internally and poorly describe the
world encountered. Here intellectual beauty changes its status: from a
quality of construction, it also becomes a wager about reality.
To see the difference, let us construct a toy world. You have observed
the values 2, 4, 8, and 16. The rule “double each time” is short and sug‐
gests 32. But another rule can coincide in the first four positions and
produce 31 in the fifth. Any finite number of observations can be in‐
cluded in alternative mathematical constructions. Preferring the short
rule is reasonable in many contexts, but does not turn finite observa‐
tions into a proof of the future.
The experiment does not say all theories are equally good. Rival rules
can differ in new predictions, stability under additional data, connec‐
tions to other explanations, and the number of arbitrary adjustments.
It says only that the pleasure of simplicity does not alone do the work
of these comparisons. A theory is more interesting when its simplicity
produces the risk of a precise prediction.
Symmetry Is Not Always What the Eye Sees
In physics, symmetry can designate a description’s invariance under a
transformation, not an object’s visual beauty. Within variational the‐
ories and under the appropriate mathematical assumptions, Emmy
Noether’s results connect continuous symmetries of the action to con‐
servation laws. In suitable contexts, invariance under time transla‐
45


tions relates to conservation of energy, and invariance under spatial
translations to conservation of momentum. Not every discrete sym‐
metry or symmetrical image automatically produces such a law. [29]
The aesthetic power of such a result comes from an unexpected con‐
nection: what does not change when we modify the description con‐
strains what can evolve in the system. We admire not merely a com‐
pact symbol. We admire the fact that two apparently different ques‐
tions are connected through a demonstrable mechanism. Here eleg‐
ance does not replace rigor; it is one of the forms in which rigor be‐
comes intelligible.
But the mathematical result does not alone decide which symmetries
exist in a given physical situation. A description may include an ex‐
ternal environment, explicit time dependence, or boundaries where
the assumptions are no longer those presumed. It would be a mistake
to demand that the universe behave according to a simplified version
of the theorem because that version sounds beautiful. First the model
must be established, then the conditions, then the relationship with
observations.
Notice also a change in sensibility: scientific beauty may require
abandoning immediately visible symmetry in favor of a more abstract
structure. What seems irregular at one level may have regularity at
another. Conversely, a highly ordered image may come from a selec‐
tion of data concealing the relevant processes. Photography and ex‐
planation do not have the same task.
The Short Theory and the Enormous Footnote
Let us compare two hypothetical models of a city. The first says all
journeys follow the shortest route. The second includes cost, accessib‐
ility, schedules, safety, preferences, and uncertainty. The first is more
elegant on a page. But if we add a verbal exception after every wrong
prediction, the cost of description has not disappeared. It has moved
from the formula into the explanations with which we defend it.
Honest simplicity counts assumptions, adjustments, and the informa‐
tion hidden in the choice of representation. A parameter summarizing
hundreds of unexplained decisions is not necessarily a victory over
46


complexity. It may merely be a spacious drawer. At the same time, an
incomplete model can remain excellent for a limited purpose. We
need not explain the entire world to calculate something useful.
Thus emerges the beauty of a well-chosen domain. An approximation
can be elegant because it knows where it works and where it must be
abandoned. A map marking the limits of its precision is intellectually
more mature than one offering equally firm outlines everywhere. This
is no shameful concession. Sometimes delimitation is the principal
achievement itself.
When Truth Looks Untidy
We can imagine a researcher discovering that the phenomenon under
study depends on three historical mechanisms, each important in a
different region. The story has no single theoretical hero. It is tempt‐
ing to consider it a provisional stage before a beautiful law. Perhaps
there will be unification; perhaps the relevant level actually retains
traces of a contingent history. We have no right to choose the answer
solely through a preference for tidiness.
This does not mean abandoning the ideal of unification. It means it is
a research program with an opportunity cost. Insistence on a shared
structure can produce an extraordinary discovery or delay noticing
differences. Beauty is useful when it makes us ask “what relationship
have we missed?” It becomes rigid when it makes us declare “the rela‐
tionship must exist, otherwise the world is badly constructed.”
A false thesis can remain beautiful as a mathematical object or an
episode of imagination, although it can no longer be defended as a
physical explanation. We need not call it ugly to reject it. This separa‐
tion allows us to preserve what we gained without turning attach‐
ment into a research method.
Perhaps the rarest form of scientific elegance is the capacity to give
something up at exactly the right moment. Not the theatrical aban‐
donment of every bold hypothesis, but acceptance of a test that could
destroy the favored idea. If the theory survives, its beauty gains
weight. If not, there remains the beauty of the question it taught us to
ask and perhaps of an error formulated clearly enough to be correc‐
ted.

The Beauty of Concepts and the
Trap of Total Explanation
A short argument can produce intense satisfaction: many things that
seemed separate become consequences of a single idea. But the same
sensation can also be offered by an oversimplified explanation that
has eliminated precisely the inconvenient details. How do we distin‐
guish an intelligent compression of the world from an elegant impov‐
erishment of it?
A good concept is not merely a shorter name. It makes possible dis‐
tinctions, inferences, and questions previously difficult to formulate.
“Feedback,” for example, can reveal how an action’s result returns
and modifies the next action. We can thus discuss a thermostat, a rela‐
tionship, or a market without pretending they are identical. The gain
lies in a shared structure; the risk lies in unjustifiably transferring de‐
tails from one domain to another.
Conceptual elegance emerges when a tool organizes many cases while
preserving the differences we need. Covering everything is not
enough. The word “thing” applies to many objects but explains little. A
powerful concept must exclude, discriminate, tell us what follows and
what does not follow from using it.
Consider “power.” It can designate the capacity to produce an effect,
control over others, prestige, influence, or available energy per unit of
time. Sometimes analogy between meanings is fruitful. At other times,
it manufactures a proof merely by sliding from one meaning to anoth‐
er. A formulation’s beauty can make the shift difficult to notice. The
word stays the same; the problem has moved.
A Theory That Can No Longer Lose
We can imagine a social model explaining every decision through a
single interest. The model is easy to recount. All altruism is reinter‐
preted as hidden self-interest, every counterexample as concealment.
Its apparent elegance comes from its inability to lose. But a theory
48


that wins regardless of what happens may have risked nothing in‐
formative. An explanation’s beauty must be compatible with the real
possibility of being wrong.
The opposite is no virtue either: complexity does not certify depth. An
overloaded theoretical apparatus can protect the same lack of contact
with data. The important criterion is the relationship between what
the model claims, what it excludes, and what we can verify. The most
trustworthy intellectual beauty would be that which survives this dis‐
cipline, not that used to avoid it.
A total explanation can seduce through the promise that nothing re‐
mains accidental. Every gesture, every opposition, every objection
finds its place. Imagine a doctrine saying that every critic confirms,
through criticism, fear of the doctrine’s truth. It has turned counterar‐
gument into fuel. The resulting coherence can be impressive. But we
no longer know what experience would distinguish the world where
the doctrine is true from the one where it is false.
Not every general theory is suspect, nor is every explanation difficult
to test worthless. In philosophy, some disputes concern the very condi‐
tions under which we call something an explanation. The problem is
more precise: when a thesis claims to explain facts, it must show what
differences among facts would matter to it. Otherwise, its elegance is a
language’s power to redescribe, not necessarily the power to discover
something.
There is a special pleasure in unmasking. An institution speaks of val‐
ues, and an interpretation reveals interests. A person speaks of love,
and an analysis reveals the need for status. This move can uncover
real things. But if whatever is more cynical is automatically declared
deeper, we have replaced one sentimentalism with another: the senti‐
mentalism of our own lucidity. We also like the explanation for the im‐
age it offers of ourselves, the ones no longer fooled.
To test an unmasking, we can ask whether it explains variation. Why
does the same person sometimes forgo an advantage and sometimes
not? Why do two institutions with similar interests behave differ‐
ently? Why does a work endure after the disappearance of the audi‐
49


ence for whom it was supposedly only a signal? If the theory responds
only by repeating that everything is interest, it has gained vocabulary,
not necessarily explanatory power.
The Distinction That Changes the Problem
A less spectacular form of conceptual beauty is a well-made distinc‐
tion. Separating a belief’s origin from its justification can untie a knot:
learning a proposition from someone with a vested interest does not
decide whether the proposition is true. Separating correlation from
causation obliges us to seek the mechanism. Separating a work’s merit
from its creator’s merit makes possible a discussion that total judg‐
ment blocked.
But distinctions, too, can become refuges. If we say too quickly that art
and morality are completely separate, we may prevent noticing how a
work organizes admiration for certain actions. If we say origin and
justification are independent, we may miss how some origins affect
information’s reliability. A good distinction does not build two sealed
boxes; it specifies their relationship.
Here conceptual elegance resembles a joint more than a sword. The
sword separates. The joint allows controlled movement between
parts. A concept becomes beautiful when it makes possible an opera‐
tion we previously did not know how to perform: comparing without
confusing, generalizing without erasing, criticizing without abolishing
the object.
A concrete exercise is to reformulate the same problem in two lan‐
guages. A city can be a transport network, a space of memory, or a dis‐
tribution of access. Each representation makes something visible and
hides something else. The elegance of one does not demonstrate its
sufficiency. The mature question is not only “which model is most
beautiful?” but “for what operation, and with what loss?”
The Beauty of a Well-Posed Question
Sometimes the greatest intellectual economy occurs before the an‐
swer. The question “is beauty real?” seems to demand yes or no. The
question “what would have to exist independently of the observer for
50


beauty to be real?” reveals several problems. We have not needlessly
complicated the discussion. We have found where the assumptions
making the answer too easy were located.
A beautiful question organizes ignorance. It delimits what we have,
what we lack, and what observation would matter. It can create a
space for inquiry without offering the illusion of a solution. But even
here beauty is no excuse for infinite postponement. We can become so
enamored of the question’s subtlety that we refuse local, imperfect,
useful answers.
Conceptual elegance thus has two opposite failures. One compresses
until reality disappears. The other distinguishes until no conclusion
can be formulated. Between them lies difficult work: preserving as
much as necessary to think, and eliminating as much as possible
without secretly changing the problem. This is a beauty concerning
not only the mind’s brilliance but also the discipline with which it
checks its own pleasures.

CHAPTER 12
Technical Elegance: Where Has
Complexity Gone?
The button is simple. The system behind it is not. Who pays the differ‐
ence?
A well-made tool sometimes seems to know what you are about to do.
The handle falls where it should, resistance is appropriate, feedback
arrives in time. In software, a well-designed operation can replace a
confusing succession of steps. The admiration is real: someone under‐
stood an activity well enough to give it a form. But the simplicity en‐
countered by the user does not yet tell us whether the system became
simpler or the difficulty moved elsewhere.
Imagine two interfaces for deleting documents. The first offers a
single gesture, without confirmation or undo. The second adds a step
and preserves a recovery period. In the commercial demonstration,
the first may seem more elegant. On the day of a mistake, the second
may reveal a deeper understanding of the task. The criterion is not
the minimum number of gestures but the relationship between ges‐
tures, consequences, and the possibility of error.
In technology, form can truly aid use. A clear interface can make a
choice visible; a well-constructed graph can show a relationship diffi‐
cult to notice in a table. But the same formal care can direct attention
in the provider’s favor rather than the user’s. Pleasing design has no
automatic morality.
Consider an imaginary subscription screen. The acceptance option is
clear, pleasant, and immediate; refusal is hidden in difficult text. Here
beauty does not mean mere ornament. It distributes visibility and the
cost of action. To evaluate the design we must ask how easily users
can achieve their own purpose, not merely how easily they fulfill the
system’s commercial purpose.
A simple ethical test would compare paths: enrollment and withdraw‐
al, acceptance and refusal, purchase and correction of an error. If one
side is fluent and the other deliberately difficult, we have reason to in‐
52


vestigate. This is a normative analytical criterion, not an automatic
verdict on every interface difference; some friction can protect the
user, for example before an irreversible action.
Technical beauty can also conceal the work needed for trust. A report
with coherent graphs can seem solid before we check the data’s
provenance. An impeccable software demonstration can present only
favorable cases. The healthy question is what does not fit into the
presentation: errors, uncertainties, failure conditions, excluded users.
A good form should help this examination too, not merely admiration.
Here beauty can receive a more ambitious role than persuasion. It can
make truth examinable. An elegant, honest argument shows where it
depends on an assumption. A good graph makes uncertainty visible. A
well-designed system does not conceal its limits to seem complete.
These are ideals of practice, but ideals translatable into concrete
checks.
Perhaps the most valuable elegance is not the apparent absence of dif‐
ficulties. It is the economy with which an object allows us to see and
work with them. Not a beauty that spares us judgment, but one that
makes it possible.
A Practical Demonstration: Searching a List
You have an ordered list of 1.024 names and seek one. You can start at
the first and continue. Or you can compare with the middle name, re‐
tain only the relevant half, and repeat. The logic of halving drastically
reduces comparisons compared with visiting every position; for locat‐
ing among positions, ten halvings reduce 1.024 possibilities to one,
while the actual verification depends on implementation. The eleg‐
ance comes from using an existing property: order.
But order is not free. If the list changes frequently, someone must
maintain it; if we cannot compare names consistently, the assumption
breaks down; if accessing the middle is costly, the calculation of ad‐
vantage changes. The algorithm is no less elegant for having condi‐
tions. It becomes more elegant when those conditions are visible and
suited to the actual task.
53


This small example shows the difference between the beauty of an
idea and the beauty of a system. The idea can be impeccable. The sys‐
tem must connect the idea to inputs, errors, resources, people, and
change. A solution shining on clean data can become opaque when
the world delivers incomplete data. Not all exceptions are flaws in the
world; some show that the initial model was too impoverished.
Minimalism and Invisible Reserves
A bridge does not become more elegant merely because we eliminate
every material reserve. A reserve can look excessive on the drawing
and be the answer to uncertain loads. Likewise, redundancy in a com‐
puter system can seem uneconomical and make continuity possible.
Functional beauty must be judged in relation to the environment and
the types of failure considered, not only the solution’s silhouette.
In a hypothetical example, a software architect removes a checking
module and obtains a much cleaner diagram. The usual tests continue
to pass. Yet that module was the only place rejecting rare but danger‐
ous combinations. Simplification did not reduce the problem; it elim‐
inated a response to it. Here we have elegance of representation pur‐
chased through an operational loss.
The reverse situation also exists: an architecture had accumulated du‐
plicate checks, historical exceptions, and steps no longer serving any‐
thing. Removing them makes the system not only more beautiful but
easier to verify. We cannot decide between cases through a general
preference for less or more. We must understand each part’s role and
what the whole must withstand.
Beauty That Remains Repairable
A sealed object can seem perfect because it hides its joints. A repair‐
able object can show how it is put together. Which is more elegant?
For a display-case viewer, the first. For someone who will live with it
for ten years, perhaps the second. Technical beauty has duration: it in‐
cludes how a form accepts wear, intervention, learning, and a change
of ownership.
54


Here an elegance of responsibility for structure emerges, without
turning every aesthetic preference into a sermon. You admire not only
that the system works, but that you can understand why and how to
bring it back to life. An intelligible joint, an exactly explained error, a
replaceable component can have the beauty of an ongoing dialogue
rather than a surface forbidding questions.
In software, a good abstraction hides what need not be tracked at
every moment but leaves access to what becomes relevant when
something breaks. A bad abstraction promises the absence of diffi‐
culty and then returns it to the user as an inexplicable failure. The dif‐
ference is not always visible at first use. Their elegance must also be
evaluated under stress, not just in the demonstration’s prepared
scene.
We might call elegant a system that distributes its complexity where
the capacity to manage it exists and does not lie about its limits. This is
not a universal definition but a design criterion. It can conflict with
visual purity or immediate speed. The conflict is precisely what makes
the discussion interesting: a single form cannot automatically maxim‐
ize every value we demand of a technology.
Technical beauty is not merely the finished object’s seduction. It is also
the pleasure of understanding how a difficulty was solved, of feeling
your own gesture meet someone else’s intelligence. When it works
well, the tool does not oblige us to admire its maker constantly. It lets
us do what we came to do. There is a rare elegance in this capacity to
disappear at the right moment.

Music and Language: Beauty That
Exists in Time
The last note can change what you thought you heard in the first.
A melody cannot be encountered in its entirety at a single glance. It
develops through memory and expectation: what has passed remains
active, what has not yet arrived is anticipated. We can admire an isol‐
ated sound’s timbre, but a musical phrase’s beauty may depend on its
place in a progression. Reducing music to a sequence of pleasant
sounds therefore loses precisely the relationships that sometimes
make tension pleasant.
Imagine a phrase seeming to end but postponing resolution. Once, the
delay may produce surprise. Mechanically repeated, it can become a
trick. In another context, the same delay may express hesitation, de‐
sire, or the impossibility of closure. There is no simple relationship in
which more surprise produces more beauty. Surprise must enter a re‐
lationship with what we can hear as expectation, contrast, or return.
Nor are expectations identical for all listeners. McDermott and col‐
leagues’ study of indifference to dissonance among Tsimane’ parti‐
cipants, discussed earlier, shows why we should not treat one musical
tradition’s preferences as the human ear’s immediate verdict. The re‐
search concerns defined tasks and groups; it does not negate every
role of acoustics or biology. [15]
Why Return Is Not Mere Repetition
Listeners familiar with a piece do not necessarily lack surprise in
every relevant sense. They may know the next note yet not know ex‐
actly how it will sound in that performance, in that hall, in their
present state. Moreover, anticipating fulfillment can have its own
value. Someone rereading a beloved letter is not trying each time to
discover new content.
56


This is one reason beauty cannot be explained solely through reduced
uncertainty. Sometimes we want to relive a familiar form. At other
times, excessive familiarity closes our access to it. The difference must
be investigated, not eliminated by definition. A theory of beauty that
calls every repetition “hidden novelty” may save the words and lose
the phenomenon.
Musical interpretation adds another form of elegance: managing
shared time. Too short a pause can turn tension into haste; too long a
pause can break continuity. But there is not always an ideal duration
independent of the rest. The pause is what comes before and after it.
This dependence shows why local properties cannot always be evalu‐
ated separately, like items in a catalog.
A Sentence That Thinks Through How It Sounds
Language has a double aesthetic life. It can be admired for sound,
rhythm, and construction, but also for what it manages to say. An eleg‐
ant philosophical sentence can connect difficult ideas; a poetic sen‐
tence can produce an experience lost when we extract its message.
Sometimes how something is said participates in what is said.
Without invoking any experiment, compare two invented formula‐
tions: “I have not forgotten, but I no longer wait” and “The memory
has been preserved, but anticipation of the return has ceased.” The
second may be more conceptually explicit. The first organizes an emo‐
tional tension through parallelism and economy. The difference does
not prove poetry truer than analysis. It shows that the two forms can
do different things with the same core of meaning.
Yet verbal form can also lend credibility to content. In Matthew Mc‐
Glone and Jessica Tofighbakhsh’s experiments, rhyming aphorisms
were rated more accurate than versions with similar meanings but no
rhyme. A warning to separate poetic qualities from content reduced
the advantage. The result concerns that type of task; it does not prove
that every rhyme manipulates or that the reader is incapable of vigil‐
ance. [30]
A memorable sentence may survive precisely because it is beautiful.
This can benefit a truth difficult to transmit and advantage an error.
Eliminating style offers no solution: apparently neutral prose also has
57


a style, and lack of expressiveness does not guarantee accuracy. The
intellectual solution is being able to admire a formulation and restate
its claim without the charm supporting it.
Voice and the Other’s Presence
Voice is not merely a vehicle for text. Rhythm, timbre, breathing, and
pauses can make a presence perceptible. A perfectly controlled voice
may impress, but one allowing effort to be heard can feel closer. There
is no rule whereby a flaw produces authenticity. There is an interpret‐
ation of the relationship between form and the person we believe we
hear.
This belief can be mistaken. Spontaneity may be rehearsed and direc‐
ted. Hesitation may be technique. But knowing a voice is constructed
does not automatically make it worthless: artistic performance is of‐
ten precisely the art of an intentional appearance. The question is
what kind of relationship we thought was being offered and whether
that relationship was honestly presented.
Music and language thus oblige us to leave behind the model of
beauty as a stable object. Some forms exist through passing, through
attention holding them together, and through memory capable of
changing them. In a melody, return does not cancel the journey. In a
sentence, the last word can reorganize the entire beginning. Beauty
may be not what lies before me but how what has just disappeared
continues working within me.

Grace: The Intelligence of a
Gesture
We see an effortless movement. What we do not see may be a lifetime
of practice.
A dancer changes direction without seeming to negotiate with weight.
A craftsperson positions the tool and cuts exactly where the material
allows. A player sees a pass the others notice only after it has
happened. We call such actions graceful or elegant. We admire not
only the result but how body and situation have been connected.
Grace differs from brute efficiency. One action may succeed with
noise, effort, and force, another through a minimal adjustment. Yet
minimality is not sufficient: too small a gesture may express fear or
incapacity. What we appreciate is the proportion between difficulty
and means, plus the continuity through which movement seems to
arise from the situation.
A recording that removes context can therefore make performance
harder to understand. We see a short movement but not the missed al‐
ternative, the opponent’s speed, or the material’s constraint. The ac‐
tion’s elegance is often counterfactual: we feel it better when we know
how many things could have gone wrong.
What Does the Trained Body See?
Beatriz Calvo-Merino and colleagues’ study of action observation in
expert dancers compared responses to movements from different rep‐
ertoires, including ballet and capoeira. Motor experience was relevant
to the activity of networks involved in observing action. The study
was not simply measuring “how beautiful the dance is” and does not
demonstrate that a single neural mechanism explains grace. It shows
that observing a movement also depends on what the observer’s body
has learned to do. [31]
59


This distinction matters. An untrained spectator can have an intense
aesthetic experience, but an expert may perceive other relationships:
weight transfer, control of a stop, the risk of a transition. Knowledge
need not diminish pleasure; it can create a richer object for it. Some‐
times, however, the expert becomes so attentive to execution that they
lose the expression the beginner receives immediately.
Imagine a violinist hearing their own child play a piece clumsily for
the first time. Technically, they hear every flaw. Relationally, they may
encounter something extraordinary. They are not obliged to choose
between the two experiences. They answer different questions about
the same event.
Ease Can Be a Mask for Effort
There is a paradox of virtuosity: the better the control, the less visible
its labor may become. The spectator sees an apparently natural solu‐
tion and may underestimate the process making it possible. This does
not mean adding sweat to every performance to make it credible. It
means ease is sometimes an achievement, not the absence of diffi‐
culty.
But manifest effort also has beauty. Someone without a professional’s
grace can be admirable in how they persist, adjust, and succeed. We
need not pretend the performances are identical to appreciate them.
The virtuoso’s grace and the expressiveness of effort are different val‐
ues. An impoverished aesthetic vocabulary puts them in a single rank‐
ing; a richer one can see them separately.
In shared activity, elegance can belong to several people at once. A
conversation in which participants complement rather than cancel
one another has a temporal structure. So does a team coordinating
movements without constant commands. Form does not require a vis‐
ible leader. Sometimes the distribution of attention itself produces
what seems an intelligence of the whole.
A culture of the individual hero may miss this beauty. We see the final
gesture and forget the others’ preparation. Admiration for the virtu‐
oso becomes more accurate when it recognizes performance’s condi‐
60


tions. Not to diminish their merit, but to understand the complete ob‐
ject: a pass can also be beautiful because someone made space before
the ball began moving.
The Elegance of a Bad Action
Can a cruel blow be elegant? At the level of precision and economy of
movement, the answer may be yes. But this claim does not exhaust
the event. If we look only at control, we eliminate intention and what
happens to the person struck. If we say evil can have no beauty, we
risk falsifying the experience that sometimes makes it seductive.
The problem is not solved by mechanically adding two grades: ten for
technique, zero for morality. Knowing the purpose can change how
we see the movement itself. Precision can become frightening, and
ease can appear as cruelty. Yet the change in evaluation need not be
complete. We can remain caught between admiration of the capacity
and revulsion at its use.
This ambivalence explains why grace is not merely a reassuring cat‐
egory. It shows a capacity in concentrated form. That capacity can
serve play, art, work, or domination. Understanding its beauty does
not mean automatically assigning it a good destination. It means re‐
cognizing that some of our strongest reasons for admiration concern
not what is done but how masterfully the doer seems to command the
means.
Ultimately, grace poses a question about freedom: is the beautiful ges‐
ture the one that seems to encounter no resistance or the one showing
us how resistance has been understood? The first can make us dream
of a weightless world. The second shows a way of inhabiting the
world’s weight. Between them lies much of the beauty of human ac‐
tion.

CHAPTER 15
Nature: Beauty That Owes Us
Nothing
The same hunt can be magnificent choreography for the spectator and
the end of the world for the pursued animal.
Nature challenges the reflex to seek intention in what we admire. A
sunset was not organized for us. An animal does not wear colors to il‐
lustrate a human theory of harmony. We can find extraordinary forms
without anyone having intended our experience. Natural beauty thus
shows that an encounter’s value does not always require an author.
But the absence of an author does not oblige us to forgo explanation.
Physical and biological processes can explain the formation of struc‐
tures. Explaining the origin, however, does not completely determine
the aesthetic experience. Knowing a rainbow’s mechanism may ampli‐
fy admiration or leave it almost unchanged. Not every form of know‐
ledge must produce the same emotional transformation.
Nature adds another difficulty. The landscape appreciated by the
viewer does not necessarily coincide with everything ecologically im‐
portant. In a hypothetical example, a wetland may seem disorderly to
a visitor and perform functions they do not see. A very orderly space
can be pleasing and biologically impoverished. To decide what to pro‐
tect, we must also use information about processes and relationships,
not just visual reaction.
This does not make nature’s beauty irrelevant. It can provide a real
reason for attachment. But a reason is not a complete inventory of
values. If we protect only what resembles a postcard, we risk requir‐
ing the living world to be photogenic to deserve existence. The prob‐
lem is structurally similar to judging people by their faces: a legitim‐
ate reaction receives too much authority.
There is also a difference between viewing nature as an object and re‐
cognizing that we belong to its relationships. In the first case, what is
offered to the eye matters most. In the second, dependencies, limits,
and changes enter the experience. A garden can be beautiful not only
62


through one day’s appearance but through what we learn to observe
across seasons. This is a proposal about educating attention, not a for‐
mula for compulsory taste.
There are two different beauties here. One is the beauty of a configur‐
ation grasped quickly: outline, color, rhythm. Another emerges
through understanding a process: growth, transformation, interde‐
pendence. The first may be immediately available. The second some‐
times requires knowledge and can change what we previously called
disorder. Neither must eliminate the other.
The Sublime: When Form Exceeds Our Grasp
Edmund Burke distinguishes the sublime from the beautiful through
its connection with power, vastness, obscurity, and terror, experienced
under conditions where danger does not directly destroy us. Kant
frames the problem differently: in experiencing the sublime, imagina‐
tion’s limits enter a relationship with reason’s ideas, and evaluation
concerns more than a pleasing property of the object. The two theor‐
ies are not identical, although both separate the sublime from the
beauty of a reassuringly grasped form. [32, 3]
To feel the difference, imaginatively compare an intimate garden and
a storm observed from safety. The garden may invite closeness and
fine attention. The storm may impose distance, unsettle bodily scale,
and make vulnerability perceptible. We need not declare one more
beautiful. The experiences have different architectures.
Distance is decisive. From a safe room, waves can be magnificent; in
the water, those same waves can become nothing but a threat. This
does not prove the admiration at the window was false. It demon‐
strates that the observer’s position belongs to the experience’s condi‐
tions. An aesthetics of nature forgetting position can turn the privilege
of safety into a supposed property of the world.
The sublime can also take an intellectual form: dimensions, durations,
or complexities we cannot represent intuitively, although we can
think them through tools. It should not be confused with any large
number. What matters is the experience of a mismatch between our
63


capacity to grasp and the object toward which we direct ourselves.
Sometimes this mismatch humbles us. At other times, being able to
formulate it produces another kind of elevation.
Predation Does Not Become Good Because It Is
Photogenic
Admiration for a hunting animal may concern coordination, speed,
adaptation of movement. The prey’s suffering does not automatically
cancel these properties. But a description seeing only elegance has
already chosen a frame. Nature offers no single moral or aesthetic
perspective from which everything falls into place without remainder.
Imagine we could simultaneously perceive the experiences of predat‐
or, prey, and observer. Would beauty disappear or become impossible
to separate from horror? We do not know. The thought experiment
shows that the total image we invoke is often a more distant image,
not one actually including every experience. Seeing from above is not
the same as seeing from everywhere.
This distinction also matters in admiration for “nature’s balance.” An
ecological relationship can be stable at one level and appalling for the
participating individuals. Stability, complexity, and beauty are differ‐
ent concepts. We can understand a process without attributing bene‐
volent intention to it. We can love nature without pretending it loves
what it contains.
Beauty Without a Spectator
Can a beautiful landscape exist in a world without humans? The an‐
swer depends on what we mean by beauty, a problem we will revisit.
Forms and processes can exist without us. Their capacity to produce
certain experiences can be described counterfactually. The experience
itself does not occur without a being to have it. These three proposi‐
tions must not be merged.
More uncomfortable is whether nature would lose anything if no be‐
ing capable of admiring it remained. Descriptive naturalism can say
the processes would continue. An axiological answer may maintain
that a real dimension of value would disappear: the possibility of ex‐
64


periencing existence as admirable. We cannot settle the dispute
merely by counting mountains and stars. It concerns experience’s role
in what we call a valuable world.
Natural beauty is therefore not merely a refuge from culture. It can be
where we discover that meaning need not have been intended to be
real and that value does not necessarily require goodwill. What moves
us most deeply may be completely indifferent to our emotion. Perhaps
this is precisely why the encounter does not resemble receiving a re‐
ward. It resembles discovering a world under no obligation to reveal
itself to us this way.

Art, the Tragic, and the Pleasure
of Unpleasant Things
A painting can depict a wound without being an unsuccessful paint‐
ing. A novel can produce unease without failing to provide the pleas‐
ure of reading. If we require art to be only agreeable, we risk forbid‐
ding it precisely what we cannot easily look at in life. But shock is no
automatic certificate of value either. A work may disturb because it
discovers something, or because it offers nothing beyond the gesture.
At least three evaluations must be separated: what the represented
thing is like, how the representation is made, and what the work does
to the spectator. The subject can be repellent, the form admirable, and
the moral effect ambiguous. This separation continues a problem
already present in Aristotle: we can find pleasure in representing
things we would not wish to encounter directly. [5]
In his lectures on aesthetics, Hegel gave art a privileged position over
natural beauty because he saw it as a manifestation of spirit. For him,
art was not merely ornament or relaxation, but a way of presenting
important contents of human life in sensible form. This is a philosoph‐
ical thesis about art’s rank and function, not an experimental finding
obliging every observer to prefer a painting to a landscape. [33]
We can retain a fruitful question from this shift in emphasis without
accepting the entire system: a work’s value can depend on what it
makes us understand, not merely how pleasing its surface is. In an
imaginary story, a character lacking charm can make ignored suffer‐
ing visible. In another, a seductive character can conceal violence
through style. Neither a character’s beauty nor ugliness alone determ‐
ines the work’s artistic value or moral truth.
The problem of aestheticizing suffering thus arises. A photograph of
poverty can draw attention to injustice, but can also turn the photo‐
graphed person into material for the spectator’s emotion. The differ‐
ence cannot be established solely from the composition’s beauty. Con‐
66


text, consent, use of the image, what we learn, and what is hidden
matter. These are criteria for analyzing a case, not a verdict that every
beautiful image of suffering is exploitation.
An uncomfortable question for the viewer would be: after my aesthet‐
ic experience, does the represented person exist for me more or less
as a person? Have I understood something about their life or con‐
sumed an emotion without consequences? Art is not obliged to pro‐
duce immediate action. Yet this lack of obligation does not make criti‐
cism of how it uses people and experiences impossible.
But explanation through learning does not cover every case. We can
return to a tragedy whose meaning we know without the experience
consisting in obtaining new information. We may seek intensity, the
form of an emotion, or the possibility of moving through it within a
delimited framework. We need not claim that every represented suf‐
fering educates us. Sometimes it intensifies us; sometimes it numbs
us; sometimes it achieves nothing.
The Distancing–Embracing model proposed by Winfried Menninghaus
and colleagues explains pleasure in negative emotions in art by com‐
bining psychological distance with processes making emotions aes‐
thetically valuable: composition, emotional mixture, formal elabora‐
tion, and the search for meaning. It is an integrative theoretical mod‐
el, with limits and avenues for testing, not proof that every negative
emotion becomes beneficial if we call it art. [34]
Dark Beauty and the Mistake of Sweetening
Everything
Some works would lose their force if made agreeable. A formal rup‐
ture may be necessary to what they express. An ending without con‐
solation can refuse a false resolution. Calling these works valuable
does not necessarily mean calling them beautiful. We can preserve
“beautiful” for certain qualities and acknowledge that art pursues oth‐
ers too.
Yet a dark work can have beauty in one sense: the precision with
which it finds a form for an experience difficult to express. We admire
not someone’s suffering, but the achievement of making intelligible
67


something that would otherwise remain mute. This achievement can
produce satisfaction coexisting with the represented pain without
canceling it.
The grotesque complicates the picture further. As used here, it mixes
registers we usually separate: comic and terrible, human and strange,
bodily excess and deformation. We can be attracted and repelled sim‐
ultaneously. If we immediately call everything “unconventional
beauty,” we risk losing precisely the experience’s instability. Some
forms are interesting because they resist absorption into a single
praise.
Likewise, humor can have elegance without being beautiful in the
contemplative sense. A good joke can suddenly change a situation’s
frame with very few means. But a cruel joke can seduce us through
the same economy. The intelligence of form and the value of the rela‐
tionship created with its target are aspects meeting, sometimes in con‐
flict, within the same experience.
Kitsch, Excess, and the Shame of Pleasure
As a working definition, let us call kitsch a form offering emotion in
predictable packaging and confirming it too quickly. The problem is
not that the emotion is popular or accessible. The problem is that the
form can demand approval before constructing the experience: the
image insistently tells us where to shed tears, what to admire, what
response proves sensitivity.
But the accusation of kitsch can itself become automatic. A sentiment‐
al object can have value in a life without being a complex work. Des‐
pising every easily accessible emotion does not automatically produce
depth. Sometimes someone feeling too refined for a simple song is
mainly defending their self-image. Criticism of aestheticized emotion
must also accept criticism of aestheticized austerity.
Excess can also be treated as conscious play: artificiality, theatricality,
the pleasure of a form displaying its convention rather than conceal‐
ing it. This is not the same as being deceived by appearance. We can
deliberately admire a mask precisely because it is a mask. This experi‐
ence will return in the discussion of fashion: sincerity must not be
confused with the absence of stylization.

Art Is Not a Single Competition
Difficult art is not fraudulent by definition, and a spectator’s lack of
understanding does not prove inferiority. A work may require con‐
text, which can be evaluated; it may have ambitious intentions and
weak execution; we may dislike it and it may still deserve attention.
The label of art is not identical to a judgment of quality. Recognizing
that an object participates in an artistic practice does not oblige us to
admire it. Likewise, price indicates a transaction or market valuation,
not a direct measurement of aesthetic experience’s intensity. A buyer
may pay for rarity, provenance, positioning, or resale potential. We
should know what value we are discussing before comparing sums
and emotions.
Institutions can protect unpopular works and simultaneously their
own hierarchies. The solution is neither total submission to experts
nor elimination of expertise, but intelligible justification of choices
and the existence of alternative paths to recognition.
A good justification shows verifiable relationships in the work: formal
solutions, relations among parts, transformations of material or con‐
vention. A weak one invokes only its admirers’ prestige. History and
institutions matter, but should not close the discussion before the en‐
counter with the object.
The decorative does not deserve automatic contempt either. A textile,
a ceramic piece, or a melody making a day more bearable need not
claim to overturn civilization to have value. Art can provoke, console,
order, play, commemorate. The mistake is demanding that one func‐
tion replace them all, then declaring worthless whatever responds to
another need.
Art’s freedom from obligatory beauty does not require prohibiting
beauty. We can defend plurality of experiences without declaring the
agreeable guilty in principle.
We can now formulate a more precise difficulty. A work can be ex‐
traordinarily successful in a project we do not want. It can refine con‐
tempt, organize admiration for domination, make a mutilated vision
69


of people seductive. Asking whether it displays talent is not enough.
But neither do we gain anything by pretending talent disappears the
moment we dislike the purpose.
Extreme positions are comfortable. One says form is everything and
allows itself to ignore what form does. The other says the message de‐
cides everything and no longer needs to look at the work. Between
them, analysis becomes difficult: sometimes a moral flaw is also an
artistic flaw because it falsifies characters or demands an implausible
response; at other times, a work remains formally powerful precisely
in how it disturbs us.
Art does not necessarily offer reconciliation of all values. It may pre‐
serve conflict more accurately than a doctrine does. An important
work need not tell us what to believe at the end, but must do more
than leave everything vague. It can construct a situation where easy
answers become insufficient. Then form does not embellish the di‐
lemma. It is what prevents us from resolving it through inattention.

Time: Patina, Ruin, and Passing
Beauty
A mark can spoil an object. The same mark can be why we can no
longer replace it.
A new table and the same table after thirty years are not merely two
degrees of preservation. Marks can change the object we encounter.
Some are damage we would like to repair. Others make duration per‐
ceptible: where a hand always rested, the worn edge, a clumsy repair
grown familiar. Beauty can emerge through transformation, not only
through preserving the original form.
Not all age is beautiful. Mold, structural danger, or lack of care do not
become values because we photograph them well. But original perfec‐
tion is not the only possible criterion either. The object can gain a nar‐
rative dimension it lacked when impeccable. What we admire is not
the flaw itself, but the relationship between mark, material, and his‐
tory.
Aesthetics discussed in Japanese traditions, including sensibilities as‐
sociated with sabi or mono no aware, offer vocabulary for relating to
age and transience. They should not become a cliché that all imperfec‐
tions are beautiful or all Japanese culture has a single sensibility. They
show, rather, that disappearance and transformation can become ar‐
ticulated objects of attention. [12]
Why a Perfect Copy May Not Suffice
Suppose an inherited mug breaks. A technology produces a copy visu‐
ally and tactilely indistinguishable, down to every old crack. Have you
received the same thing back? For a strictly perceptual evaluation, the
difference may be zero. For a historical relationship, it remains: the
new object was not held by the same person. If this provenance mat‐
ters, value was not entirely contained in appearance.
71


The answer does not require venerating the original. Someone may
prefer the copy precisely because it allows using the form without
fearing loss. Another may consider the historical connection central.
The dispute is not solved by declaring one rational and the other senti‐
mental. We must specify what each seeks: perceptual experience, con‐
tinuity, testimony, use, or possession of a rarity.
Authenticity can also be exploited. A provenance story can add price
without adding what it claims. But the possibility of fraud confirms
that the stakes are real for the buyer; it does not prove every interest
in history absurd. Sometimes we care not only how the world looks,
but what actually happened in it.
The Ruin and the Viewer’s Position
A ruin can make time visible at a scale a functioning building con‐
ceals. Human form and the process of disintegration become simul‐
taneously present. We can see the intention of an order and the fact
that it could not be preserved forever. The experience may involve
melancholy, wonder, or a sense of disproportion between project and
duration.
But the ruin also has a politics of distance. To the visitor, the decaying
wall may be picturesque. To the resident, it may mean cold, danger,
and inability to repair. We need not deny beauty to see this difference.
We must recognize that the same configuration can belong to incom‐
patible projects of life.
A difficult question arises when restoration removes precisely what
made the place attractive. Should a ruin be preserved as a ruin, its ori‐
ginal form reconstructed, or the process allowed to continue? There is
no purely aesthetic solution independent of purpose, safety, testimony,
and use. Each choice produces another relationship with time. Keep‐
ing everything exactly as it is paradoxically means intervening to stop
the transformation we admire.

The Beauty of Fragility
A song sung only once, a flower that will close, a conversation that
cannot be identically reconstructed can possess an intensity connec‐
ted to their transience. But saying death or disappearance are neces‐
sary to every value would be excessive. A proof does not become
more beautiful if we forget it, and a friendship need not be shortened
to matter.
Two claims must be separated: possible loss can change attention, and
loss is good. The first is intelligible. The second requires much more.
We can value something fragile more intensely without wanting its
fragility. Saying illness made us see a person differently does not
mean the illness was a gift.
The same caution applies to aging. A body can lose certain qualities
and gain expressiveness, character, or another relationship with ges‐
ture. We are obliged neither to deny loss nor to say everything was
better before. A mature aesthetics of time can describe transforma‐
tions without demanding that every stage win the same competition.
When We Want to Fix What Moves Us
Photography, recording, and preservation try to retain. But the pre‐
served object may not reproduce how we were receptive to it. A mo‐
ment is not merely visual or sonic information; it is also what was
happening around us, what we knew, what we expected, and who we
were. It does not follow that archiving is useless. It follows that
memory is not mere playback of a file.
Temporal beauty obliges us to acknowledge that some values are tied
to unrepeatable events and others to repeatable forms. We can love
both. The problem begins when we demand that a preserved object
restore the entire situation or declare every continuity inferior to the
lost moment.
Perhaps time’s beauty does not consist in everything disappearing. It
consists in forms becoming something else without simply becoming
nothing. A mark can remain a wound, become a story, or be repaired.
None of these possibilities should be sanctified. They show that a
form’s value is not always finished on the day the form appears.

CHAPTER 18
Everyday Beauty: Dwelling,
Touching, Tasting
In an imaginary scenario, a building wins an award for appearance.
The photographs are excellent. Inside, someone cannot find the ac‐
cessible entrance, conversations are drowned by echoes, and the light
causes discomfort. Is there a contradiction? Not necessarily between
photographs and experience, since they show different things. The
contradiction arises if appreciation of a surface is presented as a ver‐
dict on the entire space’s quality.
The aesthetics of everyday life, developed by Yuriko Saito among oth‐
ers, extends attention beyond the isolated art object. Arrangement,
cleanliness, wear, care, smells, and ordinary experiences can have
aesthetic dimensions and ethical implications. This perspective lets us
ask not only how a place looks, but what relationship it enables
between bodies, objects, and practices. [35]
Functional beauty is neither an excuse for ugliness nor proof that
every useful object is beautiful. It describes the possibility of pleasure
arising from fit: a door opening naturally, an instrument whose form
makes the gesture intelligible, a room where important things are
within reach. To judge this fit, we must know who uses the object. The
imaginary hand of a “standard” user can exclude real hands.
Accessibility is therefore not merely a check added after aesthetics is
finished. It can change the very concept of success. A ramp integrated
into a shared route says something different from an entrance hidden
at the back. It does not merely transport a body but organizes who ap‐
pears as a natural participant. Attentive aesthetic analysis can see
such relationships without automatically confusing every functional
solution with complete moral virtue.
Everyday beauty also has a politics of maintenance. A space splendid
on opening day may subsequently depend on little-seen labor. Who
cleans, repairs, tends the plants, preserves quiet? If we admire only
74


the final object, we can miss the conditions making it possible. Recog‐
nizing labor does not destroy the experience; it can extend what we
are capable of admiring.
A room can be impeccable in a photograph and tiring after an hour. A
surface can look warm and feel unpleasant. An object can have a suc‐
cessful silhouette and make an irritating sound with each use. When
aesthetics reduces itself to image, it loses the body inhabiting the
space. Everyday beauty is often multisensory and distributed through
time.
To notice it, imagine a kitchen at the start of a day. Light does not
strike the eyes directly, the mug has the right weight, the drawer
closes without aggressive noise, the smell of coffee mingles with cold
air from the window. No object needs to be spectacular. The achieve‐
ment belongs to the composition of a situation in which activity can
begin well.
The Beauty of a Fit
Such an experience does not reduce to comfort. Some comforts do not
attract aesthetic attention, and some beautiful forms require effort.
What can become admirable is the fit among elements: material, light,
gesture, rhythm, bodily scale. The space seems to let life unfold
without constantly pushing it into a representation.
In the aesthetics of everyday life discussed by Yuriko Saito and in the
literature synthesized by the cited article, attention extends beyond
the isolated artistic object toward environments, activities, and prac‐
tices of care. This extension does not oblige us to declare every banal
object a masterpiece. It changes the unit of analysis: from the admired
object to the lived situation. [35]
A shared meal is an example. Food’s taste matters, but so do sequence,
temperature, texture, others’ proximity, and how we can talk. A dish
can be extraordinary and the dinner a failure. Conversely, simple food
can participate in an aesthetically successful situation. Not because
material details are unimportant, but because the whole’s value is not
the sum of isolated scores.
75


In attentive tasting, complexity can be appreciated through contrasts,
persistence, and transformation. In hunger or haste, the same object
may be encountered differently. There is no obligation to experience
every meal as an aesthetic examination. Turning sensitivity into per‐
manent work of discrimination can destroy precisely our openness to
pleasure.
Smell, Touch, and the Limits of Control
Smell is difficult to separate from atmosphere and memory. A per‐
fume can be pleasant on a sample and overwhelming in an enclosed
space. Touch can contradict image: a material looking luxurious can
feel cold, artificial, or unsuited to the gesture. These ordinary ex‐
amples show why “beautiful for whom, and in what situation?” is not
merely an abstract philosophical question.
There is also a dimension of sensory consent. We can sometimes avoid
an image by turning our heads; a loud sound or persistent smell can
occupy shared space. Beauty for one user can be intrusion for another.
We need not resolve every conflict by neutralizing the environment,
but must see that the aesthetics of shared space is not identical to that
of private choice.
This problem makes the ideal of a perfect atmosphere for everyone
debatable. An overly controlled space can become impersonal. A
highly expressive one can become tiring or exclusive. Sometimes the
most successful design offers the possibility of change: quiet and lively
places, variable light, movable objects. Beauty can belong to a form’s
availability, not only its final state.
The City Is Nobody’s Scenery
Suppose a neighborhood receives trees, benches, and pedestrian
spaces. Residents gain a more pleasant environment. In one version of
the scenario, housing costs remain affordable. In another, property
values rise and some tenants must leave. The visual intervention is
the same, but the outcome’s distribution differs. We have not demon‐
strated that every beautification causes displacement. We have shown
why evaluating a project must include who can stay to enjoy it.
76


The question is not “should we leave places unpleasant so they re‐
main cheap?” That would accept a false choice. The question is how
investment in environmental quality connects with the rules of ac‐
cess. Public beauty can be a form of shared dignity, not a prize re‐
served for those who can afford to approach it.
A beautiful street can be a common good: everyone sees it, and one
person’s pleasure does not consume another’s. But access can become
scarce through housing prices or rules of use. The same project can
produce collective value and opportunities for private capture. When
discussing who gains from beauty, we must follow this mechanism
rather than assume beauty itself is exclusion’s sole cause.
The alternative of ugliness is not neutral either. People with few re‐
sources can lose not only money but access to environments support‐
ing attention, pleasure, and belonging. Saying beauty is a dispensable
luxury can become a justification for distributing it only to those who
can buy it. On the other hand, demanding a single standard of public
beauty can eliminate the forms through which residents recognize
their own place.
Everyday beauty has a specific difficulty: its success can become invis‐
ible through habit. We notice noise when it begins, not maintained
quiet. We notice dirt when it appears, not the work previously pre‐
venting it. An aesthetics interested only in spectacular events can miss
the conditions making life bearable and sometimes pleasant.
It does not follow that care must be turned into a heroic spectacle.
Some things are beautiful precisely because they demand no ap‐
plause. A well-lit staircase, an easily opening door, a corner where we
can sit without buying anything can possess a discretion more import‐
ant than an image’s effect. Beauty is not always what makes us stop.
Sometimes it is what lets us continue.

Can Power Be Beautiful?
The most unsettling spectacle is not the one in which we fail to recog‐
nize evil. It is the one in which we recognize it and, for a moment,
continue admiring the form.
Let us begin with the difficult answer: yes, power can have beauty.
Not every power, not in every sense, not for every viewer. But the ca‐
pacity to organize forces, achieve what seemed impossible, impose
rhythm, or concentrate action can produce real aesthetic admiration.
Declaring in advance that every unjust power is also aesthetically
worthless comforts us but leaves its seduction unexplained.
We must first distinguish power to do from power over someone. A
strong body can lift a weight; a community can build something to‐
gether; a person can control others’ freedom. These are not the same
thing. Yet spectacle can mix them: the capacity for coordination is
presented as proof that authority deserves obedience. What impresses
in one register lends legitimacy to another.
Capacity Made Visible
Imagine a bridge being erected. Distributed tasks, coordinated move‐
ments, materials finding their places, a structure beginning to support
what previously could not pass. Power’s beauty here is the beauty of a
realized possibility. Grandeur does not require domination. Collective
capacity can have form and be admired without being personified in
a single leader.
But another scene uses the same visible ingredients: coordinated bod‐
ies, shared time, scale, precision. The difference is that every move‐
ment confirms a single will, and deviation has been eliminated. From
outside, unity may seem perfect. From inside, it may be experienced
as the impossibility of saying no. A complete evaluation must consider
both perspectives; the aesthetic effect does not disappear merely be‐
cause we learn its price.
78


There is a possible pleasure in efficacy: seeing something happen, in‐
decision replaced by action, the world seeming to respond to an inten‐
tion. This pleasure can be legitimate in a lifesaving intervention and
dangerous when it becomes indifference to what is being achieved.
The problem is not attraction to capacity, but the unnoticed passage
from “can” to “has the right.”
The Seduction of Scale
Power can also produce an aesthetic effect through disproportion. A
building, a crowd, a machine, or a ritual can exceed the individual
body’s scale. The experience sometimes approaches the sublime: not
merely a form’s agreeableness, but wonder before a capacity seeming
to surpass us. In Burke’s terms, power and terror can participate in
the sublime; this must not be confused with the reassuring beauty of
proportion. [32]
The viewer can respond with fear but also identification. “I am small,
but I belong to this whole.” In this hypothetical construction, lost
autonomy is emotionally compensated by participation in grandeur.
We do not claim this is every crowd’s universal mechanism. We show
an intelligible path through which collective form can make surren‐
dering part of oneself attractive.
Yet grandeur without absorption also exists. An orchestra can have
unity without every instrument sounding the same note. A com‐
munity can produce a remarkable project while preserving differ‐
ences. The analogy does not demonstrate a political theory, but pre‐
vents us from identifying order with uniformity. Some forms are
beautiful precisely because they make audible a plurality that does
not fall apart.
Politics Turned into an Image
In his essay on the work of art in the age of mechanical reproduction,
Walter Benjamin connects the aestheticization of politics with the pos‐
sibility of offering the masses expression without a corresponding
transformation of power and property relations. His argument be‐
79


longs to a historical analysis of fascism and reproductive media; it
does not authorize identifying every ceremony or public spectacle
with fascism. [36]
The stakes can be framed through a question of our own: do you parti‐
cipate in decision-making or only in the image of participation? You
can appear within a magnificent whole without being able to change
its purpose. Spectacle offers the feeling of a historical presence, and
form can make invisible the difference between being represented
and having actual power.
Democracy is not outside aesthetics either. A debating chamber, civic
ceremony, or public campaign has forms organizing attention. The
problem is not whether collective life can exist without appearance,
but whether appearance allows access to conflict, challenge, and cor‐
rection. An image showing only unity can conceal what a free institu‐
tion must be able to reveal: disagreement.
This creates a difficulty an overly easy defense avoids. An open society
can look more disorderly than a perfectly directed spectacle. Proced‐
ures can be slow, voices discordant, compromise unglamorous. We
need not declare this disorder immediately beautiful to defend it.
Sometimes we choose a political form for other values, accepting a
local aesthetic cost.
Yet plurality can acquire its own beauty: not the image of a single will,
but the capacity of real differences to continue together. This beauty is
harder to photograph because it unfolds over time and includes cor‐
rections. It has no guarantee of instant impact. It may require a spec‐
tator who has learned to admire negotiating a relationship, not
merely executing a command.
The Charm of the Powerful Person
Power can also change interpersonal beauty. In a thought experiment,
the same voice is heard before and after we learn that the speaker can
decide a professional destiny. Its timbre has not changed, but the pres‐
ence acquires another weight. Attraction, fear, heightened attention,
or resentment may appear. This is not proof of a universal effect; it is
a way to separate sensory properties from relational significance.
80


Is attraction to power always self-interest’s lie? Not necessarily. Com‐
petence, determination, and the capacity to act can be admirable in
themselves. But prestige can blur real competence and the position
presupposing it. People may seem self-possessed because others ab‐
sorb their mistakes. The visible form of confidence is sometimes the
product of an invisible infrastructure.
Conversely, lack of power can make a person seem less elegant by for‐
cing them to hurry, ask, justify themselves, bear costs. Comparing ap‐
pearances alone can mean comparing people under radically differ‐
ent aesthetic conditions. Social beauty does not always belong only to
body or character; it may belong to the position from which someone
can afford to appear.
Power That Restrains Itself
There is also beauty in an unused capacity. A person could humiliate
and chooses not to; could dominate a conversation and leaves room;
could impose a solution and accepts a test. Admiration here concerns
the relationship between force and its control. But restraint can also
be a calculated spectacle meant to show how much power exists. Even
discretion is not immune to theatricality.
We must therefore distinguish real restraint, which changes others’
conditions, from a gesture merely decorating authority. The criterion
is not how modest the imaginary leader looks, but what can happen
without their permission. Power accepting limitation can lose some
purity of image and gain another elegance: not requiring the whole
world continually to confirm a single center.
The answer to this chapter’s question remains affirmative and uncom‐
fortable. Power can be beautiful through capacity, coordination,
grandeur, precision, and even restraint. These qualities do not consti‐
tute a single verdict on its purpose or legitimacy. Sometimes precisely
what is beautiful in a power makes it more dangerous, because it re‐
duces the desire to look at costs.
81


The hardest exercise is not saying bad power is ugly. It is being able to
say: I understand why it fascinates me and still do not grant what it
asks. But even this formula must not lull us. Admiration changes our
willingness to believe. The boundary between contemplating a form
and consenting to its world is not always a line we cross knowingly.

The Beauty of the Sacred and the
Temptation of Total Meaning
A light can change a room. Can it demonstrate anything about the
world’s ultimate nature?
In a ceremonial space, light, silence, height, and rhythm can produce
an experience exceeding mere pleasure for the participant. Objects
seem to point beyond themselves. For a believer, this opening may be
an encounter with the sacred. For another observer, it may be a
powerful aesthetic experience without the same metaphysical inter‐
pretation. We need not deny either person’s intensity to recognize the
difference.
Spiritual beauty is difficult to separate from what is believed present.
The same form can be an artistic object, ritual instrument, testimony,
or sign of a transcendent reality. The experience includes knowledge,
expectation, and participation. Reproducing light and sound is not
enough to guarantee the same relationship with the object.
Beauty That Does Not Reduce to Proportion
In Ennead I.6, Plotinus challenges reducing beauty to the symmetry of
parts: simple things, such as light, can also be beautiful. Sensible
beauty is connected to form and an intelligible order, while attention’s
ascent leads beyond mere bodily appearance. This is a metaphysics of
beauty, not an experimental theory of perception. [37]
The interest of this challenge is that it makes us ask what we do with
beauty lacking easily comparable parts. A color, a silence, a presence
can be appreciated without explicit proportion. Perhaps the relevant
relationship is not between two halves, but between an appearance
and how it is received. Or perhaps unity itself is what we admire. No
answer comes free, but the problem exceeds geometry.
83


Aquinas, discussed earlier, connects integrity, proportion, and clarity
to a theological context of manifestation. [6] In a spiritual reading,
beauty may seem not merely something added to the world but a way
reality reveals its depth. This reading has existential force for the per‐
son experiencing it. But the experience’s force and everyone’s obliga‐
tion to accept its interpretation are different things.
Experience and Inference
Suppose someone listens to music and feels existence has meaning.
What has happened? We can describe a real transformation of experi‐
ence: disorder seems bearable, loneliness changes, attention opens.
We can also formulate a thesis: the universe is grounded in a benevol‐
ent order. The first does not automatically demonstrate the second,
even if they are closely connected for that person.
Yet replying that everything is “just in the brain” would be too simple.
Every human experience has bodily conditions. This does not decide
whether the interpretation’s object exists or the experience has value.
The relevant argument must examine the passage from experience to
claim, not treat explaining a mechanism as canceling meaning.
There is also the problem of symmetry between interpretations. If the
world’s beauty is invoked as a clue to its ultimate goodness, what
weight do we give the suffering appearing in the same world? Choos‐
ing only experiences confirming an aesthetic theology is not enough.
A serious interpretation must explain why one would reveal the es‐
sence and the other be secondary, without deciding this merely
through preference.
Can a Perfect Work Justify Its Characters’
Suffering?
Imagine a being capable of creating worlds. It produces a world of ex‐
traordinary complexity and beauty, but its inhabitants suffer. Could
the creator answer that suffering is necessary to the composition?
From the external spectator’s perspective, the form may seem justi‐
fied. From the sufferer’s perspective, their life making the work more
intense may seem precisely the problem, not the solution.
84


The experiment neither disproves God’s existence nor summarizes
every theodicy. It isolates a question: does the whole’s beauty have the
power to justify every cost to the parts? If we answer yes, we accept
treating beings capable of experience as material for a composition. If
we answer no, we admit a limit to aesthetic justification even for the
greatest imaginable work.
The negative answer does not resolve every case either. In real life,
valuable projects require costs, risks, and sometimes accepted sacri‐
fices. The difference between voluntary participation in a shared form
and instrumentalizing beings cannot be ignored. A world’s beauty is
not merely the form viewed by its creator; it may also include how its
inhabitants can respond to their own existence.
The Sacred Can Refuse Spectacle
Not every spiritual experience seeks visual abundance. We can con‐
ceive a practice in which emptiness, silence, or simplicity serve to pre‐
vent attention from stopping at objects. This does not mean absence of
form. It means a form attempting to change the very desire for spec‐
tacle. An austere room can be as aesthetically constructed as a richly
decorated one.
But austerity can become prestige, and renunciation of ornament can
become ornament of renunciation. There is no reliable way out of ap‐
pearance through a particular style. We can turn even modesty into
an image of our superiority. This does not prove the practice useless,
but shows that no vocabulary of purity replaces examination of our
actual relationship with others and ourselves.
The sacred’s beauty can have value without constituting a metaphysic‐
al demonstration. It can offer a way to inhabit questions remaining
open. But its very intensity demands care in interpretation: what
makes us feel we have reached the whole may sometimes be an ex‐
traordinarily convincing form of our own perspective.
We need not choose between credulity and contempt. We can take the
experience seriously, investigate the tradition articulating it, and pre‐
serve the question of its truth. Perhaps one of the hardest spiritual el‐
egances is this: not reducing mystery to an effect, but not turning the
effect into proof of the mystery you prefer either.

Moral Beauty and Cruel
Intelligence
Some good gestures move us through how they are performed. Others
compel us to admire the person performing them.
One person helps without exposing the recipient’s humiliation. Anoth‐
er offers the same material benefit but builds a scene of their own
generosity around it. We can consider both actions useful and only
one beautiful in a particular sense. What we appreciate is not the
mere presence of good but the relationship’s form: tact, measure, tim‐
ing, and the space left to the other.
In this analysis, moral beauty is not a pleasing face onto which we
project virtue. It is appreciation of an action or disposition through
how it manifests a value. But the term must be used carefully. If we
call beautiful everything we approve morally, we explain nothing. We
need to show what aesthetic evaluation adds: a fit, expressive clarity,
lack of ostentation, or harmony between intention and gesture.
Why Kindness Can Be Clumsy
Imagine someone helping sincerely but without grace. They talk too
much, become confused, miss the right moment. It would be absurd to
diminish the good done merely because it lacks elegant form. Yet
clumsiness can affect the relationship: embarrass, frighten, make help
harder to receive. Form is neither everything nor nothing.
Conversely, a very elegant gesture can be morally impoverished. An
impeccably worded apology can avoid concrete responsibility. An em‐
pathetic speech can leave the situation it describes unchanged. Perfect
politeness can administer exclusion quietly. Here beauty of form can
make the lack harder to notice, because the interlocutor feels almost
impolite asking for more.
86


This comparison shows why aesthetics and ethics can be neither iden‐
tified nor completely separated. How we act can be part of what we
do. But an action is not exhausted by how it appears. Sometimes we
must ask what actually changed, not merely how well the intention
was expressed.
The Emotion of Elevation and Its Limits
In two experiments, Simone Schnall, Jean Roper, and Daniel Fessler
studied the emotion called elevation, elicited by observing altruistic
deeds, and subsequent helping behaviors. Participants in the relevant
conditions showed greater willingness to help in defined tasks. These
results do not demonstrate lasting moral transformation or show that
every uplifting story produces the same effect. [38]
The distinction matters: feeling moved by goodness is not identical to
becoming good. Emotion can motivate an action, support an ideal, or
offer satisfaction replacing action. To distinguish these possibilities,
we must track what happens after the experience and under what
conditions. Measuring how intensely someone reports being im‐
pressed is not enough.
We can even construct an uncomfortable case: someone constantly
consumes stories of courage and generosity but avoids situations re‐
quiring a modest cost. The aesthetic experience of virtue can become
a substitute moral life. We do not claim this is the rule; we show why
emotion and practice must be analyzed separately.
The Autonomy of Form: The Strongest Defense
A strong aestheticist position says we need to appreciate form without
constantly subjecting it to moral approval. Otherwise art would be‐
come illustration of already accepted values, and criticism would ask
only whether the message is correct. This defense rightly protects ex‐
ploration, ambivalence, and the possibility of a work making us en‐
counter perspectives we do not approve.
A strong ethicist position replies that some works build their effect by
asking us to admire cruelty, ignore an experience, or accept a carica‐
ture of a category of people. In such cases the moral problem can also
87


affect artistic success: the work demands a response its structure can
justify only through blindness. Invoking autonomy is not enough to
exempt this relationship from examination.
The contextual position does not decide beforehand that morality al‐
ways matters in the same way. It asks what the work demands, how it
constructs the response, and whether moral information changes the
experience’s relevant properties. An instrumental piece and a novel
explicitly organizing admiration for a character do not pose the same
problem. This differentiation is not convenience: it requires more
analysis than a general verdict.
Can Evil Be Truly Elegant?
A cruel intelligence can economically solve a problem it should never
have pursued. Admiration for ingenuity can be real. But the judg‐
ment’s whole object includes the purpose. “Elegant in one respect” is
not an excuse; it is a qualification. It lets us understand seduction
without pretending every admiration must become global approval.
Yet information about purpose can alter the experience of form.
When we discover that a calm gesture was meant to humiliate, calm
may cease to seem grace and become coldness. We have not simply
added a moral grade after aesthetic evaluation. We have seen the
same movement differently because what it expresses has changed
for us.
Sometimes this transformation is complete; sometimes not. We can
continue admiring a structure and detesting the world it serves. A
profound theory must allow this fracture too. It should force us
neither to deny what we feel nor to accept everything making us feel.
The Right Not to Be Admirable
There is a sentence an image-obsessed culture struggles to utter: you
do not have to be beautiful to deserve respect. Not “you are beautiful
in a way we have not yet discovered,” although that may be true. Not
“you will become beautiful if you try.” But the unconditional fact that
dignity does not depend on success in this register of evaluation.
88


This right cannot oblige anyone to admire, desire, or love. It does,
however, oblige moral judgment not to turn absent admiration into
justification for humiliation. It obliges institutions, as an ideal of fair‐
ness, to explain why they use aesthetic criteria and how these connect
to the decision’s legitimate purpose.
It may seem that we thereby weaken beauty. In fact, we remove a task
it cannot legitimately fulfill: deciding people’s total worth. It remains
capable of delighting, disturbing, directing attention, and opening
meanings. It no longer has to function as a moral identity document.
But turning this right into contempt for aspiration also carries a risk.
People may want to become more graceful, more generous, more cap‐
able of shaping their lives. We should not suspect every ideal of im‐
provement merely because it can become hierarchy. The question is
whether the ideal remains a possibility for growth or becomes the
condition for receiving basic consideration.
Moral beauty can be a rare achievement. Precisely for that reason, it
cannot be the minimum standard imposed on every tired, frightened,
or resource-poor person. We need institutions and relationships that
function even when people offer no uplifting scenes. A good world
should not constantly depend on people who are also beautiful in how
they are good.

Taste as a Cultural Passport
At a table, someone calls an object “vulgar.” The word describes more
than form. It can classify the owner too. An observation about colors
or materials becomes a map of people who know and do not know
how to live “properly.” Taste becomes a passport of belonging.
In Distinction, Pierre Bourdieu analyzed the connection between
taste, education, and social position. The concepts of cultural capital
and habitus help describe socially formed dispositions and competen‐
cies that can seem natural to their possessors. Tastes classify objects
but also participate in classifying people. This sociological analysis
does not require believing every admiration a conscious lie or every
work equally valuable. [39]
On the contrary, the mechanism can work precisely through sincerity.
Someone raised within a particular repertoire may effortlessly recog‐
nize a reference another never had the opportunity to encounter. If
this familiarity is interpreted as natural intelligence or moral refine‐
ment, a condition of formation becomes a certificate of the person.
Difference in experience is converted into human hierarchy.
Access to aesthetic education thus becomes important. But the institu‐
tion must choose: does it teach a competence or demand a password?
Competence can be explained, practiced, and challenged. The pass‐
word checks whether you already belong among the accepted.
In The Theory of the Leisure Class, Thorstein Veblen examined how
conspicuous consumption and pecuniary criteria can participate in
what a society calls beautiful. An object can communicate resources
and position, not merely sensory qualities. His analysis is historical
and polemical, not a demonstration that every expensive good lacks
aesthetic value. It does, however, make intelligible the possibility that
price and prestige influence admiration. [40]
Two identical objects, one presented anonymously and the other as a
famous creator’s rare piece, form a useful thought experiment. If eval‐
uation differs, we must ask what changed: perceptual pleasure, in‐
terest in history, desire for possession, or anticipated status? Not every
90


difference is illusory. Provenance can be a relevant property of the ob‐
ject. The error occurs when one value is declared, without argument,
proof of all the others.
This diagnosis becomes too convenient, however, if every cultivated
taste is treated as a ruling-class fraud. A competence can be real and
unequally distributed. Someone can hear subtle musical relationships
and use that competence to humiliate others. The first fact does not
disappear because of the second. Their coexistence is precisely what
makes the sociology of taste more interesting than simple unmasking.
Imagine two people describing the same painting. One uses many
famous names but can point to nothing in the work. The other lacks
established vocabulary but notices how light changes the relationship
between characters. The first possesses the password; the second pro‐
duces an interpretation. In real life, password and competence can
meet in the same person. We cannot identify them by accent or cloth‐
ing without repeating precisely the error we are trying to criticize.
The Beauty of a Shared World
An aesthetic culture is not merely a ladder of prestige. It is also an in‐
frastructure of attention. Several people knowing the same works,
gestures, or rhythms can make shared experience possible. An allu‐
sion works, a variation becomes visible, a celebration acquires con‐
tinuity. If we destroy every convention in freedom’s name, we may
also lose the instruments through which freedoms meet.
This is the strongest defense of a canon: not that it is infallible, but
that it preserves a conversation stable enough to be continued and
challenged. The strongest criticism observes that selection was made
by situated institutions, and a work’s absence from the canon does not
prove its lack of value. A canon can be memory and frontier simultan‐
eously.
An aesthetic tradition can select which bodies, voices, and experiences
deserve presentation. Feminist aesthetics has criticized, among other
things, how definitions of art and canons can marginalize women cre‐
ators, practices, and perspectives, and the treatment of the female
91


body primarily as an object of the gaze. These critiques shift the ques‐
tion from “is it beautiful?” to “who looks, who is looked at, and who
can speak about this experience?” [41]
To understand the stakes, we need not assign everyone a fixed aes‐
thetic identity. We need to observe the selection procedure. If an ima‐
ginary catalog always presents the same skin type, age, and build as
neutral while the rest appear as exceptions, its neutrality is construc‐
ted through repetition. Calling one body “universal” and another “spe‐
cific” can differently distribute the right to represent the human.
Colorism names discrimination related to skin shade; the concept is
useful here as a question about standards, not an invitation to classify
populations by beauty. A concrete investigation should compare treat‐
ments, images, and contexts, not invent a biological scale of collective
attractiveness. Neither skin nor origin justifies hierarchies of dignity.
When an aesthetic standard overlaps a social hierarchy, this overlap
must be explained rather than naturalized.
There is also the risk of superficial correction. An institution can dis‐
play diversity while preserving the same rule: only spectacular people
deserve visibility. Broadening the winners’ list is not always the same
as weakening the competition’s power. Broader representation can be
valuable; it does not replace asking whether access to respect should
be an aesthetic prize.
Criticism of elite taste can itself become contempt. An admirer of a dif‐
ficult work is not automatically an impostor; a lover of a popular ob‐
ject is not automatically manipulated. Sociology should explain pref‐
erences without declaring others’ pleasure false by default.
A similar difficulty appears in the opposition between ornament and
minimalism. Abundance can signal resources, but austerity can re‐
quire even greater resources: space, time, materials tolerating no
flaws, the possibility of removing useful objects from sight. It does not
follow that minimalism is hypocrisy. It follows that status signals are
not always glittering. Sometimes privilege looks like the absence of ef‐
fort.

Who Has the Right to Say “Vulgar”?
“Vulgar” can describe a formal problem: insistence, disproportion, an
effect pursued too forcefully. But it can also be a social judgment no
longer examining the object. To distinguish them, we can demand an
explanation also applicable to an object produced by a prestigious
person. If excess becomes boldness only when the maker has the right
name, the criterion is not as formal as it claims.
Conversely, “authentic” can become an elite’s password for admiring
others’ lives as long as they do not try to leave them. A modest setting
may be appreciated by the visitor as cultural expression, while the
owner’s desire to modernize it is treated as lost authenticity. Who then
owns the right over the form: the one inhabiting it or the one admir‐
ing it?
There is no universal answer canceling heritage or the freedom to
change. But the question reveals the conflict between shared cultural
value and individual projects. A world can be beautiful to its viewer
and limiting to the person required to preserve it unchanged.
Cultural taste matures when it can defend concrete achievements
without turning every preference into a certificate of belonging. This
does not mean abolishing demanding standards. It means someone
can learn a milieu’s language without being obliged to adopt all its
contempt. The best aesthetic passport is not one declaring you per‐
manently admitted to the class of those who see. It is one allowing you
to cross another border.

The Halo: When One Beauty

Imagine two evaluations of the same professional presentation. In the

ceive an image of someone they consider attractive. If the arguments
seem stronger in the second situation without having changed, they
have not discovered a new property of the arguments. They have
transferred an impression from one domain to another.
This transfer is what interests us in the halo effect: favorable evalu‐
ation on one dimension contaminates evaluations that should rest on
distinct information. In Megumi Hosoda, Eugene Stone-Romero, and
Gwen Coats’s meta-analysis of experimental studies on attractiveness
and professional outcomes, people considered attractive were advant‐
aged on average. The reported average effect was d = 0,37, a differ‐
ence expressed in standard-deviation units, not a 37% increase in
salary or hiring chances. Professional evaluators in those studies were
not immune to the influence. [1]
An average does not describe every decision. It does not follow that at‐
tractive people always win or an evaluator cannot judge well. The res‐
ult shows that eliminating this influence cannot be assumed merely
because people are educated, experienced, or convinced of their fair‐
ness. The belief “I look at competence” must be checked through pro‐
cedure and results.
An important asymmetry appears here. Beneficiaries may not see the
advantage because they receive an acceptable explanation: good pres‐
ence, confidence, fit with the team. Those disadvantaged may be un‐
able to prove the cause because they did not observe the alternative
decision. Each individual situation remains ambiguous, although ex‐
perimental comparison can identify an average effect. A case’s ambi‐
guity does not cancel the phenomenon; the phenomenon’s existence
does not authorize diagnosing every case without evidence.
94


Apparently neutral words therefore deserve precise questions. Does
“good presence” mean clear explanation, listening, punctuality, con‐
duct? Or does it mean the person resembles our image of who should
occupy that place? A criterion becomes more legitimate when un‐
packed into relevant, observable behaviors. It becomes more suspect
when it remains an unchallengeable global impression.
The Advantage That Can Come to Look Like Pure
Merit
Let us construct a hypothetical mechanism. A child receives more pos‐
itive attention because adults find them agreeable. They get more op‐
portunities to speak, are heard more patiently, and gain social experi‐
ence. Later, an evaluator notices ease of communication and treats it
as independently acquired merit. In this story, the final competence
can be real and the initial distribution of opportunities unequal. The
two do not exclude each other.
The story is not longitudinal proof. Langlois and colleagues’ meta-ana‐
lysis documents differences in judgment and treatment, but precisely
reconstructing a life’s course requires other data and methods. [24]
The hypothetical mechanism nevertheless helps identify what must
be measured: initial treatment, opportunities received, skills acquired,
and later explanations of success.
An institution can err in two directions. It can ignore initial advant‐
ages and call everything pure merit. Or it can reduce every achieve‐
ment of an attractive person to appearance, canceling real effort and
competence. The second error does not repair the first. Correct analys‐
is tries to separate contributions, not replace favorable prejudice with
hostile prejudice.
The Halo Does Not Stop at the Body
We can construct the analogy in the intellectual domain too. A speaker
formulates clearly, uses apt examples, and seems to command the ex‐
planation’s rhythm. This is real competence. But it can make the audi‐
ence attribute additional competence regarding evidence, neighbor‐
95


ing domains, or consequences they have not analyzed. The example is
hypothetical; we do not mechanically transfer an effect size concern‐
ing physical attractiveness to intellectual authority.
An elegant diagram can produce the same phenomenon. Well-ar‐
ranged boxes, clean arrows, symmetrical labels: the impression is that
the problem has been organized. Sometimes it has. At other times, the
hardest relationships have merely been drawn. An arrow between
“intention” and “result” is not proof that intention produces the result
under actual conditions. The diagram can be a thinking tool or a
graphical simulation of explanation.
This halo is particularly difficult to notice when the admired criterion
belongs to our identity. Someone unimpressed by bodily beauty can
be highly vulnerable to a theory’s beauty. Fashion’s critic may be se‐
duced by austerity. Someone proud of skepticism may give excessive
credit to a well-formulated unmasking.
Sociologically, beauty can function as an invitation to receive others’
time. Time is a resource: time for listening, explaining, tolerating er‐
ror. This mechanism is presented here as a hypothesis to analyze in
concrete contexts, not a law describing all relationships. It shows why
appearance’s effects can be subtler than explicit refusal.
How Much Should We Correct First Impressions?
Living without quick impressions would be impossible. They can
guide attention and capture real information. The problem is whether
we let them close the investigation. We can treat an impression as a
hypothesis: what did I notice and what did I add? Which attributions
have independent evidence? What would have to happen for me to re‐
vise it?
But correction is not simple either. A procedure completely removing
presence can lose information relevant to relational work. One pre‐
serving it can admit prejudice. There is no zero aesthetics automatic‐
ally purifying judgment. There are choices of representation and
methods of verification suited to the purpose.
96


A useful exercise is evaluating dimensions separately before con‐
structing the overall verdict. A presentation’s clarity, evidence quality,
and operational competence are not the same quantity. If we rate
them from the global impression, the halo can fill every box. If we ex‐
amine them through different tests, admiration has fewer places to
become certainty unnoticed.
Beauty is not noise to eliminate from every judgment. Sometimes it is
the very object evaluated. But praise in one register should not alone
complete someone’s biography or an idea’s validity. Otherwise, a
beautiful thing becomes a machine manufacturing merits nobody has
verified.

The Economics of Beauty: Value,
Rent, and Competition
What is a face worth? The question sounds cynical, but a market can
answer without explicitly asking it. The advantage may appear in an
interview invitation, an offer, a more visible role, or a chance to be
heard. To understand it, we must separate what research measures
from what we project onto the results.
Daniel Hamermesh and Jeff Biddle’s paper on beauty and the labor
market documented associations between appearance ratings and
earnings. It helped turn the “beauty premium” into a testable econom‐
ic question. Since the data are observational, a wage association can‐
not be read as the pure effect of an isolated trait: occupational selec‐
tion, social conditions, grooming, and other characteristics can con‐
tribute. There is no single percentage applicable to every country, pro‐
fession, and person. [42]
Markus Mobius and Tanya Rosenblat investigated the mechanisms in
an experimental labor market. Participants performed a maze-solving
task, and attractiveness did not improve actual competence at it. Yet
compensation was influenced. The authors identified channels related
to participants’ confidence, evaluators’ mistaken expectations about
their ability, and communication advantages in oral interaction. The
experiment shows an aesthetic premium can arise without superior‐
ity in measured productivity; it does not reproduce the entire labor
market. [43]
Producing more is not the same as being perceived that way. Nor are
skills interchangeable: communication can matter in negotiation
without improving maze-solving. If an employer claims to evaluate
technical competence but pays for its impression, the criterion has
shifted.
These results do not let us assign a universal percentage to every per‐
son or profession. They show appearance can enter economic mech‐
anisms through several paths. Some involve mistaken attributions,
98


others behaviors and interactions with real effects. Separating mech‐
anisms is more useful than declaring wholesale that the market re‐
wards either talent or superficiality.
An employer might say: “It is not my taste; customers buy more from
people with a certain appearance.” Even if demonstrated in a concrete
case, the ethical problem would not disappear. It would move. The in‐
stitution would transmit the customer’s preference and distribute its
costs onto employees. Deciding what is justified requires discussing
professional relevance, alternatives, and effects on the excluded.
Some activities explicitly include appearance in the product: portray‐
ing a particular character, presenting clothing, a visual project. Not
every criterion is permissible even here, but analysis cannot be
identical to that for a programmer or accountant position. The reason‐
able requirement is specific justification, not the idea that appearance
must be always relevant or never relevant.
Better evaluation could begin with work samples, criteria formulated
before the meeting, and comparisons of results with and without visu‐
al information where unnecessary. These are procedural proposals to
test, not automatic guarantees. Hiding a photograph may reduce one
channel while leaving others intact: name, voice, style, or school may
continue carrying social information. Fairness is not achieved with a
single button.
There is also a problem of defensive investment. In a hypothetical
situation, every candidate spends more on presentation merely be‐
cause the others do. If available positions remain unchanged, the
group may bear higher costs without obtaining more opportunities. A
service improving comfort or personal expression creates value; a ser‐
vice purchased solely to avoid losing in a compulsory competition also
creates an entry fee. Both functions can coexist in the same purchase.
Who gains? The person favored by the standard may receive access
and income. The aesthetic-services provider may receive demand. The
intermediary may sell the promise of approaching the ideal. Who
loses? The excluded competent candidate, the person paying conform‐
ity’s cost, and the organization confusing signal with ability. These are
positions within a mechanism, not fixed classes of people: the same
person may gain in one role and lose in another.

Beauty That Produces Something
A well-designed book can be easier to read. A pleasant space can make
an activity more desirable. An object can offer pleasure through use.
In such cases, aesthetic value is not merely an error in calculating util‐
ity; it can be part of what people seek. Economic criticism must distin‐
guish value created from price captured.
Imagine a designer improving an interface’s clarity and reducing er‐
rors. The gain is not merely an impression of professionalism. In an‐
other case, the designer preserves functionality but produces a form
users sincerely prefer. This can also be value. Not every pleasure must
demonstrate increased productivity to matter economically or exist‐
entially.
A third case differs: the product becomes a sign of belonging, and its
value to the buyer depends on others not having it. Here rarity and
comparison are central. Not every beautiful good is positional, but a
good can be beautiful and an instrument of distinction simultan‐
eously. Ignoring this combination risks either idealizing consumption
or denying the object’s real pleasure.
The Race in Which Nobody Arrives
A simple model of aesthetic competition can explain defensive invest‐
ments. If one person improves their presentation, they may gain an
advantage. If everyone does the same, the standard may shift and rel‐
ative advantage diminish. Resources have been spent although com‐
parative order changed little. This is a possible mechanism of a posi‐
tional race, not a complete description of all grooming practices.
The difference matters because investment can also produce absolute
pleasure. Someone can enjoy clothes, movement, or a more beautiful
space even if others do the same. We should not consider everything
waste merely because competition exists. The question is what part of
the benefit survives generalization and what part depends on others
remaining behind.
100


This question also applies to institutions. A university, company, or
city can invest in image to be noticed. Sometimes image communic‐
ates real quality. At other times, resources move from activity to its
representation. We need no general contempt for communication to
demand that the two be separated.
Who Owns Shared Beauty?
A successful public square can offer pleasure to many, but surround‐
ing property owners may capture part of the increased economic
value. This does not mean the development is bad. It means the distri‐
bution of gains does not automatically coincide with contributions.
Beauty can be collectively produced and selectively monetized.
On an imaginary platform, creators produce attractive forms, the pub‐
lic supplies attention, and the intermediary controls discovery and
transactions. Who gains depends on access, recommendation, and
payment rules, not merely works’ quality. An economy of beauty is
also an economy of visibility. What is not encountered can rarely be
chosen.
Yet visibility is not a simple good. Abundant beautiful objects can in‐
crease options and make attention scarcer. Creators compete not only
with mediocrity but with many other achievements. Economic success
cannot serve as a complete measure of aesthetic value, but neither
does economic failure automatically prove misunderstood superiority.
The economics of beauty ultimately requires four distinct questions:
what valuable experience is produced, what information is transmit‐
ted, what comparative advantage is obtained, and who can capture
the income. The same object may answer each differently. Only after
this separation is criticism possible that does not confuse pleasure
with naivety and profit with proof of value.

CHAPTER 25
Fashion, the Mask, and the Art of
Appearing
There is a difference between preparing for a meeting and feeling
constantly at an audition. In the first situation, presentation is one ac‐
tion among others. In the second, the body becomes a project that can
never be declared finished: every change can be evaluated, every pho‐
tograph demand correction, every life stage seem a deviation from the
standard.
At the end of the eighteenth century, Mary Wollstonecraft criticized an
education of women directed toward pleasing and becoming agree‐
able objects at the expense of reason and autonomy. The problem was
not beauty as experience, but reducing the person to producing oth‐
ers’ approval. Her historical argument remains relevant as a question:
what capacities do we cease cultivating when social value is too
tightly linked to attractiveness? [44]
Objectification theory, formulated by Barbara Fredrickson and Tomi-
Ann Roberts, proposes that internalizing an evaluative gaze on the
body can produce self-monitoring and psychological costs. It is a the‐
oretical framework organizing questions and findings, not proof that
everyone concerned with appearance has the same experience or
every act of beautification is submission. [45]
The practical question is not “do you wear makeup?” but “what free‐
dom do you have in relation to this practice?” One person may find
play, expression, and joy in it. Another may feel unable to appear oth‐
erwise. The same external action can have different meanings.
Judging autonomy requires examining real alternatives, penalties for
refusal, and the possibility of changing the choice without humili‐
ation.
Pressure may concern several genders without being distributed
identically. Standard, age, cost, profession, and consequences must be
examined. Neither universalization nor rigid opposition between
groups alone captures individual experiences.
102


Yet describing self-stylization solely as a response to pressure would
be incomplete. Someone may use clothing, makeup, posture, or ges‐
ture to explore possibilities of identity. There is not always a complete,
authentic, unstylized self hidden beneath all forms. Sometimes form
participates in what we become. Choosing a mask can also mean dis‐
covering a voice.
The problem is not the mere existence of appearance, but the relation‐
ship between play, constraint, and consequences. A transformation
chosen for pleasure differs in meaning from one undertaken under
threat of losing access. In actual life, motives mingle. We can sincerely
desire what our environment rewards without being either com‐
pletely free or completely manipulated.
Elegance and the Discretion of Effort
Elegance in dress can be relational: the fit between body, occasion,
material, and manner of wearing. An expensive object does not guar‐
antee this fit. Nor does rejecting luxury. A garment can seem elegant
because it does not require the body to play an impossible role; anoth‐
er may succeed precisely through deliberate theatricality.
What we call “natural” is sometimes an effect produced through much
work. An effortless appearance can require time, money, and compet‐
ence. This does not make it aesthetically false. In art, many effects are
constructed. It becomes problematic when apparent effortlessness
serves as proof of the person’s natural superiority or its cost is denied
to those trying to reproduce the form.
Conscious excess also has beauty. An outfit can refuse discretion, dis‐
play artifice, and turn appearance into a scene. The spectator is not
necessarily fooled; they can participate in play. This possibility pre‐
vents us from making sobriety the only legitimate form of aesthetic
maturity.
The Mirror’s Pleasure and the Permanent Tribunal
Madelaine de Valle and colleagues’ meta-analyses separate experi‐
mental and longitudinal evidence concerning social media and body
image. The distinction is essential: an experiment exposing people to
103


idealized images and an analysis of general platform use do not meas‐
ure the same thing. Effects on evaluations of one’s body should not
automatically be confused with clinical diagnoses or a single cause of
complex problems. [46]
An imaginary platform predominantly presenting bodies selected for
perfection does not offer a neutral sample. Treating the feed as nor‐
mality could make comparison unequal. The hypothesis requires ma‐
nipulating content and measuring response, not merely counting
hours online.
Businesses surrounding beauty can respond to real needs. Grooming,
clothing, and tools of expression can bring comfort and pleasure. But
a conflict of interests is also possible: a provider profiting from cor‐
recting inadequacy has few commercial reasons to declare the project
finished. This does not prove an entire industry’s conspiracy. It de‐
scribes an incentive worth tracking in messages, promises, and sales
models.
Asking individuals alone to bear resistance’s cost while institutions
apply the standard is convenient moralism. We can criticize the rule
and understand adaptation. Freedom is not measured by the compuls‐
ory heroism of the vulnerable.
Nor does “everyone is beautiful” automatically solve the problem. It
may broaden recognition and hurt less than insult. But if dignity re‐
mains conditional on inclusion in the category of beauty, the tribunal
has not been abolished. It has merely been asked to issue more favor‐
able verdicts. What happens to the person who does not feel beautiful
and does not want to spend a lifetime proving otherwise?
An ethical alternative is body neutrality modestly understood: not an
obligation to be indifferent to the body, but permission not to evaluate
it aesthetically all the time. The body can be cared for for comfort,
health, mobility, and the possibility of living without being converted
into an image at every moment. This is a normative position, not a
universal therapeutic promise.
The tension is not resolved by asking everyone to abandon vanity.
Some pleasures of appearance are real and need no excuse through
utility. Enjoying how you look does not automatically mean consider‐
104


ing others worth less. The problem arises when the mirror stops being
a place of play and becomes uninterrupted verification of the right to
appear.
Imagine someone choosing an unusual outfit for an evening. They
know not everyone will like it, and this freedom itself delights them.
In another scenario, the same outfit results from anxiously trying to
anticipate every gaze. The object is identical, but the practice differs.
We cannot infer autonomy’s degree from the photograph.
The discourse of acceptance is not immune to standards either. We
can end up demanding people be beautiful in how they no longer de‐
sire beauty: calm, confident, authentic, never jealous. We have moved
the competition from body to attitude. A less cruel culture should al‐
low ambivalence too, not only a perfect image of liberation from im‐
age.
Who Are We When We Construct Ourselves?
A mask can conceal, but also protect a fragile possibility until the per‐
son can inhabit it. A repeated role can become habit. An appearance
can produce encounters that would not otherwise happen. These
mechanisms do not make every transformation good. They show that
the authentic/artificial alternative is often too simple for social life.
The more fruitful question is whether form broadens or narrows our
possibilities. Can we change it? Can we appear without it? Does it let
us participate or require constant self-surveillance? What costs do we
bear, and what pleasures would we lose by giving it up? These ques‐
tions are not answered merely by a practice being traditional, mod‐
ern, popular, or expensive.
Fashion can be a language of difference and a discipline of conform‐
ity. It can produce creativity and waste, freedom and dependence. Re‐
ducing it to one face would make analysis cleaner and the world less
intelligible. The beauty of appearance is one way people negotiate the
right to be seen, but also the pleasure of not immediately being re‐
duced to what others already thought they were.

Algorithms and the Abundance of
Seductive Forms
Imagine a program assigning a face a score with two decimal places.
The typographic precision is impressive. But what has it measured? A
property of the person, an average of raters, or the program’s ability
to reproduce a set of labels? Without an answer, the number is more a
well-presented ambiguity than precise knowledge.
The MetaFBP paper, presented by Luojun Lin and colleagues as a pre‐
print in 2023, proposes a method for personalized prediction of facial-
beauty ratings. Personalization itself is instructive: the target is not
one observer-independent value, but shared regularities and differ‐
ences between preferences. Technical success in such a framework
means predicting ratings within the data and tasks used, not demon‐
strating a person’s objective value. [47]
If we train a model to reproduce a group’s opinions, we obtain at best
an approximation of that criterion. We can examine how well it gen‐
eralizes, where it errs, and how performance changes for underrep‐
resented groups. We cannot transform the target by renaming it. A
predictor of approval does not become a judge of dignity because the
interface calls it “objective analysis.”
Moreover, a system can predict prejudice very well. Accuracy against
labels and the labels’ legitimacy are different questions. If human
evaluators confuse attractiveness with competence, faithfully imitat‐
ing their verdicts does not correct the confusion. It can make it faster
and harder to contest, especially when the procedure is presented as
neutral because it has no emotions.
This does not mean every aesthetic model is useless or harmful. A tool
can help choose a composition, find examples, or explore personal
preferences. The important difference is between supporting someone
who can change their criterion and classifying them through a cri‐
terion they did not choose and cannot contest.
106


The score raises a broader problem: what has the system learned to
reproduce? Agreement among raters, an individual preference, click
probability, attention duration, or a judgment of artistic value are dif‐
ferent targets. They can correlate but are not synonyms. A model may
be very good at one and unsuitable for another.
A serious evaluation must therefore specify population, task, and con‐
text. If a system predicts ratings of standardized photographs, it does
not follow that it understands interpersonal presence. If it predicts
which image will quickly be chosen, it does not follow that it can anti‐
cipate which work will remain important after years. Technical preci‐
sion does not eliminate the problem of defining the question.
Who Shapes Taste While Predicting It?
A hypothetical recommendation mechanism works like this: the sys‐
tem observes what attracts attention, shows more of that content, and
creators adapt to what becomes visible. Future data also reflect the ef‐
fect of previous recommendations. If we interpret these data as pure
expression of spontaneous preferences, we confuse what people
would have chosen from a broad offering with what they choose from
an already selected one.
This is a possible feedback loop, not a claim that all platforms produce
the same effect or every user becomes identical. It requires a test:
what happens if we change exposure diversity, ranking criteria, or
users’ ability to control recommendations? Without comparison, the
story of homogenization can be as speculative as the story of perfect
freedom.
Personalization also holds real promise. A minority taste can find
forms and communities mass distribution would have ignored. In one
scenario, the algorithm narrows experience; in another, it opens it.
The difference depends on the objective and what we call success:
time spent, immediate satisfaction, discovering new things, the exper‐
ience’s retrospective value. These criteria are not interchangeable.
The loop becomes more interesting when users do not completely
know their own preferences. A system can discover something they
would not have sought alone. But it can also stabilize a version of
107


them too quickly. If every past reaction is treated as a promise about
the future, exploration becomes difficult. Taste is not merely a func‐
tion to estimate; it is also a capacity in transformation.
A design alternative would separate satisfying existing preferences
from exploring new possibilities. There is no universal optimal per‐
centage of surprise. Outcomes should be tested over time: diversity of
experiences, the possibility of rejecting the constructed profile, imme‐
diate pleasure, and retrospective value. This is a proposed evaluation
program, not a finding about every existing system.
The Same Image, Another Author
In experiments published by Lucas Bellaiche and colleagues in 2023,
AI-generated images were randomly labeled as made by a human or
by AI. The human label favored ratings such as liking, beauty, pro‐
fundity, and worth. The design studies the effect of attributing author‐
ship on the same categories of images; it does not demonstrate human
art’s visual superiority over generated art. [48]
Is this result merely prejudice? That depends on what judgment we
believe we are making. If the question strictly concerns visual proper‐
ties, the label may introduce a factor foreign to the target. If it con‐
cerns a work as someone’s act, provenance may be relevant. Some‐
times we admire not only form but what we think it expresses, the ef‐
fort, intention, or relationship with a life.
But provenance’s relevance does not justify every story. We may over‐
estimate labor, project absent intention, or reject a successful form be‐
cause we dislike its author’s category. The label does not automatically
determine value. It changes the object we believe we are evaluating,
and this change must be explicit.
What Becomes Scarce When Beauty Becomes
Abundant?
Automatic image generation adds a philosophical experiment. If a sys‐
tem could produce any requested agreeable surface at negligible cost,
would beauty become worthless? Not necessarily: an experience can
108


remain pleasurable even when not rare. Value might, however, move
toward choice, context, provenance, or the relationship with a creator.
This is scenario analysis, not a certain forecast of the art market.
Another scenario raises authorship. Two images look identical; one
results from a long human search, the other is generated instantly.
They can have equal perceptual value and different values as testi‐
monies of action. If we care only about what we see, the difference
may matter little. If we care about encountering a person through
what they made, it may matter greatly. Neither position should hide
within a single word: “beautiful.”
Let us push the experiment: suppose producing highly agreeable im‐
ages becomes almost costless for users. It does not follow that all
beauty has become free. Time, attention, trust, shared experiences,
and access to contexts where form matters remain limited. A thou‐
sand synthetic landscapes do not automatically replace walking in a
place or the memory of a day spent with someone.
Value may shift from the isolated object toward selection, relation‐
ship, and event. But this is a possibility, not a certain forecast. Some
will continue seeking perceptual form itself. Others will seek proven‐
ance, rarity, or participation. Abundance can democratize access and
produce new hierarchies of authenticity. Neither tendency is guaran‐
teed merely by the tool’s existence.
There is also the risk of saturation through local success. Each image
may be optimized to capture attention immediately, while their suc‐
cession becomes tiring. What works as an exception does not neces‐
sarily work as a permanent environment. A world where every sur‐
face shouts “look at me” can make attention not permanently solicited
difficult to achieve.
Can a Machine Understand Beauty?
At least three questions must be separated. Can a system predict judg‐
ments? Can it produce and explain structures people appreciate? Can
it itself have an aesthetic experience? Success at the first two does not
automatically resolve the third. Nor does one concrete version’s fail‐
ure prove all systems impossible in principle.
109


A functional criterion more demanding than mere prediction would
require transfer: identifying what makes a solution elegant in a new
context, showing what simplification loses, and proposing a counter‐
example to its own evaluation. It should distinguish real elegance
from hidden complexity and fruitful novelty from noise. This program
would assess competencies, not directly demonstrate the existence of
an experience.
The problem would remain difficult even for humans. We cannot veri‐
fy beauty solely through “I felt it,” but neither can we reduce experi‐
ence to a single test. We can observe sensitivity to differences, how
someone justifies and revises, the capacity to discover new forms. For
artificial systems, such criteria can make discussion more precise
without settling it metaphysically.
The most interesting question is not whether algorithms will produce
beautiful objects in general. It is what relationship with form we will
construct around them. Will we use abundance to explore more or re‐
main within an ever-smoother version of our preferences? A techno‐
logy can multiply available beauties without automatically multiply‐
ing our capacity to encounter them.

CHAPTER 27
Real or Imagined? A Beauty That
Needs Us
On an island no being capable of perceiving it reaches, a flower opens.
Is it beautiful? The answer seems to require choosing between two
worlds: one where beauty awaits discovery and another where it be‐
gins existing only with the gaze. But the question conceals several
meanings of existence.
Strong objectivism says beauty belongs to the object independently of
our reactions. The position’s advantage is preserving discovery’s
meaning and the possibility of errors of taste. Not all evaluations
would be equally good. The difficulty is identifying the relevant prop‐
erty and explaining disagreement: how do we recognize it without ul‐
timately using observers’ judgments? If the answer is “through ex‐
perts,” their selection without circularity must be explained.
Strong subjectivism says beauty is essentially the person’s reaction.
The position naturally explains taste’s variation and intimacy. Diffi‐
culty arises when we want to distinguish a capricious preference from
an attentive judgment, explain learning, or discuss reasons. If
everything ends with “I like it,” aesthetic conversation risks no longer
producing knowledge, only an inventory of reactions.
Social constructivism emphasizes norms, institutions, and histories. It
explains why certain classifications become dominant and acquire au‐
thority. The difficulty is not reducing experience to merely executing a
rule. Someone may discover a pleasure their environment disap‐
proves. Moreover, objects and perceptual capacities impose con‐
straints: culture cannot obtain any effect merely by issuing a label.
Philosophical debate about beauty traverses precisely the tensions
among such families of answers. [2]
111


None of these positions should be presented in its weakest form. Ob‐
jectivists need not believe in a simple formula; subjectivists can ac‐
knowledge reasons for preferences; constructivists can recognize
body and world. Serious differences concern what makes a judgment
correct and how independent beauty can be of possible observers.
The Strongest Argument for Objectivity
Objectivists can begin with the experience of correction. You listened
hurriedly to a piece, then understood a relationship you had missed.
You do not merely say your mood changed; you say you saw some‐
thing that was there. Some judgments are better informed, more at‐
tentive, more capable of holding parts together. This makes intelligible
the idea that beauty is not at the discretion of every reaction.
But a single objective ranking does not immediately follow. We can re‐
cognize the same properties and value them differently. One prefers
tension, another calm; one admires density, another economy. Object‐
ivists must show why certain weights are correct, not merely why
shared properties exist. Otherwise, truth about structure is confused
with uniqueness of value.
The Strongest Argument for Subjectivity
Subjectivists can observe that a world entirely lacking beings capable
of appreciation would contain forms but not beauty’s experience.
Moreover, taste is intimate and can remain different even among at‐
tentive observers. No apparatus can turn these differences into an in‐
disputable measure of value. This is a serious objection to treating
beauty like mass or temperature.
Yet dependence on a subject does not imply arbitrariness. Pain de‐
pends on beings feeling it, but is not invented by a vote. Readability
depends on the reader, but some configurations make reading harder
under specified conditions. These analogies do not prove beauty
works exactly the same way. They merely show that “independent or
unreal” is an impoverished alternative.
112


Relational Realism and Its Price
The position proposed in this book is relational realism: beauty is real
as a way of encounter between properties of the world, a being’s capa‐
cities, and the conditions of experience. The formula represents
neither a proven scientific theory nor a philosophical invention
without precedents. It is the book’s argumentative choice: avoiding
both beauty’s complete detachment from the observer and declaring it
arbitrary fiction.
A relationship is no less real because it requires more than one term.
Being readable presupposes a text and a reader with certain capacit‐
ies. Fitting a hand presupposes an object and a body. It does not follow
that readability or fit can be decreed regardless of properties. Like‐
wise, beauty can depend on observers without being completely at
their will’s disposal.
In the island flower, we can distinguish properties existing without a
viewer, the possibility of an experience for certain beings, and the ac‐
tual experience. The first and second can exist without the third oc‐
curring. Calling the flower beautiful in the absence of any spectator
may attribute a relational disposition: it could produce a certain kind
of experience under suitable conditions. But choosing those condi‐
tions remains a philosophical problem, not a measurement solved by
vocabulary.
The immediate objection is that we have moved the difficulty into
“suitable conditions.” Who establishes them? If we choose only ob‐
servers confirming our taste, relational realism becomes circular jus‐
tification. The answer must be procedural: compare perspectives,
make conditions visible, distinguish factual errors from preferences,
and leave open the possibility of different but well-grounded experi‐
ences.
We can be factually mistaken about an object: believe it handmade
when it is not, mishear a sequence, or attribute an intention to a work
that documents refute. Correction can alter aesthetic judgment. We
can also see the same properties and give them different weights. Not
all disagreements are of the same type, and not all must be resolved
by declaring a loser.
113


How, then, do we speak of progress in taste? Not necessarily as ap‐
proaching a final ranking, but as extending the capacity to perceive,
compare, and justify. Someone understanding more possibilities and
recognizing their limits may have more cultivated taste without pre‐
ferring the same things as another equally attentive person. Pluralism
need not mean absence of demanding standards; it can mean several
forms of demand irreducible to one another.
To strengthen this position, we can require more than a group’s agree‐
ment. An evaluation should identify properties, withstand correction
of factual errors, and remain intelligible when irrelevant conditions
change. If admiration for a proof disappears only because we learn its
author lacks prestige, we have reason to examine reputation’s role. If
it disappears because we discover an error, the change has another
structure.
But some apparently external conditions belong to the object. A love
letter’s author is not irrelevant to evaluating it as a love letter. An in‐
herited object’s provenance is not identical to its auction price. We
therefore cannot construct a universal rule that context must be re‐
moved. We must specify what kind of thing we appreciate.
Would an Ideal Observer See Everything?
Imagine a being knowing every property of a work, every intention,
every effect, and every relevant tradition. Would it have a single taste?
This is not obvious. Complete knowledge does not automatically estab‐
lish a single weighting of values. Perhaps such an observer would un‐
derstand more incompatible beauties rather than reduce them to one.
Moreover, idealization can eliminate what makes certain experiences
possible. A being that never forgets, fears, or loses anything would en‐
counter an elegy differently. One without a body would not have the
same relationship with grace of movement. Demanding judgment
from an observer lacking our limits can change the object, not merely
improve evaluation.
This does not mean every limit should be celebrated. Fatigue, preju‐
dice, and inattention can obstruct experience. But some conditions of
beauty are also conditions of finitude: expectation, incomplete
114


memory, vulnerability, surprise. An ideal of objectivity eliminating all
of them might eliminate, along with error, part of the phenomenon it
sought to understand.
Beauties Beyond the Human
We can imagine beings with other senses, lifespans, and ways of mov‐
ing. Some mathematical structures might be accessible to them in
common with us. Other experiences, tied to body, environment, and
history, might differ radically. This is a philosophical experiment, not
a claim about extraterrestrials’ existence or psychology.
If the differences were great, would we conclude our beauty false?
Not necessarily. It could be real for a kind of encounter without being
universal for every possible being. A key fits a lock without being the
key to all locks. The analogy has limits, but shows how a relationship
can have real constraints and a limited domain.
The question remains whether “beautiful” names a single property or
a family of related values. The book favors the second possibility. This
does not prohibit seeking shared mechanisms such as attention, learn‐
ing, or evaluation. It only prevents treating a local explanation’s suc‐
cess as absorbing all experiences into one formula.
The book’s answer is therefore affirmative with a decisive qualifica‐
tion: beauty is real, but not as a mass measured in kilograms is real.
Its reactions, relationships, and effects exist. The words and standards
organizing them are historical constructions. Some constructions help
us see more; others turn a partial perspective into total authority. Cri‐
ticism’s task is not to destroy experience but to identify this difference.
This position does not eliminate mystery, but makes it more precise.
We do not ask whether the mind invented beauty from nothing or the
world stored it like a mineral. We ask how certain forms become valu‐
able in certain encounters and how much of that value can be shared,
justified, and transformed. Beauty’s reality can be a reality of relation‐
ships without therefore being second-rate reality.

CHAPTER 28
Who Gains, Who Loses, and What
Is Worth Sacrificing?
If we eliminated the word “beautiful” tomorrow, would appearance’s
privileges disappear? Probably not: in such a thought experiment,
people could continue preferring certain forms, faces, and voices us‐
ing other words or none. An important distinction follows: the
concept alone does not produce all the effects discussed. Reactions ex‐
ist, and the concept names, groups, and can authorize them.
Yet the word does something an isolated reaction does not. It permits
transmitting a criterion, defending a canon, constructing a reputation.
“I like it” speaks of a relationship; “it is beautiful” can sound like a
verdict others must recognize. This passage is not always abusive. It
makes critical discussion possible. But it also makes imposing a pref‐
erence as a norm possible.
Beauty’s first beneficiary is the person enjoying it. This gain should
not be minimized because it does not immediately become money or
performance. An experience enriching life has value without passing
a usefulness test. Creators and those caring for forms, places, and
practices can likewise find meaning and livelihoods in this shared ca‐
pacity to appreciate.
Another kind of beneficiary is the person matching a socially rewar‐
ded standard. The evidence about professional evaluations and com‐
pensation discussed earlier shows why this advantage cannot be re‐
duced to the disadvantaged’s mere imagination. [1] Yet the benefit de‐
pends on domain and context. It offers no suffering-free life and can‐
cels no other vulnerabilities. Identifying privilege does not mean
claiming its bearer can no longer suffer injustice.
Those defining and administering standards can gain authority: se‐
lecting what is seen, taught, worth buying. Providers of goods and ser‐
vices can profit from the desire to approach the ideal. An owner of a
desirable good can gain from its appreciation. These gains are not all
116


illegitimate. They become problematic when presented as natural ef‐
fects without alternatives or when costs imposed on others are hid‐
den.
Those losing may be people excluded from opportunities by irrelevant
criteria, creators who do not fit the dominant channel, and those ob‐
liged to invest constantly in conformity. The public can also lose when
selection for attractiveness conceals important competencies or ex‐
periences. Loss is not always visible as insult. It may be an absence:
someone was not invited, a work did not circulate, a problem did not
become perceptible.
There are also costs borne by the apparent winner. If the value as‐
signed to someone depends on maintaining an image, the advantage
can come with an obligation to preserve it. This is a structural possib‐
ility, not a diagnosis of all attractive people. Being admired does not
automatically mean being known, and being desired does not auto‐
matically mean being respected.
Shared Beauty and Competitive Beauty
To judge distribution, separating two situations helps. In the first, sev‐
eral people enjoy the same music without one person’s pleasure redu‐
cing another’s. In the second, only a limited number can occupy the
position of “most beautiful,” and rewards depend on rank. The first
permits shared gains; the second can turn individual improvement
into escalating competition.
The two can mingle. A garment can offer pleasure and status. A mu‐
seum can be a place of discovery and a sign of belonging. An urban in‐
tervention can create a common good and private advantage. We will
not understand distribution if we decide beforehand that every object
is either innocent or an instrument of domination. We must ask what
functions it has and what rules amplify them.
Analysis of winners should not stop with people. Certain forms of
activity also gain: what can be shown, narrated, or elegantly com‐
pressed sometimes receives attention more easily. Research with a
clear result can be easier to communicate than research cautiously
117


delimiting three mechanisms. Discreet work can remain invisible be‐
side spectacular performance. These are mechanisms to analyze, not
universal statistical verdicts.
Likewise, those losing are not only people evaluated unfavorably. The
public may lose when seduction replaces knowledge; creators when
required to repeat the selling formula; institutions when optimizing
activity’s representation instead of activity; future users when imme‐
diate beauty hides maintenance costs. But so may those denied beauty
in the name of supposedly more serious utility.
The First Choice: Truth Without Brilliance
A team has two ways to present a result. The first is spectacular, unify‐
ing, memorable, but omits an important limit. The second is less eleg‐
ant and more accurate. Choosing correctness seems obvious. Yet the
practical difficulty remains: the accurate version risks going unheard,
and a good idea may lose access to the public.
It does not follow that omission is justified. It follows that the mature
alternative is additional aesthetic work: finding an accurate form that
can be received. Sometimes we find it. Sometimes not immediately.
We must then accept diminished impact rather than redefine truth ac‐
cording to its capacity to seduce. Beauty matters precisely because giv‐
ing it up can cost.
The Second Choice: The Beautiful City and the
Livable City
An imaginary urban project produces a spectacular square but re‐
duces accessible places where people sat without consuming. Another
project is less photogenic but preserves that possibility. The dispute
cannot be reduced to “beauty versus justice,” since the second project
can have its own aesthetic qualities. But neither should we automatic‐
ally invent reconciliation: sometimes there is a real conflict between a
composition’s effect and certain uses.
Deciding means specifying who looks, who lives there, who maintains,
and who bears the cost. An aesthetics of photography can favor one
solution, an aesthetics of everyday life another. There is no neutral
118


universal spectator experiencing every consequence. Precisely for
that reason, public beauty needs more perspectives than the form’s
author’s.
The Third Choice: Freedom to Desire
Imagine a mechanism distributing interpersonal attraction more
equally. Nobody would remain so invisible. But it would modify pref‐
erences without people’s agreement. We would obtain an apparently
less cruel distribution at the price of intervening in intimate freedom.
The experiment shows that reducing aesthetic inequality is not always
an operation without costs of its own.
The alternative is not abandoning those who suffer. We can change
contexts, opportunities, institutions, and how appearance becomes
rights or access. But we cannot promise a world where all desires
match reciprocally. Beauty often involves selection, and selection
leaves unchosen possibilities behind. An analysis hiding this fact be‐
neath an optimistic formula does not become more humane; it be‐
comes less accurate.
The Fourth Choice: The Safe Life and the Intense
Life
Someone may choose a difficult artistic project over a more predict‐
able life. Not every risk is wise, and not every sacrifice creates value.
But neither can we declare only maximizing comfort rational. Some
lives are directed by forms of excellence the person considers worth
the cost. Requiring art always to prove it loses nothing would judge it
by a criterion many important human choices do not satisfy.
The limit arises when costs are transferred to others without their
participation. Creators can decide about their own time but do not
automatically command every surrounding life. Here aesthetic
autonomy meets real relationships. No formula resolves every situ‐
ation, but there is a precise question: who accepted the sacrifice, and
who was assigned the role of bearing it?
119


Without a Single Currency of Value
The four choices do not demonstrate values are always incompatible.
Many projects can become simultaneously more beautiful, accurate,
and livable. But optimism about total reconciliation is itself an aes‐
thetic preference: we like the image of a world where all good things
fit. Sometimes this very image prevents us from seeing conflict.
A more demanding position admits beauty has real value but no
monopoly. It need not disappear for truth or freedom to matter; nor
must truth and freedom be reduced to forms of beauty for us to ad‐
mire them. Sometimes we choose something less beautiful for good
reasons. Sometimes we choose beauty and accept a cost that is not
simply error.
Aesthetic education might also be judged by its capacity to see these
costs without becoming incapable of pleasure. It would produce not
only consumers of finer taste but people knowing what they admire,
what does not follow from admiration, and what they lose in choos‐
ing. This demands more than moralizing every form and is less com‐
fortable than unconditionally defending charm.
In the end, “who gains from beauty?” turns toward its questioner. The
critic gains something too: lucidity, perhaps prestige, perhaps the pro‐
tection of no longer being easily moved. They should also ask what
they lose. A completely unmasked world can become one where we
no longer know how to receive anything without suspecting its func‐
tion. Beauty deserves criticism, but not because we have proved we
should manage without it.

EPILOGUE
After the Three Rooms
Not all beautiful things ask to be followed. Some ask only to be seen
accurately, including what makes them dangerous.
Let us return to the three rooms. The proof is still there. Now we know
its beauty was not merely a formula’s appearance, but how a relation‐
ship made necessity visible. The sleeping person is still there. Their
beauty cannot fit a ranking because the experience’s object includes
shared history. Power’s spectacle continues on the screen. We can see
its precision without pretending precision justifies the world it serves.
We have not discovered a single ingredient. We have discovered ways
form, attention, and value meet: recognition, surprise, coherence, ex‐
pressiveness, presence, intensity, belonging, skill. Sometimes they sup‐
port one another. Sometimes they conflict. A form can become more
accurate and less seductive; more accessible and less singular; freer
and less unified.
Beauty can take us out of the immediate calculation of self-interest. It
can equally become its most effective instrument. It can open atten‐
tion toward what we did not know and close investigation through the
impression that everything is already in order. This ambivalence is
not a peripheral flaw of the concept. It is one condition of its power.
It would be tempting to end with a recipe: preserve good beauty and
reject bad beauty. But experience does not always arrive with labels
prepared. Moreover, some conflicts are not between true and false
beauty, but between beauty and other real values. Recognizing con‐
flict can be more honest than redefining terms until our preferred
choice wins every test.
We can nevertheless learn something practical. When we say “beauti‐
ful,” let us better know what we praise. When we say “elegant,” let us
ask where complexity was placed. When a form promises truth, let us
see what evidence it lacks. When criticism promises liberation from
every seduction, let us see whether it, too, asks us to admire our own
image of lucidity.
121


There remains the value of attention rushing neither to adore nor dis‐
mantle. It can receive pleasure without declaring it universal. It can
acknowledge loss without romanticizing it. It can call a work remark‐
able and not want its world. Sometimes it can allow itself to be
changed before knowing exactly in what direction.
Perhaps the deepest question is not whether beauty belongs to world
or mind. It is what beings we become when certain things come to
seem beautiful. We do not choose our initial sensitivity from nothing.
But neither do we remain identical to its first form. What we learn to
admire changes our life’s possibilities, and what we refuse to admire
any longer may sometimes be liberation and sometimes amputation.
On leaving, the world is no less beautiful for offering no single verdict.
It is harder to classify. A proof, a voice, and a crowd do not demand
the same fidelity. Perhaps maturity begins not when fascination
ceases, but when fascination no longer obliges us to call good
everything briefly shining.

CONCEPTUAL APPENDIX
An Atlas of the Forms of Beauty
and Their Neighbors
The map below is not a list of exclusive species. Some categories con‐
cern the object, others the mechanism of appreciation or mode of ex‐
perience. They can overlap: a movement can be bodily, functional, ex‐
pressive, and graceful simultaneously. Neighboring categories remain
distinct where reducing them to “beautiful” would lose an important
difference.
Forms, Bodies, and Environments
Formal beauty. Appreciation of relationships among parts: propor‐
tion, rhythm, contrast, balance, tension. It does not necessarily re‐
quire symmetry. A deviation may organize the whole better than reg‐
ularity without differences. The test is asking what would be lost if we
changed a part, not merely how orderly the configuration seems.
Bodily beauty. Appreciation of a being through appearance, posture,
expression, and presence. It can be static or temporal. It does not coin‐
cide with assessing health, character, or intellectual capacity, and a
face’s photograph does not exhaust it. Different observers can admire
a body in different registers.
Erotic beauty. Beauty here participates in desire for intimate close‐
ness. Desire and aesthetic judgment can meet but are not identical: we
can admire without desiring and desire without considering the per‐
son an exemplar of a canon. Context and reciprocity change the ex‐
perience.
Expressive beauty. The value of how a form makes emotion, attitude,
or perspective perceptible. An imperfect voice can be expressive; an
impeccable one can remain neutral. Not every expressiveness is
beauty, but sometimes expressive precision becomes admiration’s
principal reason.
123


Natural beauty. Encounter with forms and processes not appreciated
primarily as intentional works. It may concern an immediate config‐
uration or a structure understood through knowledge. Beauty does
not prove nature benevolent, and ecological value does not reduce to
appearance.
Ecological and processual beauty. Appreciation of relationships of
transformation, interdependence, and continuity. Using the term re‐
quires precision: complexity and stability are not automatically
beauty. Sometimes understanding the process changes how we see a
previously unpleasant or apparently disorderly configuration.
Architectural beauty. The success of an organization of space, mater‐
ial, light, and movement. It can be admired from outside but is also in‐
habited. Photography, use, and maintenance can produce different
evaluations. A building is more than the composition offered to a fa‐
vorably positioned viewer.
Everyday and atmospheric beauty. The quality of a situation distrib‐
uted among objects, light, sound, temperature, rhythm, and practices.
It does not always have a spectacular center. Habit can make it invis‐
ible, noticed only when a relationship breaks down. See chapter 18.
Tactile, olfactory, and gustatory beauty. Appreciation of texture,
temperature, resistance, smell, taste, and their transformation in ex‐
perience. Not every sensory pleasure is aesthetic judgment, but it can
become so through attention to organization and contrast. Neither im‐
age nor verbal description fully reproduces these encounters.
Intelligence, Knowledge, and Achievement
Mathematical beauty. It may concern economy, generality, surprise,
transparency, or unification. Correctness is a separate question, al‐
though verification can strengthen admiration. A short proof may
hide much knowledge, and a long one contain a remarkable idea. See
chapter 9.
124


Scientific beauty. The elegance of an explanation, a model, or a con‐
nection between phenomena. Unifying power is valuable when it pro‐
duces testable constraints and predictions. A construction can be
beautiful and empirically unsuitable. A well-delimited domain can it‐
self be an aesthetic and intellectual achievement.
Conceptual beauty. A concept succeeds when it organizes cases, per‐
mits inferences, and preserves necessary differences. An explanation
covering everything but excluding nothing can seduce without in‐
forming. Elegance also appears in the questions it enables, not only
the definition’s brevity.
Intellectual beauty. Appreciation of a way of thinking: clarity, mobil‐
ity among perspectives, proportion of argument, capacity to find a
fruitful representation. It coincides neither with intelligence meas‐
ured by a single test nor with every conclusion’s truth. Seductive intel‐
ligence can serve an error.
Explanatory and pedagogical beauty. A successful passage from
non-understanding to understanding. A good explanation does not
secretly eliminate difficulty but finds an accessible form for it. It may
be elegant for a beginner and needlessly slow for an expert: the rela‐
tionship with the audience belongs to the object.
Technical and functional beauty. The fit between means, purpose,
user, and conditions. It includes how a solution accommodates error
and change. A minimum component count is no universal criterion.
Sometimes a reserve or repairability makes the whole more elegant
although the image becomes more complex.
The beauty of craftsmanship. Admiration for an execution’s material
intelligence: gesture, knowledge of resistance, adaptation, and finish.
It can appear in the finished object or only in the process. It does not
necessarily require hiding traces of the hand; these may contribute to
success or remain mere errors.
125


Time, Expression, and Intensity
Musical beauty. The organization of sonic experience in time: timbre,
rhythm, tension, return, contrast, and relationship. It is not the sum of
agreeable notes. Expectations matter, but maximum surprise does not
automatically produce pleasure. Knowledge and repetition can
change the heard object. See chapter 13.
Verbal and poetic beauty. The achievement through which a formu‐
lation’s sound, rhythm, meaning, and structure work together. A para‐
phrase may preserve information and lose experience. Yet a formula‐
tion’s charm can increase the impression of truth without additional
evidence. Admiring and verifying remain distinct operations.
Narrative beauty. The relationship among events, perspectives,
rhythm, and retrospective transformation of meaning. An ending can
change the beginning. Coherence does not require the narrated world
to be just or agreeable. A story can be too tidy precisely because it
eliminated experiences that would complicate its message.
The graceful. Ease, measure, and continuity perceived in a move‐
ment or gesture. It can conceal much practice and depends on under‐
standing difficulty. It is not identical to the beauty of manifest effort.
Nor does control of movement determine the value of its purpose. See
chapter 14.
The sublime. A neighboring category, not a simple superlative of
beauty. It involves a relationship with what exceeds our scale, grasp,
or power, theorized differently by Burke and Kant. The observer’s dis‐
tance and safety can radically change the experience. See chapter 15.
The tragic and dark beauty. A form’s success in preserving loss, con‐
flict, or suffering without an easy resolution. Value may lie in express‐
ive precision, not approval of what happens. Not every important
work in this register must be called beautiful. See chapter 16.
The grotesque, strange, and unsettling. Categories disturbing famili‐
ar divisions and potentially mixing attraction with repulsion. Immedi‐
ately calling them “unconventional beauty” may lose ambivalence. In‐
terest, intensity, and artistic value are not obligatory synonyms of
beauty.
126


The comic and humor’s elegance. A successful change of frame, con‐
trast, or surprising economy of means. It can produce intellectual ad‐
miration, not merely laughter. A cruel joke can be formally ingenious,
and its content can change how we experience its ingenuity.
Temporal beauty, patina, and fragility. Appreciating form through
transformation, trace, duration, or possible loss. Not every deteriora‐
tion is beautiful, nor every disappearance necessary to value. Some‐
times what matters is the object’s actual history, not an appearance of
age that can be imitated. See chapter 17.
Relationships, Cultures, and Authority
Relational and intimate beauty. A presence becomes valuable with‐
in a shared history. It cannot automatically be replaced by a superior
specimen under general criteria. Intimacy can reveal and idealize; it
offers no infallibility. See chapter 8.
Cultural and ritual beauty. The fit among practices, gestures, objects,
and shared time. It can support participation and discipline roles. A
world’s coherence has value but does not prove every participant
lives well within it. See chapters 4 and 22.
Moral beauty. Appreciation of the form in which a value manifests:
tact, measure, generosity without ostentation, fit between intention
and gesture. Kindness can be clumsy, and elegance can cover absent
responsibility. Nor does virtue’s emotional effect guarantee its prac‐
tice. See chapter 21.
Spiritual beauty. Experience of a form interpreted in relation to ulti‐
mate meaning, transcendence, or the sacred. Its intensity can be real
without compelling a single metaphysical conclusion. Austerity and
abundance can function differently as forms of attention. See chapter
20.
The beauty of power and grandeur. Admiration for capacity, co‐
ordination, scale, precision, or control. It can exist in cooperation and
domination. Aesthetic impact does not demonstrate legitimacy, but
condemning the purpose does not alone explain form’s seduction
either. See chapter 19.
127


Social elegance. The fit between gesture and others’ situation: speak‐
ing at the right time, leaving room, avoiding unnecessary exposure. It
can be real care or refined administration of distance. A learned code
and sensitivity toward the person are not always the same thing.
Artificial and theatrical beauty. The pleasure of an appearance that
may deliberately display its construction. A mask is not always fraud;
it can be play, exploration, or expression. The relevant criterion in‐
cludes the relationship offered to spectators, not merely the opposi‐
tion between natural and manufactured. See chapter 25.
The cute and tender. Registers inviting closeness, protection, and at‐
tention without monumentality. They are not inferior versions of the
sublime. Yet reducing a being to what is adorable can conceal capacit‐
ies, conflicts, and autonomy that the preferred form does not show.
The picturesque and photogenic. Qualities of a configuration en‐
countered as scene or image. They can be real yet partial. What suc‐
ceeds compositionally from one point can be hard to inhabit from an‐
other. Photogenic quality should not be confused with the place’s total
value.
Digital and generative beauty. Not a single aesthetic category but a
family of means and production relations. It can produce visual, sonic,
narrative, or interactive forms. Questions of quality, provenance, ex‐
perience, and distribution must be separated. See chapter 26.

METHODOLOGICAL NOTE
How We Read the Evidence and
How We Might Investigate Further
This book is a narrative synthesis of philosophy and empirical re‐
search, not a systematic review or a new meta-analysis. Philosophical
texts are discussed as arguments and historical perspectives; studies
are used for delimited questions. Thought experiments, design ex‐
amples, and evaluation criteria are the volume’s analytical proposals,
not results reported by the cited researchers.
What Each Type of Evidence Can Support
Research on beauty begins with a choice: what counts as beauty? It
may use scale ratings, choices between images, viewing time, willing‐
ness to pay, or a brain response. These measures are not synonyms.
Viewing time may indicate interest without admiration; price may in‐
clude rarity; ratings may depend on instructions. The first check is
whether the conclusion preserves the measure’s meaning.
Next comes the sample. We must know who evaluated, what objects
were selected, and who is absent. Agreement within a group is not a
law of humanity. A difference between two groups does not alone
show whether the explanation is genetic, cultural, economic, or pro‐
cedural. Comparing means must be accompanied by variation within
groups.
An experiment changes a condition and compares outcomes, ideally
through random assignment. It can support a causal conclusion about
the tested change. It does not automatically turn a laboratory task into
all social life. Andersson’s study of a bird’s tail length and Mobius and
Rosenblat’s economic experiment exemplify interventions with pre‐
cise domains. Their strength also comes from that domain being
nameable. [22] [43]
129


An observational study finds relationships between variables without
manipulating them in the same way. It can be highly informative, but
alternative explanations must be analyzed. Statistical control helps
within the limits of the variables and assumptions used; it is no wand
eliminating every unmeasured difference.
Meta-analysis combines studies without eliminating their limits. Selec‐
tion, differences between tasks, and unpublished results matter. An
average effect does not describe every person.
An evolutionary or algorithmic model demonstrates or tests some‐
thing relative to defined assumptions and data. A mechanism’s possib‐
ility does not prove it produced actual history. A model’s prediction of
ratings does not prove the ratings just. A precise score can answer a
wrong question very well.
Finally, a moral conclusion needs arguments about rights, con‐
sequences, and justification. It cannot be mechanically extracted from
correlation, brain activation, or reproductive outcome. Data can show
what happens; choosing what should be accepted requires an addi‐
tional argumentative step.
How We Might Test an Explanation of Elegance
An experimental program could compare two correct solutions to the
same problem: one shorter, another more explicit. Participants would
evaluate elegance, explain the solution, and solve a new problem. If
preference for the short form is not accompanied by understanding or
transfer, we would have reason to distinguish compression’s seduction
from cognitive gain. The result would not settle every form of eleg‐
ance but would test a precise mechanism.
Another comparison would preserve the object and modify proven‐
ance information, clearly indicating what is evaluated: perceptual
pleasure, the work’s value as an act, or estimated price. It should then
check whether participants believe the labels and whether the effect
depends on experience. Bellaiche and colleagues’ study exemplifies
experimental research on attributed authorship without closing every
question about authenticity. [48]
130


Algorithmic recommendations would require evaluation over time,
not merely of a click. We could compare immediate satisfaction with
diversity of exploration and discoveries’ retrospective value. A system
faithfully reproducing current taste and one helping develop it do not
pursue exactly the same target. Choosing the target is also a cultural
decision, not merely an optimization problem.
These outlines are research proposals. They were not carried out for
this book’s writing. Their role is showing how an interesting claim can
connect to observations that might support or challenge it.
