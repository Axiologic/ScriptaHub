Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein

Introduction

"Frankenstein" is more than just a gothic horror tale about a man and his monstrous creation. It’s a reflection on human ambition, responsibility, and the consequences of pushing scientific boundaries too far. Written over 200 years ago, the novel’s themes resonate just as powerfully today, in an era when artificial intelligence, biotechnology, and scientific advancement pose ethical questions that mirror the ones Mary Shelley explored in her time.

The story begins with Victor Frankenstein, a bright young scientist from Geneva, who becomes obsessed with the idea of creating life. His ambition leads him to the University of Ingolstadt, where he immerses himself in the works of ancient alchemists and modern science. But Victor doesn’t stop at theory—he becomes consumed by a desire to achieve the impossible: to bring a lifeless body to life. And one stormy night, he succeeds. But instead of feeling triumph, Victor is horrified by the monstrous being he has created. In a moment of panic, he abandons the creature, leaving it alone and confused in the world. This single decision sets off a tragic chain of events, as Victor’s refusal to face the consequences of his actions haunts him at every turn.

As time passes, Victor tries to move on with his life, but his creation does not disappear. The creature, rejected by its creator and society, becomes bitter and vengeful. Its search for companionship and meaning turns into a quest for revenge, beginning with the murder of Victor’s younger brother, William. Victor, wracked with guilt, realizes the creature is responsible, but he remains silent, allowing an innocent woman, Justine, to be executed for the crime. This pivotal moment underscores one of the novel’s core themes: the devastating consequences of shirking responsibility. The creature eventually confronts Victor and tells him its side of the story—a tale of loneliness, rejection, and a longing for connection. It demands that Victor create a companion for it, someone who will not fear or despise it. Victor, feeling both guilt and responsibility, reluctantly agrees. But as he begins work on this second creation, he becomes consumed with fear over the potential consequences of unleashing yet another being into the world. In a moment of panic, he destroys the second creature, sealing his fate. The enraged monster vows to make Victor suffer by taking away everything he loves.

The story hurtles toward its tragic conclusion as Victor loses his closest friends and family, one by one, to the creature’s revenge. In a final, desperate attempt to stop the monster, Victor pursues it across the frozen Arctic. But in the end, both creator and creation are consumed by their mutual hatred and despair, leaving behind a chilling reminder of the dangers of unchecked ambition.

Chapter One — A Mad Scientist’s Dream

Victor Frankenstein is not your average student. In fact, if he were around today, he’d probably be that one person in class who never missed a lecture always raised his hand, and spent all his free time in the lab. But Victor's love of learning goes way beyond acing exams—he's obsessed with unlocking the greatest mysteries of life itself. Picture this: while most people are content with studying biology or chemistry, Victor is diving headfirst into the world of alchemy, ancient texts, and forbidden knowledge, determined to discover the secret to creating life. And this is where his story begins.

Victor grows up in a well-to-do family in Geneva, Switzerland, surrounded by loving parents and a best friend, Henry Clerval, who’s his complete opposite in many ways. While Henry is all about chivalry, poetry, and adventure, Victor is consumed by the desire to push the boundaries of science. From a young age, he’s enchanted by the idea of understanding the forces that govern nature, and this passion only grows stronger as he gets older. But instead of sticking to the safe, established sciences, he becomes fascinated by the works of long-dead alchemists—men who believed they could turn lead into gold and discover the elixir of life. You know, minor details.

When Victor is a teenager, he stumbles across a book by Cornelius Agrippa, one of these ancient thinkers, and it’s like a switch flips in his brain. His father, in one of those classic moments where a parent tries to steer their child away from a bad idea, casually tells him not to waste his time on “sad trash.” But, of course, this only makes Victor more determined to read every word. He devours the works of Agrippa, Paracelsus, and other old-school scientists, all while dreaming of the great discoveries he might make.

It’s easy to see where this is going, right? Victor is one of those people who thinks, “If no one else can figure it out, I’ll just do it myself.” And so, he makes it his life’s mission to uncover the secret to creating life—because what could possibly go wrong with that?

As he grows older, his parents support his education, sending him off to study at the University of Ingolstadt. This is a big moment for Victor, as it's his chance to explore the world beyond Geneva and dive deeper into his scientific pursuits. But just as he’s preparing to leave for university, tragedy strikes. His beloved mother, Caroline, falls ill and dies. Her death shakes Victor to his core, and in her final moments, she expresses a wish that Victor and Elizabeth, a close family friend who’s like a sister to him, will one day marry. Victor is heartbroken, but his mother’s death only strengthens his resolve. Now, more than ever, he’s determined to conquer death itself. If he can find the secret to creating life, maybe no one else will have to suffer the pain of loss as he has.

With his heart heavy but his mind determined, Victor arrives at Ingolstadt, ready to throw himself into his studies. He meets two professors who will shape his path—one who dismisses his old alchemy books as nonsense, and another, Professor Waldman, who recognizes Victor's potential and encourages his curiosity. Waldman’s enthusiasm for the modern sciences—chemistry, biology, and physics—inspires Victor to combine his knowledge of both ancient and modern sciences in a way no one else has ever done before. He’s convinced that if he can just find the right combination of knowledge, he can unlock the ultimate mystery: how to create life from inanimate matter.

And so, Victor begins his secret experiments. He becomes consumed by his work, spending long hours in the lab, isolating himself from friends and family, driven by a single, obsessive goal. As the months pass, he pushes himself to the brink of exhaustion, scavenging materials from graveyards and butcher shops to piece together what he hopes will be his crowning achievement: a living being, created from scratch.

Now, at this point, you’re probably thinking, “Victor, maybe slow down a bit. Are you sure this is a good idea?” But Victor is too deep into his work to consider the consequences. He’s convinced that once he succeeds, the world will praise him as a genius, a modern-day Prometheus who has stolen the secret of life from the gods. He imagines a future where he is hailed as the man who conquered death and gave humanity the power to create life. What he doesn’t realize is that playing God comes with a cost—a cost he’s about to find out in the most horrifying way.

One stormy night, the moment finally arrives. Victor stands over his creation, a creature pieced together from the bodies of the dead, waiting for the spark of life to ignite. With a surge of electricity—because of course, it’s a dark and stormy night—the creature’s eyes open. It lives.

But instead of feeling the triumph he expected, Victor is immediately filled with dread. His creation, far from being the perfect specimen he imagined, is a grotesque, monstrous being, with a body stitched together in a way that looks anything but natural. The creature’s yellow eyes stare back at him, its gigantic frame lumbering awkwardly as it struggles to move. In that instant, Victor realizes the full horror of what he’s done.

Terrified by the sight of his own creation, Victor does what any rational scientist would do: he runs. He abandons the creature, leaving it to fend for itself, hoping that if he ignores the problem, it’ll just go away. Of course, nothing is ever that simple, and Victor’s decision to flee marks the beginning of a nightmare that will haunt him for the rest of his life.

In this first chapter of Victor’s story, we see the classic arc of ambition turning into disaster. Victor starts out with noble intentions—after all, who wouldn’t want to conquer death and bring life to the lifeless? But his blind obsession with achieving greatness leads him down a dangerous path, one that he can’t turn back from. And as we’ll soon see, the consequences of his actions are far more terrifying than anything he could have imagined.

Victor Frankenstein’s journey into the world of forbidden knowledge is just beginning, and already, it’s clear that his quest to create life may have some very dark and deadly consequences.

Chapter Two — The Monster’s Birth

In the second chapter of "Frankenstein", Victor’s life begins to unravel faster than a poorly made Halloween costume. After creating his creature in a fit of scientific ambition and immediately regretting it, Victor tries to return to normal life—if you can call ignoring the giant, living reminder of your terrible decision “normal.” But, as we all know, you can’t just run from your problems. They tend to catch up with you, and in Victor’s case, this is especially true.

After abandoning the creature, Victor experiences a complete mental and physical collapse. He’s struck with an illness so intense, it seems less like a common flu and more like a “my-moral-conscience-is-crashing-down-on-me” kind of sickness. For months, he’s bedridden and cared for by his friend Henry Clerval, who has just arrived in Ingolstadt. Let’s pause here to appreciate the irony: while Victor is lying in bed, recovering from the monster he created, the creature is out there, alive, and without a single idea of what to do with its newfound existence. While Victor avoids facing his creation, his problems are far from over.

Henry’s presence is a bright spot in this dark period. His kindness and enthusiasm slowly help Victor recover from his breakdown. And when Victor finally regains his health, he vows to leave all thoughts of the creature behind him. "No more monster business," he tells himself. "From now on, it’s back to regular old studying." It’s a nice thought, but we all know that’s not going to happen. Ignoring the monster doesn’t mean it ceases to exist.

Victor tries to move forward with his life, diving back into his studies and spending time with Henry, who is eager to learn and explore the university. Henry’s joy for life is infectious, and for a while, it seems like Victor might actually be able to forget his dark experiment. But this brief moment of peace is shattered when Victor receives a letter from his father, Alphonse Frankenstein. The contents of the letter hit him like a bolt of lightning: his youngest brother, William, had been murdered.

Overcome with grief, Victor immediately returns home to Geneva. The journey is long and filled with dread, as Victor is haunted by the thought that his creation—his "abandoned" creation—might be responsible for his brother’s death. As soon as he arrives in Geneva, he visits the scene of the crime, a location shrouded in dark woods and eerie silence. While walking alone, Victor spots a shadowy figure in the distance. There, lurking between the trees is the creature he brought to life. In that moment, it all becomes clear—his creation is responsible for the tragedy.

Victor is overwhelmed by guilt and horror, but does he tell anyone what he suspects? Nope. Instead of confessing that he might have accidentally unleashed a killer on his family, Victor keeps quiet. He convinces himself that no one would believe him, which—let’s be honest—might be a valid concern. “Hey, I accidentally created life in a science experiment gone wrong, and now my creation is murdering people” isn’t exactly the most believable story. But still, staying silent? Not the best move.

Here’s where things go from bad to worse: Justine Moritz, a young woman who works for the Frankenstein family, is accused of the murder. The evidence? A locket belonging to William is found in her possession. It seems that the creature, in a cruel twist of fate, had placed it there. Despite her protests of innocence, Justine is arrested and put on trial.

Victor, tormented by his knowledge of the truth, struggles with whether or not to speak up. On one hand, he knows that Justine is innocent and that his silence could lead to her wrongful execution. On the other hand, he is terrified of the consequences of revealing the existence of the creature. As Victor wrestles with his conscience, the weight of his guilt grows heavier with each passing day.

At the trial, Justine maintains her innocence, but the evidence is stacked against her. Victor, sitting quietly in the courtroom, watches as an innocent woman is condemned for a crime she didn’t commit, knowing that he is responsible for the true murderer’s existence. His guilt gnaws at him, but still, he says nothing. Justine is sentenced to death, and Victor’s silence seals her fate.

The execution of Justine is the final blow to Victor’s already fragile mental state. He is consumed by guilt, shame, and despair, knowing that two people he loved—his brother and an innocent woman—have been killed as a result of his actions. The weight of his responsibility becomes unbearable, and he begins to spiral once again into a deep depression.

Now, at this point, you’d think Victor might finally decide to take some responsibility for the creature he created. Maybe track it down, have a heart-to-heart, and, you know, stop it from killing more people. But instead, Victor chooses a different path: he decides to run away from his problems once again. This time, he heads for the Swiss Alps, hoping that a nice hiking trip will clear his mind. Nothing says “problem-solving” like fleeing into the mountains when there’s a murderous creature you created on the loose.

The natural beauty of the Alps provides a temporary escape for Victor. As he climbs higher into the mountains, surrounded by snow-capped peaks and fresh air, he feels a brief sense of peace. But the tranquility doesn’t last long. In a scene that could only be described as "Frankenstein meets extreme hiking", Victor encounters the creature on a glacier. The sight of the creature brings all of Victor’s guilt and fear rushing back, but this time, the creature doesn’t attack him. Instead, it speaks.

This is a pivotal moment in the story, where the creature reveals its side of the tale. Abandoned at birth, confused and rejected by everyone it encounters, the creature has been wandering the wilderness, learning about the world and growing more bitter by the day. It tells Victor of the misery and loneliness it has endured, describing how it tried to befriend humans, only to be met with fear and violence at every turn. The creature’s isolation and pain have driven it to a dark place, and it blames Victor for its suffering.

But instead of seeking revenge, the creature makes a request. It tells Victor that all it wants is companionship—someone like itself, who will not reject or fear it. The creature asks Victor to create a female companion for it, promising that if Victor fulfills this request, the two of them will disappear into the wilderness and live in peace, far away from humanity.

Victor, still reeling from the deaths of William and Justine, initially refuses. But the creature’s tale of suffering strikes a chord with him. He begins to feel a twisted sense of responsibility—not just for the creature’s actions, but for the creature’s loneliness as well. After all, he "did" create this being and then abandoned it. Perhaps this is a way to make things right.

And so, in one of the more questionable decisions of his life (and there have been many), Victor agrees to the creature’s request. He promises to create a female companion for it, believing that this will finally put an end to the nightmare he’s been living.

As the chapter closes, Victor is left standing on the glacier, contemplating the enormity of what he’s just agreed to. He’s already played God once, and it’s cost him dearly. Now, he’s about to do it again, and there’s no telling what new horrors might follow.

Stay tuned for the next chapter, where Victor attempts to play matchmaker for the world's first pair of reanimated corpses. What could possibly go wrong?

Chapter Three — The Bride of Frankenstein... Sort Of

Victor Frankenstein, having just agreed to create a second creature, returns home, but instead of feeling relieved, he’s filled with dread. The creature’s request haunts him day and night. After all, his first attempt at playing God led to disaster—so what’s to stop the second creation from being just as bad, if not worse? Imagine Victor as the world’s worst matchmaker: he’s promised to build a “wife” for the creature, but he can’t shake the idea that he’s just doubling the potential for chaos.

Back in Geneva, Victor tries to return to normal life, but it’s clear he’s a broken man. He can barely look his family in the eye, knowing that his silence led to Justine’s execution and that William’s death remains unresolved. His fiancée, Elizabeth, senses his turmoil and urges him to talk, but Victor remains tight-lipped, believing that sharing his secret would only bring more pain. And in true Frankenstein fashion, he distracts himself with his work.

Victor knows that in order to fulfill the creature’s request, he needs to get away from prying eyes. There’s no way he can build a second monster in his family’s home—imagine the awkward dinner conversations that would ensue. So, he convinces his father that a trip abroad will help clear his mind. This is a pretty bold excuse for someone who’s secretly planning to create a second abomination, but his father agrees, thinking that maybe a change of scenery will do Victor some good.

With Henry Clerval as his travel companion, Victor embarks on a journey across Europe, stopping first in England. Henry, always the optimist, is thrilled by the adventure. For him, it’s a chance to learn, explore, and soak in the culture. For Victor, it’s an escape from his growing sense of doom. The two friends travel through picturesque landscapes, but while Henry is basking in the beauty of nature, Victor is silently spiraling into a pit of anxiety. Every time Henry suggests they stop to admire a view, you can practically hear Victor’s internal monologue: “Yeah, yeah, nice waterfall. But I’ve got a corpse bride to build.”

Eventually, Victor and Henry part ways. Henry continues on his journey through Scotland, while Victor heads to a remote island in the Orkneys, where he plans to work on his grim task in solitude. Picture this: a tiny, desolate island with only a few scattered huts, constant winds, and endless grey skies. It’s the perfect place for someone trying to create a monster without being noticed.

Now, if you were expecting Victor to get right to work, think again. As soon as he settles into his little island lab, he’s struck by an overwhelming sense of dread. The memories of his first experiment flood back, and he starts second-guessing every decision. He spends weeks pacing, thinking, and procrastinating. It’s like he’s a student with a massive deadline, except instead of an essay, he’s building a second creature capable of wreaking havoc.

Despite his hesitation, Victor finally begins the gruesome task of assembling the female creature. But the deeper he gets into the work, the more his conscience nags at him. He’s plagued by thoughts of what might happen if the two creatures breed, creating a new race of beings that could threaten humanity. (Yes, Victor goes from zero to apocalyptic worst-case scenario in record time.) The idea of unleashing not one, but two potentially dangerous creatures terrifies him. It’s at this point that Victor realizes he may be on the verge of making an even bigger mistake than the first time.

One night, as he’s working on the creature, he’s struck by a sudden and powerful realization: he cannot go through with it. In a fit of panic and revulsion, Victor tears apart the half-finished body, destroying all the work he’s done. He refuses to play God any longer. But just as he’s disposing of the remains, he senses a presence nearby. It’s the creature, watching him from the shadows.

The creature is not happy—an understatement of the century. It had trusted Victor to fulfill his promise, to give it the chance for companionship and peace. But now, seeing Victor destroy their potential mate, the creature is consumed by rage. In a chilling confrontation, the creature vows to make Victor pay. “I will be with you on your wedding night,” it hisses before disappearing into the darkness.

Victor, already on the edge of madness, is now pushed even further into despair. He knows the creature’s threat is serious, and the thought of what it might do to him—or worse, to Elizabeth—drives him deeper into fear. He decides to leave the island immediately, abandoning the lab and all traces of his failed experiment. But the weight of what he’s done—or rather, failed to do—follows him everywhere.

Returning to the mainland, Victor’s mind is a swirling mess of guilt, terror, and regret. He reunites with Henry, but things take a tragic turn almost immediately. Victor’s fragile mental state catches up with him, and he falls ill again, collapsing under the pressure of his actions. Henry, ever the loyal friend, nurses him back to health once more, but even Henry can’t save Victor from what’s coming next.

One day, while traveling, Victor receives the devastating news: Henry Clerval has been found dead—murdered. Victor’s worst fear has come true. The creature has made good on its promise to destroy everything Victor holds dear, and now, one of his closest friends has paid the price. To make matters worse, Victor is accused of the crime. He’s arrested and thrown into prison, his mind and body pushed to the breaking point.

It’s only through the intervention of his father, who travels to clear his name, that Victor is eventually released. But by this point, Victor is a shell of the man he once was. His obsession, his ambition, his refusal to take responsibility—all of it has led to death and destruction. And now, with the creature’s ominous threat hanging over him, he knows that the final confrontation is coming.

Victor’s return to Geneva is a somber one. He tries to prepare for his wedding to Elizabeth, but the creature’s promise looms large in his mind. He knows that the wedding night will be the moment of reckoning, and though he tries to protect Elizabeth, deep down he knows that he’s powerless to stop what’s coming.

As this chapter draws to a close, we see a man consumed by his own hubris, haunted by the consequences of his actions. Victor’s descent into madness is nearly complete, and the creature’s wrath is far from over. What began as a quest for scientific glory has become a nightmare of death and despair. And with the final act fast approaching, it’s clear that neither Victor nor the creature will emerge unscathed.

Chapter Four — The Wedding Night and the Final Reckoning

As Victor Frankenstein returns to Geneva, a sense of impending doom settles over him like the weight of a storm cloud ready to burst. The creature’s chilling promise, “I will be with you on your wedding night,” echoes in his mind, casting a shadow over what should be the happiest day of his life. But, in true Victor fashion, he decides to press forward with his plans to marry Elizabeth, despite the lingering threat of destruction. Nothing says "perfect wedding" like the looming fear of a murderous monster crashing the reception.

Victor tries to keep his paranoia at bay by throwing himself into wedding preparations, but let’s face it—he’s not fooling anyone. Elizabeth can sense that something is terribly wrong, though Victor refuses to tell her the full story. Instead, he reassures her in vague, cryptic ways, all while secretly preparing for a final showdown with the creature. He arms himself, convinced that the creature will come for "him" on the night of the wedding. He envisions a dramatic confrontation—perhaps a duel under the moonlight, a chance to put an end to this nightmare once and for all. But as we’ve seen time and time again, Victor’s assumptions about his creature are disastrously wrong.

The day of the wedding arrives, and for a brief moment, everything seems calm. Victor and Elizabeth are married in a quiet ceremony, surrounded by family and friends, the tension in Victor’s mind momentarily soothed by the joy of the occasion. But as the sun sets and the newlyweds retire to a secluded villa for their honeymoon, the dread returns, more powerful than ever. Victor is on high alert, his nerves frayed, his senses tingling with fear. He tells Elizabeth to go to bed, promising to join her soon. Little does he know, the real danger is far closer than he realizes.

Victor patrols the house, weapon in hand, ready to confront the creature. But as the hours pass, nothing happens. The silence stretches on, and Victor begins to wonder if perhaps the creature’s threat was an empty one. Maybe, just maybe, he’ll escape this night unscathed. Then comes the scream.

Victor rushes back to the bedroom, his heart pounding in his chest. The door swings open to reveal the scene he fears most: Elizabeth, his beloved wife, lies lifeless on the bed. The creature has struck—not at him, as Victor expected, but at the person he loved most. The ultimate act of revenge. The sight of Elizabeth’s body paralyzes Victor with grief and horror. The promise the creature made was not for his death but for the destruction of his happiness. It’s a cruel, calculated move, and it leaves Victor shattered.

As the realization of what has happened sets in, Victor’s mind unravels completely. He chases after the creature, filled with a rage so intense it’s almost blinding. But the creature is long gone, leaving Victor alone with his grief and guilt. His wife, his brother, his best friend—everyone he loved has been taken from him, and he knows he is the reason. His ambition, his pride, his decision to play God—these are the forces that have destroyed his life.

In the days that follow, Victor buries Elizabeth and confesses the full story to his father. The weight of his secrets and the horrors they have wrought prove too much for his father’s heart to bear. In another cruel twist of fate, Alphonse Frankenstein dies, leaving Victor completely and utterly alone. The last vestiges of his family, his life, and his sanity crumble before his eyes.

At this point, Victor’s guilt transforms into a single-minded obsession with revenge. He vows to hunt the creature to the ends of the earth if necessary, to stop it before it can hurt anyone else. The once-brilliant scientist who sought to unlock the secrets of life now becomes a man consumed by the pursuit of death. His pursuit of the creature takes him across Europe and into the frozen wilderness of the Arctic. It’s a bleak, desolate landscape, fitting for the final leg of Victor’s journey—a land where life itself struggles to survive, mirroring the state of Victor’s soul.

Victor’s health deteriorates as the chase drags on. He’s no longer the confident, ambitious young man we met in the early chapters. Instead, he’s a shell of his former self, driven forward only by the desire for revenge. The creature, always one step ahead, leaves taunting clues for Victor as if enjoying the game. It becomes clear that the creature is deliberately prolonging the hunt, relishing Victor’s suffering.

Eventually, Victor finds himself stranded in the Arctic, on the verge of death. He’s rescued by a ship's captain named Robert Walton, whose own expedition has been interrupted by the sight of a strange, broken man wandering the icy wasteland. Walton, fascinated by Victor’s story, listens as Victor recounts the entire tragic tale—the creation, the abandonment, the deaths, the unrelenting guilt. By this point, Victor knows he is near the end of his life, but he still clings to one final hope: that Walton will take up the pursuit and kill the creature if he dies before he can finish the job.

Despite his desperate condition, Victor's thirst for revenge has not dulled. He warns Walton about the dangers of unchecked ambition, but his words come across as the bitter reflections of a man who has lost everything. Victor’s advice is tainted by his own failures, and his own inability to see the consequences of his actions until it is too late.

In the final days of his life, Victor’s health continues to decline. He dies aboard Walton’s ship, still tormented by the creature he created and the lives it took. It’s a bleak and tragic end for a man who sought to conquer death but instead became its victim.

After Victor’s death, the creature appears one last time. It boards the ship, standing over Victor’s body with a strange mixture of sadness and triumph. The creature, now fully aware of the destruction it has caused, speaks to Walton with a sense of melancholy. It acknowledges the pain it has inflicted, but also the pain it has endured. The creature reveals that its plan was always to die after Victor, to escape the misery of its existence and put an end to the suffering for both of them.

With that, the creature disappears into the icy wilderness, leaving Walton and the crew with nothing but the haunting story of a man who dared to defy the natural order of life. The tale of Victor Frankenstein and his creature serves as a warning—a reminder that ambition without responsibility and knowledge without wisdom can lead to untold destruction.

And so, the tragic tale of "Frankenstein" comes to its bitter conclusion, with both creator and creation meeting their inevitable ends. What began as a quest for greatness ends in ruin, and the questions raised by Victor’s experiment linger long after the final page: What does it mean to create life? And what responsibilities come with wielding such power?

In the end, neither Victor nor the creature can escape the consequences of their choices, and their fates are forever entwined in a story of ambition, revenge, and ultimately, loss.

Final Words

As we close the final pages of Frankenstein, the story of Victor and his creature lingers with us, not just as a gothic horror tale, but as a deeply human narrative about ambition, responsibility, and the consequences of pushing the boundaries of knowledge. Even though Mary Shelley wrote this novel in 1818, its core themes remain startlingly relevant, especially in today’s world, where technology, artificial intelligence, and scientific breakthroughs challenge us to consider the ethical implications of our own creations.

At its heart, Frankenstein is a story about the dangers of unchecked ambition. Victor Frankenstein, driven by his desire to unlock the mysteries of life, abandons his responsibility as a creator, leading to a chain of tragic events. His failure to consider the consequences of his actions—creating life without thinking about how to nurture it—is a warning that resonates strongly in the 21st century. As we develop technologies that could radically alter life—whether through AI, genetic engineering, or automation—Shelley’s novel serves as a cautionary tale about the need for foresight, responsibility, and humility.

The central question Frankenstein asks is not just about science, but about what it means to be human. Victor’s creature is, at its core, a symbol of alienation and the search for identity. Born into a world that immediately rejects him, the creature’s journey mirrors the experience of anyone who has felt cast aside or misunderstood by society. In today’s digital age, where people are often more connected by technology than by direct human interaction, the creature’s story of loneliness and rejection feels all too familiar. His plea for companionship, for acceptance, is universal—and it reminds us that, even in an era dominated by technology, the need for human connection remains as strong as ever.

In fact, Frankenstein speaks to a broader cultural anxiety that is incredibly relevant today: the fear of what our creations might become. Just as Victor lost control of his creation, modern society grapples with similar fears regarding artificial intelligence. AI has the potential to revolutionize the world, but it also raises difficult questions. What happens if our creations evolve beyond our understanding or control? How do we ensure that what we build doesn’t outgrow our capacity to manage it? These are questions Frankenstein explored in the context of 19th-century science, but they echo powerfully in our modern world, where rapid technological progress sometimes outpaces ethical considerations.

Books like Frankenstein often get compared to other cautionary tales of scientific overreach. Novels such as Brave New World by Aldous Huxley or 1984 by George Orwell deal with dystopian futures shaped by human ambition and the misuse of power, just as Frankenstein does with its tragic portrait of creation gone awry. In these stories, technology, science, and power all offer promises of a better world but often lead to unforeseen and destructive consequences when not tempered with moral responsibility.

But while Shelley’s story is rooted in the anxieties of the Industrial Revolution, it has aged remarkably well because its themes touch on something timeless—human nature. Technology may change, and society may evolve, but the complexities of human ambition, morality, and emotion remain constant. Victor’s fatal flaw is not merely his scientific curiosity; it’s his refusal to accept the ethical responsibilities that come with wielding such power. Similarly, the creature’s descent into violence is not because it is inherently evil, but because it is denied the basic human experiences of love, belonging, and compassion. These themes remind us that, no matter how advanced our technologies become, the need to understand and care for one another never goes away.

In the 21st century, where artificial intelligence and automation are rapidly reshaping industries, culture, and human interaction, Frankenstein stands as a reminder that progress without reflection is dangerous. It invites readers to think deeply about the responsibilities that come with creation—whether we’re building robots, genetic sequences, or entire digital landscapes. At the same time, it teaches us that the pursuit of knowledge is not inherently bad; rather, it is how we handle the power that knowledge brings that truly matters.

To a modern reader, Frankenstein is more than just a classic gothic novel. It’s a story that bridges the past and the future, a narrative that connects the scientific questions of the 19th century to the ethical dilemmas of the 21st. As we stand on the edge of new technological revolutions, from AI to biotech, Mary Shelley’s tale remains a potent reminder that no matter how far science may take us, we must never forget our humanity along the way.

In the end, the relevance of Frankenstein lies not just in its commentary on science and ethics, but in its exploration of what happens when we lose touch with the very values that make us human—compassion, empathy, and responsibility. These are the threads that connect Victor Frankenstein’s tragic tale to our modern world, and they ensure that this novel will continue to resonate with readers for generations to come.
