Prologue. The Funeral Where the
Deceased Is Reading
Imagine a conference about the future of knowledge. Onstage, a man
announces the death of the book. Behind him, an enormous screen
confirms the diagnosis with an animation. The audience photographs
the screen, someone livestreams, and an automated service is already
producing the presentation’s seven essential ideas. In the third row, a
woman is reading a book. Not in protest. The conference simply star‐
ted late, and her novel had reached a good part.
The scene is invented, but the conflict deserves to be taken seriously.
Not because a paper object needs a lawyer. Books do not suffer when
ignored. People, however, can lose something without noticing, espe‐
cially when the lost thing resembles not information but a particular
relationship with time.
We have spectacular explanations, conversations with specialists, in‐
stantly accessible articles, collaborative encyclopedias, and systems
answering questions about a text. Some are extraordinary. Disdaining
them to save the book’s prestige would be like defending walking by
denying ambulances exist. The question is not whether we must
choose one technology. It is what we want to happen to us when we
learn, imagine, or try to understand a life different from our own.
If we want a page number, a search engine is preferable to an after‐
noon leafing through pages. If we want to see how a complicated
movement is performed, a filmed demonstration can be decisive. If
we want to follow how an argument withstands its own objections,
the speed at which we receive the conclusion is no longer the sole cri‐
terion. And if we want to experience a novel, its summary can be as
correct and useless as describing a dinner: “Food was consumed, after
which the participants left.”
This book defends reading, but does not start from the idea that every
volume is worth more than every film. Some books are articles per‐
suaded, through editorial means, to put on weight. Some podcasts ac‐
5


complish in an hour what an author failed to do in three hundred
pages. Scientific papers a few pages long can change a field, while
hefty treatises change only the shelf. Length is not a unit of truth.
The thesis we will test is more precise: the book offers a valuable com‐
bination of extensive development, controllable pace, stability, and
encounter with a perspective that does not constantly rearrange itself
to please us. None of these properties belongs exclusively to it. Togeth‐
er, they can create an experience difficult to replace by simply accu‐
mulating quick answers.
We will separate what research tells us from what we can argue and
what we merely imagine about the future. Studies will not serve as a
chorus of people in lab coats approving every literary preference.
Sometimes they will contradict book lovers’ prejudices. At other times,
they will temper the technology industry’s promises. Where evidence
is limited, it will remain limited, even if a spectacular conclusion
would sell the chapter better.
At the end, we will not organize a solemn return to the last century.
We will attempt something harder: preserving a freedom we can lose
precisely while surrounded by options. The freedom to stay with a
question long enough for the answer to be understood, not merely re‐
cognized. And the equally important freedom to close a bad book
without feeling we have betrayed civilization.

1. What Are We Defending When
We Defend a Book?
The Object, the Form, and the Habit
Let us place a printed novel on the table, the same novel on an e-read‐
er, its audio recording, and an accessible Braille edition. Which is the
real book? The question seems innocent until someone starts distrib‐
uting certificates of authenticity. If we answer “only the first”, we de‐
fend paper, not the work. If we answer “all are exactly the same ex‐
perience”, we ignore real differences in pace, orientation, vocal inter‐
pretation, and access.
We need three distinctions. The medium is where and how we en‐
counter the text: paper, screen, sound, tactile relief. The form is the
work’s organization: a novel, monograph, textbook, encyclopedia, po‐
etry collection. The practice is what we do: read continuously, look up
a term, listen, compare passages, discuss, or consult only the conclu‐
sion. The same book can host very different practices. An attentive
reader and a decoration buyer may own exactly the same hardcover
edition.
There is a fourth level: the commercial and social environment. A text
on a dedicated device without interruptions does not have the same
neighbors as a text opened among messages and recommendations.
The difference is not merely ink versus pixels, but two arrangements
of possibilities. The book in our hand does not simultaneously invite
us to check whether a former classmate has become a millionaire. The
phone can host both the novel and the classmate. Sometimes we
choose the latter, though the former has better-developed characters.
Here we will use “book” for a work of some extent, organized and re‐
cognizable as a whole, without claiming a perfect boundary exists. A
very short novella and a very long essay can cross the border without
passports. An encyclopedia is a book without requesting cover-to-cov‐
er reading. A novel can be fragmentary. Precisely for this reason, we
must not turn one property of good books into a definition excluding
half the library.
7


What we call reading a book is sometimes an encounter with an ex‐
tensive construction. Authors ask us to remember something before
showing why it matters. A detail becomes important later. A claim is
complicated by an exception. A character we judged too quickly gains
a history. Value lies not only in individual pieces but in relations that
become visible through the journey.
This advantage has a reverse side. Organization can manipulate. Au‐
thors decide what we learn, in what order, and what remains outside
the frame. An argumentative book can lead readers toward a conclu‐
sion by hiding alternatives precisely when they would matter. A frag‐
mented feed can produce disorder; a coherent book can produce false
order. Coherence is a quality of construction, not a certificate of truth.
The difference between a stable work and a response adapted on de‐
mand matters here. A book can be criticized for something it said on
the previous page. The text remains, available to another reader. An
interactive system can correct, clarify, and personalize, which is use‐
ful, but must also preserve the ability to see the initial formulation.
Otherwise, the ideal interlocutor becomes impossible to confront:
whenever you contradict it, it was just about to say something else.
We must not, however, confuse stability with freezing. A technology
textbook needs new editions. A history book can be revised on docu‐
mentary evidence. A digital work can include corrections. What de‐
serves preservation is knowing which version we read and which
claims changed. A living book must not be an amnesiac book.
Defending the book therefore begins with a renunciation: we will not
defend every feature of the traditional object. Some people enjoy pa‐
per’s smell, but it is not an epistemology. We will defend capacities:
following something extensive, returning, confronting, temporarily
inhabiting an intellectual or artistic construction. Sometimes paper
supports these capacities excellently. Sometimes an accessible digital
format makes them possible for someone otherwise excluded from
reading.

An Invention Suspect from the Start
In Phaedrus, Plato stages a memorable objection to writing: it might
offer the appearance of knowledge without understanding and en‐
courage reliance on external signs for memory. Written text has an‐
other defect: asked something, it cannot respond appropriately to its
interlocutor. This is a philosophical critique formulated in a dialogue,
not an experiment on the brain. The irony remains remarkable: we
know it because it was written. [1]
It would be easy to laugh at the ancient suspicion. But a fear’s age
does not prove every version false. Nor does its repetition prove it
true. The relevant question is which function a new tool moves out‐
side the person and which capacities it cultivates or leaves unprac‐
ticed. A notebook can free memory for a harder problem. It can also
become where we store things we never process again.
In 1945, in “As We May Think”, Vannevar Bush imagined an informa‐
tion-working device, the memex, through which researchers could
build associative trails between documents. He dreamed not merely of
more cards in a cabinet, but a more flexible relationship with the
archive. This vision offers a useful counterpoint to nostalgia for ex‐
clusively linear reading: connections between texts can be a form of
thought, not merely escape from it. [5]
At the other pole, Nicholas Carr’s 2008 essay described difficulty sus‐
taining concentrated reading and asked how browsing habits shape
us. His testimony formulated an important cultural problem; it is not,
alone, evidence that the internet produces the same effect on every‐
one. [2]
In Reader, Come Home, Maryanne Wolf proposes preserving deep
reading alongside developing digital competences, not simply evacu‐
ating screens. A reader deliberately able to use different reading
modes seems a more fruitful idea to me than two tribes, one with a
library and the other with a power bank. It is an educational and cul‐
tural orientation, not a promise that one formula resolves every diffi‐
culty. [3]
9


These interlocutors help separate two frequently mixed questions.
First: which tools give us access to information? Second: what activity
do we undertake once we have it? We can radically improve the first
situation while neglecting the second. A perfect library does not guar‐
antee an attentive reader. Nor can an attentive reader compensate for
an inaccessible library.
An objection to the rhetoric of rescue follows. When someone says
“people no longer read”, we must ask what they call reading, whom
they observe, and which period they compare with. We may read
many messages and few books. We may buy books without finishing
them. A minority may read extensively while reading grows rarer
among everyone else. These situations are not equivalent and cannot
be repaired by the same sermon.
We need not embellish the past until every imaginary inhabitant has a
novel in their pocket. A cultural ideal remains valuable even if never
equally distributed. We seek not a perfect era, but opportunities that
should exist now: public libraries, good teachers, accessible formats.
These are more convincing arguments for books than contempt to‐
ward those without reading time.
Conservatives assume everything we lose was good; enthusiasts as‐
sume everything replaceable deserves replacing. But what deserves
preserving even if it is not the fastest solution? Not “everything”. Nor
only what the stopwatch of a commercial demonstration approves.

2. Reading Is Not Downloading
Files
What Remains When You Have Forgotten Almost
Everything?
A troubling objection to reading goes like this: if after a few years we
can no longer reproduce a book’s contents, would reading the sum‐
mary not have been more efficient? The objection is forceful because
it attacks a familiar experience. We recognize the title, perhaps a
scene, sometimes only that we enjoyed it. Our inner libraries are not
well-indexed repositories. Sometimes they are buildings where we
know we left something important but can no longer find the stairs.
The question nevertheless smuggles in a very narrow criterion: read‐
ing’s value supposedly equals the number of sentences retrievable
later. For some purposes, precise retention is essential. If studying a
procedure, declaring the manual changed you as a person does not
help much. For other purposes, how you learned to distinguish a prob‐
lem, formulate a question, or recognize an alternative also matters.
These must be assessed through what you can do, not merely recite.
Imagine two readers of an essay on responsibility. The first retains the
central definition. The second cannot reproduce it but notices during
a dispute that people confuse intention, control, and consequences.
We cannot automatically infer the second benefited more: the distinc‐
tion may come from another source. The example nevertheless shows
why a reproduction test does not exhaust reading’s possible values.
Understanding must also be sought in use.
The opposite trap also exists: declaring every forgotten book mysteri‐
ously transformed us. This is unverifiable consolation. If we can
identify no new question, nuance, pleasure, or competence, we need
not invent an invisible benefit. Sometimes we read inattentively.
Sometimes the book was poor. Sometimes we rested, which is suffi‐
cient without requesting university certification.
11


Research on the “illusion of explanatory depth”, associated with Le‐
onid Rozenblit and Frank Keil, shows that people can overestimate
their understanding of complex mechanisms. Believing you know an
explanation and being able to construct it are different things. The
result concerns not exclusively books, nor does it prove reading pro‐
duces this illusion. It nevertheless offers a useful question for any me‐
dium: can I explain without constantly relying on the formulation I
just encountered? [10]
A fluent book can hide this distance. The author makes elegant trans‐
itions, the example arrives exactly on time, and the conclusion seems
inevitable. Readers understand every sentence yet may be unable to
reconstruct the route. An excellent video and a patient chatbot can
produce the same problem. Ease of following is not a sufficient test of
mastery. Nor is discomfort proof of depth: sometimes the author
simply explains badly.
For this reason, knowledge transfer is an incomplete metaphor. When
copying a file, the destination receives the same data structure. In
learning, readers interpret through prior knowledge, reconstruct rela‐
tions, and may misunderstand. A sentence clear to a specialist may be
almost empty to a beginner. The same sentence, read after a year’s
practice, can suddenly become dense. The text has not changed; the
encounter’s conditions have.
The good question is therefore not only “how much content did I con‐
sume?” but “what new relationship can I sustain between things I
know?” A book can support this construction through examples, re‐
turns, and development. It can also sabotage it through ornamental
repetition. We should demand not volume from authors but necessity
of the journey: why did we need this page to reach the next?
The Right to Slow Down and the Obligation to
Participate
Imagine two versions of the same lesson. In the first, explanation con‐
tinues regardless of where you got lost. In the second, you can stop, re‐
turn, and spend five minutes with one passage. The second offers a
control advantage. But control is not its use. Someone can turn pages
as quickly as another skips a clip. Pages cannot force anyone to think.
12


Readers nevertheless have a discreet freedom: a sentence’s duration is
not fixed by someone else’s interpretation. They can move quickly
through the familiar and slow down at difficulty. Audiobooks and
films can offer pause, replay, and speed changes; the difference must
not be absolutized. Visible text’s particularity is that several formula‐
tions can remain simultaneously available for comparison, and re‐
turning does not always require traversing them in time.
In educational research, John Dunlosky and colleagues’ broad syn‐
thesis assigned high utility to distributed practice and active know‐
ledge testing. Mere highlighting and rereading received more modest
assessments as general strategies. The conclusion neither prohibits lit‐
erary rereading nor guarantees any test’s success: it concerns studied
learning techniques, tasks, and conditions. The relevant lesson here is
that reader activity can matter more than a medium’s prestige. [11]
We can translate this idea into an ordinary gesture. After a difficult
section, briefly close the text and formulate the problem in your own
words. Do not organize an examination against yourself. Simply try to
see where the explanation breaks. On returning, you seek not merely
a beautiful sentence but the exact point of misunderstanding. The
book becomes an interlocutor although it does not speak. Your ques‐
tion changes the reading.
For a novel, participation may look different. Every chapter need not
become a set of worksheets. Noticing we judged a character and ask‐
ing what we based it on may suffice. Or rereading the beginning after
knowing the ending. Relations between passages change without the
passages changing. Such an experience is poorly described as extract‐
ing a message. It is reconfiguring an interpretation.
Here also lies the difference between a useful summary and one falsi‐
fying the purpose. If choosing among ten volumes, summaries can
save time. If checking whether a chapter contains an idea, a summary
is a map. But a map does not always substitute for the experience you
set out for. No one considers a list of a meeting’s moments equivalent
to the meeting, though it may reproduce every decision.
It does not follow that difficulty must be worshipped. An obscure
demonstration can be improved. A term can be explained without los‐
ing the idea. Readers asking for clarity do not necessarily ask for su‐
13


perficiality. The useful distinction is between a problem’s difficulty
and difficulty produced by presentation. The first may require time.
The second often requires an editor.
We can call “fruitful effort” the work needed to construct something
not yet present in our understanding. “Sterile effort” is work imposed
by poor organization, an unfindable reference, or functionless jargon.
A good book reduces the second without promising the disappearance
of the first. A good digital tool should be judged by the same rule.
In this sense, reading is not efficiency’s opposite. It is an activity in
which efficiency must be correctly defined. If the objective is travers‐
ing two hundred pages as quickly as possible, bypassing them is the
perfect solution. If the objective is acquiring a particular understand‐
ing, the saved page may be either a gain or precisely the exercise we
needed. Minute-counting alone cannot decide.

3. Paper Is Not Sacred. Nor Is the
Screen Neutral
What Comparisons Say and What They Do Not
One reader opens a printed book and says they concentrate better. An‐
other enlarges letters on a device and says only now can they read
comfortably. Both may accurately describe their experience. Dispute
begins when personal experience becomes a rule for everyone. Paper
then acquires almost spiritual properties, while the screen becomes
either demon or inevitable progress. Research offers a less dramatic
and therefore more useful picture.
Pablo Delgado and colleagues’ 2018 meta-analysis found a small aver‐
age paper advantage for text comprehension. It was more pro‐
nounced under time limits and for informational or mixed texts; it
was not established in the same way for exclusively narrative texts.
The analyzed studies covered 2000–2017. These limits matter: the res‐
ult is not a verdict on every current device and every novel. [6]
Another meta-analysis, published by Virginia Clinton in 2019, also
identified a small average paper advantage, especially for expository
texts, with no clear difference for narratives. It also analyzed readers’
assessments of their own comprehension. The two syntheses should
not be treated as completely independent experiments because in‐
cluded literature may overlap. Together, they justify caution in medi‐
um choice, not the slogan that screens cancel thought. [7]
“Small” does not mean unimportant. A modest advantage may matter
in a difficult or repeated situation. But “average” does not mean inev‐
itable. The aggregate result does not tell you that a particular person
must abandon the tool enabling reading. Above all, comparing media
does not always compare all possible versions. Navigation, page size,
annotation options, and presentation can vary.
An example from research on children makes the difference visible.
May Irene Furenes, Natalia Kucirkova, and Adriana Bus’s meta-analys‐
is examined 39 studies involving children aged one to eight. Mere di‐
15


gitization was associated with weaker comprehension than paper, but
story-relevant digital enhancements could reverse the advantage. An
embedded dictionary could help vocabulary without simultaneously
helping story comprehension. Not every useful feature serves every
objective. [8]
This is a design lesson, not an invitation to add gimmicks. Imagine a
book about a boat caught in a current. A discreet animation showing
water direction may clarify the action. A game collecting coins in the
page’s corner may entertain, but does not explain why the boat cannot
reach shore. Interactivity needs a complement: interactive with what,
and for what?
Paper has constraints that can be advantageous. An ordinary copy has
stable spatial organization and does not request updates before
chapter four. But not every constraint is a virtue. Letters do not en‐
large on command, weight accumulates in luggage, and searching an
expression may take time. A well-made digital edition solves real
problems. It does not become less a book because it fits with a thou‐
sand others on one device.
Reasonable judgment is situated. For a dense text you compare and
annotate, try the medium in which you work better, not the one ex‐
pressing your preferred cultural tribe. For readers needing large print
or speech playback, access is no secondary bonus. For an evening nov‐
el, comfort may outweigh an average difference observed in other
tasks.
A mature choice says neither “always paper” nor “inevitably digital”.
It asks: what must this person do with this text under these condi‐
tions? The question is less suitable for T-shirts, but more suitable for
schools, libraries, and real life.
Listening Is Not Cultural Fraud
There is a little imaginary reading police asking, when someone dis‐
cusses a book: “Did you read it or just listen?” The emphasis falls on
“just”, as though literature had been smuggled into the head through
the ear. For access to the work, this distinction justifies no moral hier‐
archy. For certain learning tasks, it nevertheless deserves precise ex‐
amination.
16


Virginia Clinton-Lisell’s 2022 meta-analysis, based on 46 studies, found
no reliable overall difference between comprehension through read‐
ing and listening. Reading advantages did emerge under some condi‐
tions, including reader-controlled pacing and certain inferential tasks.
Absence of an overall difference does not mean all experiences are
identical; it means “listening is inevitably inferior” is unsupported by
this synthesis. [9]
An audiobook preserves the text but adds vocal interpretation. Pause,
emphasis, and timbre can open meanings or close them too quickly.
Reading silently, a line’s ambiguity may remain available. A voice
must say it in a particular way. This is not a deficiency but an artistic
feature. The interpretation can be extraordinary, just as one’s own
reading can be monotonous and inattentive.
Listening also changes the cost of returning. In visible text, we can
compare two formulations by looking. In sound, we must retrieve
them successively or have a transcript. An audiobook with well-
marked chapters, bookmarks, and synchronized text can reduce the
difficulty. Edition quality therefore becomes part of intellectual exper‐
ience, not merely a technical detail.
Focused listening must also be separated from listening alongside
something else. Comparing attentive reading in a quiet room with an
audiobook half-heard while solving three problems, then blaming the
ear, is unfair. Equally unfair would be comparing excellent audio in‐
terpretation with poorly scanned text and declaring writing obsolete.
Conditions are not the comparison’s scenery; they are part of it.
For someone with visual difficulties, someone fatigued by visual read‐
ing, or someone finding listening time where there is no time to sit
with a volume, audio can open an otherwise inaccessible work. Re‐
quiring that person to prove their contact with literature authentic is
a distinctly unliterary form of snobbery.
At the same time, accessing a story and learning text decoding are dif‐
ferent objectives. A child can listen to and enjoy a complex story; this
does not automatically replace exercises through which written signs
are learned. The distinction does not diminish listening. It simply pre‐
vents using a real benefit as the answer to another question.
17


We can imagine reading moving naturally between media: text in the
morning, audio during a walk, returning to two pages at night. What
matters is preserving orientation and contact with the work. Readers
lose no purity by pressing play. There are only different purposes and
means serving them better or worse.
The book may survive precisely by refusing to identify with one sens‐
ory route. Defending its experience means keeping paths to the work
open, not guarding an entrance and congratulating only those able to
climb the same stairs.

4. There Is No Single Future for
All Books
A Novel Is Not a Box of Information
Suppose someone constructs the perfect novel summary. It misses no
important event, explains character relations, and identifies themes. It
even tells us what the upstairs window symbolizes, though we
thought it was a window. Have we received everything? Only if the
work’s purpose was transporting a list of events and some interpreta‐
tions.
But a story also distributes knowledge over time. Learning immedi‐
ately that a character lies is not the same as discovering it after trust‐
ing them. Receiving the verdict on a choice is not the same as passing
through conditions making it attractive, then regrettable. A summary
can conserve the result while eliminating precisely the experience of
changing judgment.
This difference is not exclusively literary. Film also constructs revela‐
tions, and conversation can gradually change participants’ under‐
standing. The novel’s particularity lies in its resources: extensive
verbal development, controlled access to perspectives, the possibility
of returning, and space readers complete. Not every reader imagines
scenes the same way, and imagination must not be reduced to an in‐
ner cinema. We can also follow rhythms, relations, tensions, and
meanings difficult to draw.
A narrator can say something without understanding its implications.
Readers are then called to notice the distance between account and
situation. An apparently confident voice can become suspect. A single
expression can change a scene’s value. Reducing the work to “what
happens” eliminates the question of how we are asked to believe what
happens.
Poetry makes the objection even clearer. A poem can be very short
and profound precisely because verbal form is not detachable pack‐
aging. A clip’s having the same duration proves nothing against it. Nor
19


does it prove every poem good. It merely shows that long versus short
misses part of the problem. Sometimes what must be preserved is not
extent, but a formulation’s precision and the space it opens.
Comics also complicate the convenient division between book and im‐
age. Readers must construct connections between panels, follow the
relationship between dialogue and drawing, and interpret what is
missing between moments. Illustrated books and art books pose other
problems: size, color, and arrangement may participate in meaning. A
file preserving every word may not preserve the whole work.
Literary reading need not be justified exclusively by benefits pro‐
duced outside it. “What use is the novel to you?” can receive good an‐
swers, but sometimes hides the assumption that delight in encounter‐
ing form is insufficient. We do not demand every walk produce a pro‐
ject. A poem need not improve our professional profile to deserve
time.
This does not mean immunity to criticism. A work may be boring,
pointlessly confusing, stereotyped, or morally repugnant. Readers
may decide it is not worth continuing. Defending literary experience
is not a captivity contract. On the contrary, if we ask time and atten‐
tion for a book, we must accept readers’ right to ask why.
In a summary market, literature has a strong defense: its product is
not always identical to its summarizable message. But this defense
guarantees no audience. An experience can be valuable and rarely
practiced. The novel’s distinctive offering does not mean people auto‐
matically have the time, energy, and habits to receive it. Here the
problem moves from aesthetics of form to life’s conditions.
The novel’s future therefore depends not only on good stories existing.
It also depends on people being able to stay with them without feeling
every hour must immediately become an outcome. Literature is not
utility’s opposite. It is one practice allowing us to discuss what we con‐
sider useful and who has the power to decide.

A Textbook Must Work, Not Be Worshipped
A textbook does not have a novel’s obligations. If I want to learn a tool,
I must be able to do something after reading. I owe the author no fi‐
delity to an order obstructing the explanation. A good textbook can
have a recommended beginner route and alternative entry points for
those already familiar with some material. Selective consultation is
not disrespect. It may be precisely its intended use.
A popularization book has a different task: turning a field into a space
readers can navigate without becoming specialists overnight. Its
danger is not merely oversimplification but an excessively good story.
When every study seems to lead to the same conclusion, we should
check whether the field is truly so peaceful or the author sent contra‐
dictions on holiday.
A monograph can justify its length through developing a thesis, ex‐
amining sources, and addressing objections. A paper can state a res‐
ult; a book can show what makes it important within a broader con‐
figuration. But excess exists here too. Not every idea needs three hun‐
dred pages. Sometimes editorial form imposes a volume the argument
cannot support. Readers end up paying, in money and time, the rent
between two observations.
Reference books are more exposed to selective replacement. For an
updated definition, a well-maintained database may be preferable to
an old volume. For a stable synthesis, a clean edition, and justified se‐
lection, the book may remain convenient. We need not save slow
searching to preserve editorial order’s benefit. We can want both rap‐
id access and intelligible structure.
Practical books add the problem of contact with the world. A recipe
must be cooked. A musical exercise must be tried. An explanation of
negotiation does not replace every negotiation experience. For ges‐
tures, feedback, and error correction, demonstration, a teacher, or as‐
sisted practice may matter more than text. A book can prepare action,
organize experience, and permit return; it must not pretend to have
acted in the reader’s place.
21


There are also books whose value lies in selection: an anthology, atlas,
annotated document collection. They do not necessarily produce new
information but establish meaningful neighborhoods. Selection is in‐
terpretation. Precisely for this reason, we must be able to ask what
was excluded, by what criterion, and with what effect. An anthology is
not the world in miniature, but a proposal about what deserves to be
seen together.
For books on rapidly changing fields, date and version are compon‐
ents of meaning. A claim correct at publication can become useless or
false. The solution is not abandoning every synthesis, but separating
relatively durable principles from changing details. A programming
book can explain general ideas while maintained documentation
provides a tool’s current behavior. One builds orientation; the other
checks the terrain.
In How We Read Now, Naomi Baron treats choosing print, screen, or
audio as a strategic decision connected to reading purposes. This ori‐
entation also matters across genres: we ask not which format is ab‐
stractly superior, but which combination supports reader activity. [4]
Consequently, some books will have good reasons to transform radic‐
ally. Others will have good reasons to remain almost unchanged. A
textbook may gain from interactive exercises and rapid corrections. A
poem may lose if every word activates an explanatory window. A nov‐
el can benefit from an accessible edition without needing a dash‐
board. Innovation appropriate to one can be elegant vandalism to an‐
other.
The question is not whether we want progress. It is where we meas‐
ure it. If an app has ten new features but readers understand less and
find the thread harder to follow, progress belongs to the product
sheet. The book, like every cultural tool, must be judged by what it
makes possible, not how modern it looks in a presentation.

5. Film Has a Point. Sometimes
the Book Loses
Eight Minutes That Save Eighty Pages
Let us try explaining a complicated knot using words alone. It is pos‐
sible. We can number strands, describe positions, and introduce
terms. After three pages, readers might understand. They might also
use the string to tie up the manual and send it back to the author. A
filmed demonstration with a good angle and replay can clarify the
movement much more directly.
This example does not humiliate the book. It recognizes that repres‐
entations have different resources. Movement, spatial change, vocal
timbre, or hand coordination are often easier to show than exhaust‐
ively translate into sentences. Text can add criteria, exceptions, and
explanations. Images can make visible what text asks us to construct
ourselves.
A synthesis by Michael Noetel and colleagues, published in 2021, ana‐
lyzed 105 randomized studies on video use in higher education. It
found average benefits when video replaced other teaching forms
and, more strongly, when added. Comparisons varied, and the authors
discuss bias risks. The result does not mean “film beats books”, but
contradicts the idea that educational video is by definition intellectu‐
ally inferior. [12]
An educational film can be well organized, anticipate errors, and slow
at a difficult point. A textbook can do the same differently. Comparis‐
ons must retain as many relevant conditions as possible: content
offered, participant time, what can be controlled, and what is reques‐
ted afterward. If video contains an excellent demonstration while text
contains only a definition, the difference does not simply measure me‐
dium.
Likewise, “learning” can mean very different things. Recognizing a
component, executing a procedure, explaining a mechanism, and solv‐
ing a new situation are different outcomes. Spectacular material can
23


provide initial orientation without enough precision for work. Diffi‐
cult text can contain necessary precision without allowing orienta‐
tion. Used together, they can avoid the false choice.
Imagine a reader trying to understand an eclipse. An animation can
show the bodies’ configuration. Text can fix conditions and distinguish
related phenomena. A drawing made without a model can show what
the reader understood. Conversation can expose confusions. Declar‐
ing one of these gestures the sole legitimate route would be strange, as
though learning were a competition requiring all other tools’ elimina‐
tion.
The book has a possible advantage here: density, precise formulation,
and targeted consultation. Film has another: showing transformation
without decomposing it into a difficult verbal sequence. Each can also
have the corresponding flaw. Density can become a wall. Fluidity can
hide skipping an unexplained step. “Have I understood?” must there‐
fore be followed by “what can I now do with this understanding?”
A healthy approach to mixed material would assign each medium its
suitable task. Text should not endlessly describe movement obvious in
a demonstration. Film should not hold a dense paragraph onscreen
long enough for a presenter to recite but too briefly for us to examine.
Simulation should let us change a relevant condition, not merely press
a button lighting up an animation.
Defending the book does not mean making it win every event. A cul‐
tural tool becomes more credible when we know its limits. Perhaps
one of a book’s best actions is telling us, at the right moment: here a
demonstration is worth seeing. Authority recognizing a better tool for
a task does not diminish; it becomes useful.
Cinema Is Not Literature with Comfortable Seats
Comparing a novel with its adaptation sometimes produces a verdict
before viewing: “The book is better.” It may be true in that case. But
the formula becomes suspect when it means film should reproduce
every novelistic advantage without using its own form’s advantages.
An adaptation faithful to every sentence might be a poor film pre‐
cisely because of its fidelity.
24


Cinema works with duration, editing, framing, gestures, sounds, and
absences. It can show simultaneously what a sentence must unfold
successively. It can place an expression in conflict with a line. It can
leave an object in the background without announcing its importance.
Text has other freedoms: moving through an idea without finding a
visual equivalent and explicitly saying what a character could not ex‐
press.
We need not choose which art is “deeper”. We must see what each
does with its material. A film can be superficial. A novel can be super‐
ficial. A film can require intense interpretation while a novel delivers
prepackaged formulas. Readers do not automatically receive intellec‐
tual activity because they see letters, nor do viewers automatically be‐
come passive because they see images.
The pace imposed by a screening and that of reading nevertheless dif‐
fer. In a cinema, shared experience has a duration viewers do not al‐
ter after every shot. This limit may be part of film’s power. Reading
permits other returns and interruptions. At home, technology can
bring practices closer through pause and replay. No configuration
should be declared universally superior; each produces possibilities
and sacrifices.
Documentary adds a distinct epistemic problem. Filmed images can
be highly convincing, but framing, sequence order, and commentary
construct an interpretation. The same happens through other means
in nonfiction. A camera does not eliminate selection, just as a biblio‐
graphy does not eliminate bias. Readers or viewers need to distin‐
guish a document from conclusions based on it.
Suppose we see three interviews with people dissatisfied with an insti‐
tution. They may recount real experiences. Without further informa‐
tion, this does not establish how widespread the experience is or its
causes. Film can make us feel the problem; analysis must help us un‐
derstand its extent. A book can commit the same error by selecting
three testimonies and turning them into a social law. Emotion is not
knowledge’s enemy, but cannot alone replace justification.
A good reason to keep reading alongside film is comparison. After an
explanation convinces us, we can check sources, follow objections,
and examine terms. Sometimes film itself provides this route through
25


transcripts and documentation. Then it does not compete with read‐
ing but provokes it. A good documentary can begin a personal library
rather than close it.
Turning reading into the tribunal legitimizing every other art would
be equally wrong. A cinematic experience need not end in a written
assignment to be valuable. Sometimes it is simply worth experiencing.
When we use film to learn about the world, what we ask is responsib‐
ility for its claims. Not every work makes the same promise.
A mature relationship between book and film is not a war of succes‐
sion. It is a possibility of translation, supplementation, and disagree‐
ment. Sometimes the book says more. Sometimes film sees something
the book could not show. Readers benefit by not turning love for one
medium into an obligation to be unfair to the others.

6. Wiki, Scientific Paper, and
Blog: Three Awkward Relatives
The Map That Repairs Itself and the Route That
Guides You
You open an encyclopedia article to learn a term’s meaning. Twenty
minutes later, you are in the history of a dispute you did not know you
needed to have. This may be wandering or discovery. Hypertext does
not decide alone. It offers branches; readers must distinguish explora‐
tion from merely sliding between novelties.
A well-constructed book reduces the cost of choosing a route. The au‐
thor implicitly says: first you need this, then we can proceed. A collab‐
orative encyclopedia offers entry points and links instead. It is good
for orientation, comparing terms, and discovering sources. It must not
be assessed exclusively by how well it imitates a treatise. Just as a city
is not a guided walk, a collection of pages need not contain one ideal
order.
English Wikipedia’s policies offer an important distinction: verifiabil‐
ity requires support from published sources, while the rule against
original research excludes presenting new results or syntheses as
already established knowledge. These are project rules, not guaran‐
tees that every page is correct. They nevertheless explain why an en‐
cyclopedia should not be confused with the place where an idea is
first demonstrated. [13, 14]
For readers, this suggests active use. A page can begin the route to
sources, not merely hold an answer we take. A controversial claim de‐
serves tracing to its cited document. A rich bibliography does not
alone prove it supports the sentence before us. This verification also
applies to books: sometimes a note points to a source saying some‐
thing more modest than the author.
Revision’s advantage is real as a possibility: text can be corrected
without reprinting an edition. But the possibility of correction does
not guarantee every change’s quality. History, date, and version there‐
27


fore help. For work we cite, reference stability is necessary. A living
page can serve knowledge precisely by preserving change traces, not
rewriting itself as though the past never existed.
The book sometimes offers the opposite: stability without enough
maintenance. We can pinpoint a passage, but it may be outdated. The
best solution is not declaring one flaw a virtue. It is combining edition
stability with access to errata and updates. Archive and correction are
not adversaries. We need to know both what was said and what re‐
mains defensible.
The wiki–book difference becomes decisive for beginners. Without a
picture of the field, you do not know what you do not know. A well-
chosen route can protect against accumulating unrelated details. Once
oriented, the same route can obstruct. Then you want to enter the pre‐
cise problem directly. Expert and beginner may need the same in‐
formation but different intellectual interfaces.
Imagine someone wanting to understand climate change, without as‐
suming a complete lesson on the field here. An encyclopedia can clari‐
fy terms. A book can organize relationships between mechanisms,
evidence, and consequences. Papers can answer specialized ques‐
tions. None should pretend to be the whole process. Danger appears
when initial orientation is confused with mastery.
A map is not inferior to a route. It is another tool. Sometimes we want
an address. Sometimes we want to understand a neighborhood. Some‐
times we need someone who knows where stopping is worthwhile.
The book can fulfill this last role without denying the other two’s use‐
fulness.
The Paper Contributes a Piece; the Blog Opens the
Workshop
A good scientific paper can be denser and harder than an entire pop‐
ularization book. Its short length does not make it superficial. On the
contrary, it may assume extensive knowledge the author does not re‐
introduce. An uninformed reader sees ten pages; a specialist also sees
the invisible library they depend on.
28


A research paper often tries to delineate a contribution: a question,
method, result, and its limits. A book can place the contribution in a
broader perspective. Yet there are also extensive review articles, just
as there are books with narrow original results. The distinction is
functional, not an editorial law. We cannot discover a claim’s quality
by measuring its publication medium’s thickness.
When reading research, the abstract is useful but not sufficient for
every purpose. It may say an advantage was observed without giving
all comparative details. For whom, compared with what, under which
conditions, and at what interval? These questions determine how far
we can take the conclusion. A result about understanding a short text
does not automatically become a verdict on two months with a novel.
Editorial assessment and peer review are oversight mechanisms, not
demonstrations of infallibility. Likewise, absence of a prestigious pub‐
lisher does not make a claim false. Readers need a hierarchy of justi‐
fications, not merely logos. Data, method, transparency, and the pos‐
sibility of criticism matter relative to the claim’s type. In this book, for
instance, future scenarios are conditional arguments, not disguised
experimental results.
The blog occupies an interesting place between rapid intervention
and developed essay. It can show an idea in progress, document an ex‐
periment, correct quickly, and provide access to a voice not filtered by
an editorial institution. These are possibilities, not guaranteed qualit‐
ies. A blog can be rigorous or careless, and comments can clarify a
problem or demonstrate that a keyboard requires no preliminary ex‐
amination.
There is even a small instructive irony: Naomi Baron discussed
screen–paper reading differences in an Oxford University Press blog
article. The blog form can therefore host analysis of the digital medi‐
um’s limits. Content must be judged, not punished for its address. [15]
The blog is often a more visible workshop than the book. It can pre‐
serve hypotheses, attempts, and successive corrections. A book can be‐
nefit through selection and reorganization. But copying twenty posts
between covers does not automatically produce a whole. Readers
sometimes pay twice for the same introduction, enthusiastically re‐
peated in every chapter.
29


Turning texts into a book requires distinct work: removing repeti‐
tions, explaining dependencies, establishing terms, and constructing a
shared question. This can create value even if the basic sentences
were already accessible. An ordered collection is not always merely a
collection. But order must do something, not simply be announced on
the cover.
Conversely, a book may need a public life after publication: replies to
criticism, corrections, references to new research. An associated blog
or wiki can fulfill this function without secretly rewriting the initial
edition. Readers gain when they can separate the work, the update,
and conversation about it.
These forms do not form a ladder with blogs at the bottom, papers in
the middle, and books on top. They form a circulation. An observation
may appear in a post, be investigated in a study, and interpreted in a
book. Criticism can circulate in reverse. Knowledge culture grows
healthier when we follow arguments through these passages instead
of stopping at packaging prestige.

7. The Three-Hour Podcast and
the Seduction of the Voice
When Conversation Thinks Before Our Eyes
A podcast begins with a good question. The guest hesitates, reformu‐
lates, admits not knowing something, and recounts reaching an idea.
Listeners receive more than a conclusion. They receive a public
demonstration of someone trying to think. At best, this has real value
a highly polished text can conceal.
In a book, hesitation may be removed during drafting. The argument
appears ordered, as though it existed that way from the start. Conver‐
sation can preserve searching, unexpected objections, and explanat‐
ory limits. A competent interlocutor can ask exactly what readers
wanted: not merely “what do you think?” but “why would that fol‐
low?” or “what example would force you to revise the claim?”
This advantage concerns conversation quality, not recording duration.
Three hours can contain careful investigation or an idea taking a
stroll to meet its step count. Podcast length is no safer a depth indicat‐
or than book thickness. Both can hide lack of selection beneath the
prestige of patience demanded from the audience.
Conversation also has affective resources. You hear doubt, humor, ir‐
ritation, or the attempt at precision. These can make an idea more ac‐
cessible and a person more intelligible. But convincing tone is not
evidence. Someone can speak sincerely about a mistaken idea. An ex‐
pert in one field can become very confident in another, where confid‐
ence lacks the same foundation.
It helps to distinguish three things that may appear together: personal
testimony, competence in a problem, and justification of a specific
claim. Testimony can be authentic without representative. Compet‐
ence can be real without covering the topic discussed. A claim can be
true but poorly supported. Neither familiar voice nor title resolves
these distinctions for listeners.
31


Unlike text, improvised speech can contain incomplete formulations
repaired along the way. Judging every hesitation as a serious error is
unfair. But using spontaneity as a shield against all checking is equally
unfair. When conversation makes claims about the world, it can offer
sources, corrections, and bounded uncertainty. Naturalness does not
demand absence of responsibility.
Face-to-face conversation has another advantage over both products:
the interlocutor can notice what you failed to understand and change
the explanation. A teacher, colleague, or craftsperson can see the error
in your gesture. Podcasts provide access to conversation but do not
automatically make you a participant. A book offers a voice but does
not observe your expression. For some learning types, this difference
is essential.
Imagine a novice learning to tune an instrument. A book explains
principles, a video shows movement, a musician’s podcast offers con‐
text and experience, but someone beside the novice can say: that is
not the problem; you turned too far. Situated feedback is not merely
another piece of subject information. It is information about what you
did, here.
This observation limits the promise of knowledge transfer through
any recorded content. Some competences require trial and correction.
The book does not disappear from this process but knows its place. It
can support preparation, reflection, and returning. Presenting it as a
universal substitute for teachers or practice would be an unserious
defense.
A good podcast need not become a book to be valuable. It can pre‐
serve conversation’s openness and movement. But listeners benefit by
asking what they receive: an experience, orientation, verified argu‐
ment, or merely the pleasant feeling of spending time near interesting
ideas. The last is no sin. It should not, however, be entered in the re‐
gister under “competence acquired”.

Three Hours Do Not Make a Bibliography
Suppose a guest makes a surprising claim at minute ninety-two. A
listener remembers it but no longer knows whether it was presented
as a solid result, hypothesis, or anecdote. A week later, they tell
someone else without qualifications. We need assume no bad inten‐
tion. It suffices that the format and its use make precise return harder.
A podcast can reduce this problem through clear chapters, transcripts,
sources linked to relevant moments, and correction notes. These do
not turn it into an academic article. They make it easier to use when it
promises knowledge, not merely company. An important claim should
not be hidden in a three-hour file like an object forgotten at an air‐
port.
The book has a conventional advantage here: contents, chapters, and
passages offer return points. But it is not innocent either. A general
bibliography without clear links to claims can serve as a decoration of
authority. Readers see many names and assume every sentence was
checked. In fact, they do not know which source supports the exact
conclusion that convinced them.
I would propose a common criterion for books, podcasts, and video:
verification cost. How much effort must a good-faith person expend to
find the exact claim, understand qualifications, and reach the relevant
source? A format reducing this cost is friendlier to readers or listen‐
ers, even if less spectacular in presentation.
Podcasts can gain through availability when visible text is inconveni‐
ent. But time thus occupied should not automatically count as “saved”.
Perhaps the journey or walk would have been useful precisely
without a stream of ideas. We need not turn every free interval into
absorption capacity. Sometimes thought needs a period without re‐
ceiving a new guest.
This is not a medical thesis about the correct number of headphone-
free minutes. It is an observation about purposes. If we never leave
time to formulate what we think of what we heard, accumulation may
remain without personal response. We listened to ten interpretations
of a problem without attempting one ourselves. Continuous informa‐
tion can become an elegant postponement of judgment.
33


The book can produce the same postponement. Some readers move
between volumes to avoid writing their page or making their decision.
Describing listeners as consumers and readers as inner authors is un‐
fair. Any of us can use culture to postpone encountering our own
problem.
The practical difference could be simple: after important material, a
moment of response is worthwhile. It may be conversation, a note, a
question to the author, or verification. Not every episode needs a re‐
port. But if nothing we receive is ever confronted, the most prestigious
source risks becoming mere ambience.
A podcast can be a companion, a legitimate function. A book can be a
refuge, also legitimate. Problems begin when we call all these func‐
tions “learning” and assume interchangeability. Sometimes we need
company, sometimes demonstration, sometimes quiet. Knowing our
need is more useful than defending one format against all others.

8. The Tweet, the Short, and the
Person Who Came for Just Five
Minutes
A Spark Is Not a Stove
A thirty-second clip shows something you would never have searched
for. A short message formulates an excellent question. An ironic image
reveals contradiction in pompous discourse. There is no serious reas‐
on to deny these possibilities. Culture does not become profound by
banning concision. Some thoughts need one sentence; others use a
hundred to avoid the necessary one.
The problem begins when opening is confused with formation. A clip
can awaken curiosity about a demonstration but rarely contain all its
conditions, objections, exercises, and applications. This is no defect if
the promise is modest. A road sign is not poor because it does not con‐
tain the city. It becomes misleading when selling the arrow as the
complete journey.
A short message has selection’s advantage. It forces authors to choose.
It can remove ballast and reveal an idea’s structure. But limited space
can eliminate precisely the condition making a claim true. “In this
context and with these reservations” becomes “science has demon‐
strated”. Not because brevity always requires lying, but because not
every idea retains meaning under the same compression.
Take an invented example. A study shows a particular method helps a
particular group on a particular task. The promotional message says:
“This is the best way to learn.” We gained space and lost almost the
entire claim’s structure. Adding heroic music does not recover the
loss. It only makes it more determined.
35


A sequence of messages can reintroduce nuance. A clip can link to ex‐
tensive explanation. Authors can explicitly present limits. The prob‐
lem is therefore not the short unit itself, but its relation to the rest of
knowledge. Is it a gateway toward something verifiable, or a complete
conclusion leaving no room for questions?
There are also short forms needing no expansion. A joke can lose
force when explained. An aphorism can open a problem through its
tension. A short poem is not a failed novel sketch. Correct comparison
concerns the promise: if material offers an observation, we judge it as
observation; if it claims to solve a field, we may ask more.
Seeing social networks only as competition would be unfair. Readers
may discover books, encounter unexpected interpretations, or find
discussion groups there. A well-chosen excerpt can persuade someone
to enter a work conventional advertising would not have shown them.
The important difference is direction: does short circulation lead to a
broader encounter or merely the feeling the encounter already
happened? The same channel can serve both. It needs not blanket
condemnation but use that knows when to move beyond it.
The book has a structural advantage for ideas requiring accumula‐
tion. It can preserve premises, return to them, and introduce differ‐
ences without restarting from a provocation each time. But this ad‐
vantage is wasted when every chapter tries to be a new viral clip.
Titles shout, examples shock, and objections appear only to be in‐
stantly defeated. Then we have a reaction feed bound in covers.
Humor can work both ways. It can expose a weak argument’s pom‐
posity or ridicule a position before we understand it. When laughing,
it is sometimes worth asking whether the joke identified a contradic‐
tion or merely avoided it. This book is not exempt. An amusing sen‐
tence does not become true because the author got the rhythm right.
A healthy short–long relationship is not moralizing. Short material
can invite; extensive material develops; a short response afterward
fixes an idea. The problem arises when invitations occupy the entire
evening. You were called to fifty doors and entered none. You feel in
motion but still have no place from which to view the problem.

The Competition for the Next Glance
An endless feed and a book with a final page propose different rela‐
tionships with stopping. A book can be abandoned, and a series can
continue. Nevertheless, each bounded work offers a recognizable
point saying: this is all I had to construct here. A feed continually gen‐
erating the next item needs no such point. Continuation is available
before you decide what the preceding item meant.
We need not invoke legends of destroyed brains or compare people to
fish to see the problem. Time’s arithmetic suffices. An hour occupied
by one activity cannot simultaneously be occupied in the same way by
another. Part of reading’s competition with new media is simply this
exclusivity. It does not prove the chosen activity bad. It requires noti‐
cing the choice.
More difficult is that systems do not all share user objectives. Imagine
a platform evaluated by how long people remain. Users may want to
learn something and leave. The platform may have reasons to offer
another reason to stay. This is a possible objective conflict, not a claim
that all apps work identically. Concrete design must be examined, not
assumed.
A service wanting to help understanding might count closing the app
and applying knowledge as success. A service tracking only session
duration might count the same departure as failure. The difference
changes which recommendations, pauses, and evaluation mechan‐
isms deserve building. Attention’s discussion is therefore not merely
about personal discipline. It is also about what we reward in products.
The book market is not outside these tensions. A publisher may prefer
a spectacular title to an accurate formulation. Authors may needlessly
lengthen a volume or promise impossible transformation. The differ‐
ence is not corrupt commerce versus an angelic library. It lies
between concrete mechanisms: what is sold, measured, corrected, and
who bears exaggerated promises’ cost.
Readers naturally have some control. They can disable notifications,
choose a dedicated device, and establish reading time. But reducing
the whole problem to willpower is convenient for whoever designs
37


circumstances. A tired person with responsibilities and fragmented
time is not a moral defect to repair with a library quotation. Reading’s
material conditions matter directly: light, access, quiet, available time.
There is another paradox. To defend reading, some apps turn it into a
new contest: consecutive days, page counts, speed, rankings. These
tools may motivate some people. They can also shift the objective
from encountering text to maintaining a score. If you no longer dare
reread a page because it spoils your speed, measurement has become
the activity’s adversary.
I would prefer indicators asking what you could do or formulate, not
merely how much you covered. For literature, sometimes even these
are unnecessary. Not every good experience needs visibility on a dash‐
board. Perhaps one freedom books protect is having an activity that
does not continuously report progress.
The book sends no notifications. This does not make it profound by
definition, but creates a rare possibility: the text does not renegotiate
every few seconds whether you will remain. You can stay for a slowly
forming reason. In a culture of the next glance, this silence is not ne‐
cessarily missing functionality. It may be the main function.

9. When the Book Starts
Answering
The Impossibly Patient Librarian
A reader stops at a difficult sentence and asks a chatbot: “What does
this term mean here?” They receive an explanation, request an ex‐
ample, and return to the text. In this scene, technology did not kill
reading. It removed an obstacle. For someone reading a foreign lan‐
guage, entering a new field, or needing accessible reformulation, such
help can be precious.
We can imagine even better uses. The system shows the relevant pas‐
sage, distinguishes concept meanings, compares chapters, and asks
readers how they would apply the distinction. When lacking informa‐
tion, it says so. When formulating its own interpretation, it does not
present it as the author’s statement. When detecting a possible contra‐
diction, it supplies both passages, not a mysterious verdict.
Here the interactive response fulfills one desire the critique of writing
in Phaedrus exposed: we can question a text. But a crucial difference
must remain. The historical text itself has not become a person, and
the absent author has not answered. A system generated a response
from information and rules. It can be useful without being confused
with the author’s authentic voice. [1]
I would impose three simple labels on such an interlocutor: “quota‐
tion”, “paraphrase”, and “interpretation”. A quotation must be find‐
able in the edition used. A paraphrase must preserve meaning
without claiming verbal identity. Interpretation may go beyond word‐
ing but must state its basis. These are proposals for design and critical
reading, not a description of guaranteed behavior in existing systems.
Imagine the following illustrative dialogue:
Reader: Does the author say people are selfish?
39


Assistant: In that passage, the author discusses a type of incentive,
not whether all human motives are selfish. Here is the wording. Shall
we examine the difference between the claims?
Reader: So I overgeneralized?
Assistant: That seems to be the problem with your interpretation. But
let us also check the next chapter before drawing a conclusion about
the whole book.
The dialogue’s value lies not in the system’s agreeableness but in send‐
ing readers back to the shared object. The text does not disappear into
conversation. It becomes more accessible and easier to challenge. A
good assistant can say “that does not follow” even when a more con‐
venient conclusion would please its interlocutor.
Research on AI support for learning already exists but needs the same
caution. An exploratory randomized study by the LearnLM and Eedi
teams, published as a preprint in December 2025, involved 165 stu‐
dents from five British schools. The mathematics tutoring system was
directly supervised by human tutors able to edit messages. Reported
results favored supervised AI support; they do not demonstrate an
autonomous chatbot’s equivalence to a teacher and do not concern lit‐
erary reading. [18]
An artificial interlocutor can therefore do more than summarize. It
can support questioning and practice if built and evaluated for these.
But technical demonstration—the system can formulate a good ques‐
tion—must not be confused with educational demonstration—users
learn better under well-defined conditions. The latter requires studies,
comparisons, and relevant outcomes.
The librarian metaphor helps but has limits. A real librarian has a
professional role, social relationship, and responsibilities. An auto‐
mated system has behavior produced by architecture and a service.
We can design it to support orientation without pretending every flu‐
ent answer represents care or human understanding. Respect for
users begins with this honesty.
40


The most promising conversational book, in my view, is not one al‐
ways sparing us reading. It knows when to answer directly and when
to help us read better. A good dictionary does not destroy language. A
good assistant should not destroy the encounter with text to demon‐
strate its usefulness.
The Reader Who Discussed Everything and Read
Nothing
Now imagine another evening. Readers request the book’s summary,
then key ideas, objections, and replies to objections. Finally, the sys‐
tem produces a personal opinion “in their style”. In less time than the
first chapter would take, they feel they have traversed the entire de‐
bate. What did they gain, and what did they not do?
For orientation, they may have gained much. We need not fully read
every book about which we need information. The problem begins if
we treat the conversational product as an experience we never had or
competence we never checked. Knowing what someone might say in a
discussion of a book is not identical to following its argument.
An exploratory study by Yue Fu and colleagues, in a preprint version
revised in July 2026, followed 15 students’ AI use for course readings
over eight weeks. The authors describe both comprehension support
and strategies of triage and AI-mediated reading. The small sample
and observational design permit no causal verdict about all students.
But the study reveals the distinction between reading with assistance
and delegating the encounter with text. [17]
Delegation is not automatically bad. In Evan Risko and Sam Gilbert’s
analysis, “cognitive offloading” means using actions and external re‐
sources to reduce task demands. External tools can be useful, not
merely weakening. The relevant question is what we delegate and
what we must remain capable of doing. [16]
If I delegate finding the page, I can preserve energy for examining the
argument. If I delegate examination, objection, and formulating my
position too, what do I learn? The answer is not necessarily “nothing”:
41


I can learn by reading the response. But I must check whether I can
independently sustain the system’s distinctions. Otherwise, compet‐
ence lies in the accessed service, not necessarily in me.
For some activities, reliable access to external competence suffices.
We do not memorize every tool we use. For others, internal evaluative
capacity is precisely what enables responsible assistance use. If I can‐
not recognize a mistaken interpretation, a personalized answer can
become more convincing the better it matches my vocabulary.
There is another cultural risk: reducing otherness. A good book can
ask questions I would not formulate. It can use an order I would not
choose. If my access is limited to what I ask the system, I mainly en‐
counter problems I already have language for. Personalization can
open a work but also cut it to the outline of my ignorance. This is not
technological fate; it is a design and usage choice.
An assistant can counter this risk by saying: “You asked about the con‐
clusion, but chapter two raises a problem your question omits.” Or by
proposing an uncomfortable passage, not merely one easily appreci‐
ated. Misunderstanding and disagreement must not automatically be
treated as experiential defects to eliminate.
I would assess a book with a chatbot beyond immediate satisfaction. I
would ask whether users can find passages, distinguish source from
interpretation, formulate objections, and solve new tasks without as‐
sistance. For literature, I would also ask whether the system allows
ambiguity or supplies a “correct meaning” for every sentence. These
are proposed criteria requiring testing, not already demonstrated be‐
nefits.
The possibility of not conversing must also be protected. A text inter‐
rupting every page to ask how we felt has become a tiring guest.
Sometimes the best interface is a visible button we need not press.
Help must be able to wait.
The conversational book deserves to exist. But turning it merely into a
device speaking ever better about works no one encounters would be
a loss. A good future is not one where books answer every question
for us. It is one where, after assistance, we have more questions of our
own and greater ability to sustain them.

10. The Case Against Books
Covers Do Not Sterilize Ideas
A book enters a room accompanied by a presumption of seriousness.
It has a title, cover, perhaps notes, and a name recommended by other
names. These can help us choose. They can also make a weak argu‐
ment harder to notice. Sober packaging is no less packaging than dra‐
matic music in a clip.
Sometimes a book earns authority through real work: research, writ‐
ing, verification, and correction. Sometimes it borrows those signs
without delivering the result. Authors may enumerate studies without
showing how they support a conclusion. They may construct a story
where every example confirms the thesis and nothing seriously chal‐
lenges it. Readers must ask whether the bibliography participates in
the argument or merely holds its coat.
There is also the coherent-whole effect. An explanation linking many
things can seduce precisely by reducing disorder. But a theory ex‐
plaining every possible outcome risks helping us distinguish nothing.
If success confirms the idea, failure also confirms it, and missing data
show how well the phenomenon hides, the book constructed a circle,
not a path.
A critical reader can request vulnerabilities. What observation would
weaken the thesis? Where does author competence stop? What rival
explanation would produce the same facts? These questions help in
any medium, but extensive books have a special obligation: room to
address objections. When they fail, lack of time is not always a de‐
fense.
The second charge is waste. Some volumes repeat the central idea
through superficially different stories. Repetition helps when adding
an angle or fixing an important relation. It becomes ballast when the
example changes nothing and readers could skip fifty pages without
loss. Authors have no right to turn reader availability into a deposit
exploited to exhaustion.
43


The third charge is exclusion. Book access requires money or provid‐
ing institutions, but also time, language, and skills. A culture praising
reading without building access may reward existing advantages.
Telling exhausted people reading will enrich life is insufficient. Per‐
haps the first enrichment would be an hour owed to no one.
The fourth charge is turning taste into moral rank. “I read” can be‐
come a declaration of superiority, not an activity description. But liter‐
ary quantity gives no right to treat others contemptuously. Readers
can have impressive libraries and modest capacity to listen to a real
person. Someone reading little may have judgment, attention, and ex‐
perience books never tested on their behalf.
The fifth charge concerns cultural selection. Every library, curriculum,
and collection chooses. Choices may be good and necessary but must
be discussed. Who is translated, who receives context, which experi‐
ences count as universal and which as exceptions? Asking does not
cancel established works. It refuses to equate historical selection with
the totality of value.
Digital text does not escape these problems. Recommendation lists
also have criteria, even when invisible. Apparently unlimited access
does not eliminate selection; it shifts some into ordering what be‐
comes visible. I would prefer an explained, challengeable choice to
one presenting itself as a neutral reflection of my preferences.
The conclusion of this indictment is not that books should be aban‐
doned. It is that they deserve to be defended without immunity. A seri‐
ous reading culture allows readers to say: this book does not justify its
length, this thesis is poorly supported, this recommendation excludes
me. Criticism does not destroy the prestige of the book. It gives it a
chance to deserve it.
Readers Receive No Certificate of Goodness
One of the most appealing defenses of literature says that it makes us
more empathetic. The intuition is plausible: we encounter different
perspectives, follow motives, and see consequences. But there is a dis‐
tance between an opportunity to understand and a guaranteed moral
transformation that enthusiasm can conceal.
44


The meta-analysis published by David Dodell-Feder and Diana Tamir
in 2018 found a small positive effect of reading fiction on some meas‐
ures of social cognition. The authors synthesized 14 experimental
studies. The finding does not establish that readers automatically be‐
come more generous in real life, or that every novel produces the
same change. Recognizing a mental state more accurately is not
identical to choosing to help. [19]
A systematic review by Júlia Ferreira Rezende and Nadia Shigaeff in
2023 examined research on fiction read and watched. It discusses pos‐
sible contributions from both forms, with differences between inter‐
ventions and a need for further research. We therefore have no solid
reason to grant books a monopoly on access to other people’s per‐
spectives. [20]
The distinction between understanding another person and caring
about them is essential. I can understand what frightens you and use
that information to protect you or to manipulate you. Literature can
expand the range of imaginable situations; it does not decide on its
own what I do with it. That is why the moral defense of reading must
remain both modest and demanding: opportunities exist, but respons‐
ibility cannot be delegated to the author.
Moreover, a story selects whom it gives an inner life. We may come to
understand one character deeply and ignore the people the story
keeps in the background. This is not an accusation against fiction, but
a question for reading: whose motives are explained, and whose are
assumed? The same question can be asked of a documentary, a piece
of reporting, or a public conversation.
Let us imagine a novel about an ambitious man. We know his fear, his
humiliations, and his hopes. We understand why he makes comprom‐
ises. But the person affected by his choices appears in only two scenes.
A critical reading need not refuse sympathy for the protagonist; it can
simply notice how attention is distributed. Sometimes the book itself
gives us the tools to ask what it has left unsaid.
There is also a difference between visiting suffering in our imagina‐
tion and responding to real suffering. The former can have value. It
does not replace the latter. A reader can finish a moving book and
then choose not to listen to someone close to them. We do not need a
45


study to see that these two actions are logically distinct. Yet we should
not infer that reading made no difference at all; only that it was not
enough.
That is why I think it wrong to use books as certificates of character.
Culture then becomes a form of moral accounting: I have read enough
about suffering, so I have done my part. But aesthetic experience does
not automatically discharge our obligations toward people. It can
make certain questions harder to ignore. The answer still has to be
given beyond the page.
A stronger defense of literature says that it can create conditions for
judgment: complicated examples, conflicting perspectives, situations
where a simple formula falls short. Readers can work with these or
turn them into confirmations of their own beliefs. A book does not
eliminate the freedom to use it badly. If it did, it would be a program‐
ming tool, not an encounter.
Ultimately, we do not need reading to guarantee virtue in order to
consider it valuable. Neither music, conversation, nor education as a
whole spares us from choosing. The book deserves to be preserved as
one of the ways we can become more attentive and more capable of
thinking. But modesty deserves to be preserved too: at the end of a
volume, we receive no diploma. At best, we receive something we still
have to work with.

11. Will Books Die? The Question
Needs Unpacking
The Market Can Grow While Reading Retreats
“The book is dying” can mean at least three different things: volumes
are no longer published or bought; substantial works are replaced by
other forms; sustained reading becomes a rare practice. The first is a
statement about the market, the second about culture, the third about
behavior and access. They can develop differently. An industry can
sell well to part of the population while the practice contracts in other
groups.
Data published by the Association of American Publishers on August
10, 2026, estimate United States revenue of 33.4 billion dollars in 2025
from books and course materials in print and digital formats. Print ac‐
counted for 76.3% of revenue in the general-interest book segment.
These are American industry estimates, not a global measure of read‐
ing or a measure of the time spent with each volume. Even so, they do
not describe an industry that has disappeared. [22]
On a different measure, a study presented by UCL in August 2025,
based on the American Time Use Survey, shows a decline in the pro‐
portion of people reading for pleasure on an average day: from 28%
in 2004 to 16% in 2023. The category also included digital formats and
listening to audiobooks. This is not the percentage of people who read
at least one book in a year. The study is observational and does not es‐
tablish the cause of the decline. [21]
The two findings do not cancel each other out. Money and time meas‐
ure different things. Revenue can be influenced by prices, product
types, and the concentration of purchases. A purchased copy may be
read by several people or by none. A borrowed book may matter
without appearing as a new sale. We cannot move from one indicator
to the other as though they were synonyms.
47


In June 2026, the Audio Publishers Association reported 9% growth in
audiobook revenue for 2025 in its market research. The estimate has
its own methodology and should not be mechanically combined with
AAP figures. It is nevertheless a sign that a work in audio form can of‐
fer a direction for commercial growth, not merely a hypothesis about
the future. [23]
These American data do not authorize identical claims about Ro‐
mania, Switzerland, or the whole world. We use them to show how
the vitality of some segments can coexist with serious reasons for con‐
cern about the practice. A local analysis would need its own data and
comparable definitions. A book about the future becomes more cred‐
ible when it refuses to turn one country into the planet.
The real question is what we want to survive. Is it enough for expens‐
ive editions, beautiful collections, and a passionate community to ex‐
ist? From the perspective of the object, perhaps. From the perspective
of a society in which people have access to substantial arguments and
can discuss shared texts, no. Survival as a luxury good is not the same
as the vitality of a shared practice.
A book culture also needs work that is hard to see in the cover price:
selection, editing, translation, verification, preservation, and guid‐
ance. If producing text becomes very cheap, these functions do not
disappear by definition. On the contrary, in a scenario of abundance,
readers may have an even greater need to know who checked it, how
errors were corrected, and why a particular work deserves their time.
It does not follow that existing economic models should be frozen in
place. Some can restrict access; others can support the necessary
work. The question is how we pay for real value without confusing
the difficulty of distribution with intellectual merit. A file may be easy
to copy, while writing a good book remains demanding work. An
abundance of copies does not imply an abundance of judgment.
Four Futures, None Inevitable
The first scenario is selective retreat. Books used mainly for specific
answers lose ground to faster tools, while the novel, the carefully con‐
structed essay, and some syntheses remain attractive. This would not
48


be the death of the book, but a redistribution of its functions. Some
losses might be healthy. A volume that hides a single idea among
many repetitions has no natural right to the reader’s time.
But a risk arises here too. An introductory book may look like a collec‐
tion of simple answers, although its value lies in the order that con‐
nects them. If we replace it with isolated answers, we may save
minutes and lose the framework that allows us to ask better ques‐
tions. In this scenario, the challenge is to distinguish useful compres‐
sion from the amputation of structure.
The second scenario is the book as a privileged refuge. Part of the pub‐
lic retains the time and space for reading, while everyone else mainly
receives fragmented content, quick guidance, and answers on de‐
mand. I am not claiming that this is the proven future. I consider it a
risk to take seriously, because preserving the object does not guaran‐
tee that the conditions for access are shared. A world with splendid
libraries and little free time can be deeply unequal in the way it al‐
lows people to think.
The third scenario is good hybridization. The work has a stable core,
but can be read, listened to, annotated, and discussed. A video demon‐
stration appears where it explains something real. The assistant an‐
swers when called and stays quiet otherwise. Readers can see what
belongs to the author, what is a correction, and what is an automated
interpretation. In this scenario, the book survives through an adapta‐
tion that preserves its identity, rather than despite adaptation.
The fourth scenario is abundance without guidance. Producing
volumes that look convincing becomes easy, but their quality varies,
and verification remains costly. Readers no longer lack text; they lack
sound reasons for choosing. The answer need not be an automatic re‐
turn to a few closed editorial gates. It can be the construction of more
transparent mechanisms for review, provenance, correction, and re‐
commendation.
These scenarios can combine. A book can be hybrid and financially in‐
accessible. An open project can be thoroughly checked. A printed edi‐
tion can contain careless text. A conversational service can encourage
reading or replace it with tailored answers. The medium alone does
not determine the distribution of quality and power.
49


Who stands to gain depends on the configuration. Authors can reach
new readers through accessible formats. Readers can reduce barriers
of language and navigation. Intermediaries can create value through
selection and verification, or capture the relationship with the public
without assuming the same responsibility. Libraries can become more
important as spaces for access and guidance, but only if they have the
necessary resources.
For society, the stakes go beyond the existence of a cultural product.
They concern the possibility of having shared objects of discussion. If
each person receives a different, continually adapted reformulation,
what exactly are we criticizing together? Personalization can be use‐
ful, but it should preserve a text and a version we can return to.
Without a shared object, disagreement risks becoming a conversation
between summaries that do not match.
My prediction is therefore conditional: books have strong reasons to
continue, but the shape of their success is not guaranteed. I would bet
on transformation and differentiation before total disappearance. Yet
I would not comfortably bet that sustained reading will remain ac‐
cessible to everyone without institutional effort. The future depends
on more than how good books are. It also depends on what kind of life
leaves people time to enter them.

12. The Book of the Future Must
Also Know When to Be Quiet
A Stable Core and Help on Request
Let us design a book of the future without the obligation to fill it with
every feature technology can offer. The first thing is the work: an iden‐
tifiable text, with a structure and a version. Readers must be able to
say what they are reading and return to the same passage. Corrections
are welcome, but their traces must remain visible. An improved edi‐
tion must not erase the memory of the criticism that made it neces‐
sary.
The second thing is freedom of access. The text can have adjustable
type, audio playback, clear navigation, and compatibility with access‐
ibility tools. These are not ornaments. They can determine who has
the opportunity to encounter the work. A sophisticated interface that
excludes readers for whom the simple format worked is not a univer‐
sal improvement.
The third thing is help that does not seize the initiative. A glossary
opens when you need it. An explanation shows you where it begins.
An animation clarifies a relationship. An assistant preserves the dis‐
tinction between text and interpretation. Readers can close every lay‐
er and continue with the work. This ability of the interface to with‐
draw seems to me a more important measure of quality than the num‐
ber of features.
The fourth thing is continuity. Bookmarks, notes, and position within
the work should be reasonably easy to preserve and move between
forms. It is not useful for text and audio to be two disconnected
worlds, or for notes to disappear along with a service. These are pro‐
posed requirements for durable cultural products, not assumptions
about what every platform already offers.
The fifth thing is privacy. The questions we ask a book can reveal
fears, beliefs, or personal circumstances. We should not assume that
every interaction must be collected for good reading to exist. The
51


American Library Association treats confidentiality in library use as a
condition of intellectual freedom. This is an American institutional
ethical reference point, not a description of every legal system. Yet the
principle deserves serious consideration when designing digital read‐
ing. [24]
A printed book can be observed, its purchase can leave traces, and its
notes can be read by someone else. It is not magically invisible. But
the text on the table does not need to tell a service how long I lingered
over a passage. We can ask a digital product for comparable discre‐
tion in features that do not require a connection. Technology should
explain why it needs data, rather than treating readers’ curiosity as a
resource available by default.
The sixth thing is preserving an ending. Not every conclusion needs to
trigger a new recommendation immediately. Sometimes the work can
be allowed to end. Readers need the option of disengaging without be‐
ing instantly pulled toward the next object. A product that respects its
ending acknowledges that its value can continue outside the interface.
All of these things can be done well or badly. A stable core can become
rigidity; personalization can become fragmentation; explanation can
become excessive supervision. No architecture automatically resolves
every tension. But we can establish a priority: the tool must increase
readers’ freedom and ability, not their dependence on an intermedi‐
ary.
In this future, the publisher does not necessarily disappear. Its role
can become more explicit: ensuring coherence, source quality, access‐
ibility, and clear boundaries between layers. Authors remain respons‐
ible for what they sign. The automated system must be identified
wherever it contributes. Readers must be able to distinguish the pub‐
lished work from an answer generated later, during conversation.
Such a book is no less a book because it can speak. It becomes less of a
book if there is no longer anything stable to encounter, if every dis‐
agreement is smoothed away, and if the work changes solely to con‐
firm your profile. Good adaptation also preserves resistance: the text’s
right to say something other than what you would have ordered.

A Life with Reading, Without the Tribunal of Pages
We need not end with a universal quota of pages per day. A quota can
help someone build a habit, but it is not the moral unit of a life. There
are periods when we read a great deal and periods when we can
barely open a book. Loving reading does not require us to despise the
life that interrupts us. Sometimes someone needs us, and the book can
wait.
A better beginning is to choose a purpose. Do I want to find my bear‐
ings in a field? I can start with an overview and a few definitions. Do I
want to learn to do something? I need explanation, demonstration,
and practice. Do I want to follow an argument? A book or a substan‐
tial essay may be suitable. Do I want an artistic experience? I do not
have to justify it through productivity. Stating our purpose keeps us
from asking one medium to provide every benefit.
Then comes choosing a level of difficulty we can live with. A book that
is too easy may repeat what we know; one that is too difficult may re‐
quire an introduction. There is no shame in changing the route. Read‐
ers need not prove anything to authors through suffering. Nor should
they give up at the first resistance. It is worth asking whether the diffi‐
culty conceals an important idea or merely careless presentation.
We can create a simple space for reading: a text chosen before the
available time begins, a convenient place, and fewer voluntary inter‐
ruptions. This is a personal experiment, not a guaranteed treatment.
We observe what helps us and adjust. One reader may prefer paper,
another a device, another alternating with audio. The aim is the en‐
counter with the work, not the victory of a material preference.
The right not to finish is useful too. A book may be unsuitable right
now, poor, or simply less important than another. Giving up should
not automatically be confused with superficiality. At the same time, it
is worth distinguishing justified abandonment from a reflexive flight
toward anything new. Only readers, with a certain honesty toward
themselves, can make this distinction in a particular case.
Reading can become social without losing its inner time. A group can
discuss a passage instead of organizing a parade of general opinions.
Everyone can show where their interpretation begins. Disagreement
53


thus becomes something we can check. We do not argue only about
what the book seemed to be saying; we return to the shared object.
Conversation does not replace reading; it gives it a second life.
Schools could cultivate this relationship through practices that make
understanding visible without reducing the work to an official answer.
A student can defend an interpretation and be asked which passage
justifies it. They can compare an excerpt with an adaptation. They can
use a digital assistant and check where it oversimplified. What mat‐
ters is learning the difference between accessing an answer and con‐
structing a justification.
The public library has a mission here that goes beyond storage. It can
offer access, quiet, guidance, and gatherings around texts. For those
who lack these conditions at home, they are not cultural accessories.
They are an infrastructure that makes reading possible. Defending the
book in public life therefore also means defending the places and
people that make reading practicable.
We should also allow a way of reading that requires no explanation to
anyone. No photograph, no score, no message about what we have fin‐
ished. Not because sharing is bad, but because an experience’s value
need not depend on publishing it. We can read something that in‐
terests us without turning it into an identity.
Ultimately, a life with reading is not a life from which films, podcasts,
and conversations disappear. It is one in which each can find its place
without colonizing all the others. Perhaps the reader of the future will
not read in the same way as the idealized reader of the past. Yet they
will have to preserve an old and difficult ability: not to confuse having
received an answer with having finished thinking.

Epilogue. The Last Page Is Not a
Notification
Let us return to the woman in the third row. The lecture on the death
of the book has ended. The speaker takes questions about his new
book. This is not a fatal contradiction: criticisms of a cultural form can
themselves be expressed in that form. But the scene reminds us how
easily we turn “it will disappear” into “it will change” once the ap‐
plause has faded.
The woman closes her novel. Not because she has finished it. She has
encountered a sentence that slightly changes how she sees a conversa‐
tion she had years ago. For a moment, she sits without doing anything
observable. It is unclear whether we would count that minute as read‐
ing. She has covered no pages, received no new information, and
pressed no button. But perhaps that was precisely where something
happened that made reading worthwhile.
The scene remains imaginary. It does not prove the universal superi‐
ority of the book. A film, a song, or a conversation can open up such a
pause. What we have tried to defend is not a monopoly, but a possibil‐
ity: a sustained, repeatable, and relatively autonomous encounter
with a work that does not have to renegotiate its existence after each
of our reactions.
The book deserves to survive. Not because it is always the quickest
way to find something out, or because people who read are thereby
superior. It deserves to survive because it can accommodate forms of
unfolding that a hurried culture risks treating as needless costs. It can
let a problem grow more complicated before resolving it. It can pre‐
serve an ambiguity. It can ask us to reconsider a judgment.
Yet we must accept a strict condition: not every book deserves to sur‐
vive in our attention. Some deserve to be archived and criticized. Oth‐
ers can be forgotten without loss. Defending the form does not mean
absolving every instance. On the contrary, the more precious readers’
time becomes, the more authors and publishers must respect it.
55


The book of the future can be printed, digital, spoken, or accessible
through several routes. It can have demonstrations and an automated
conversation partner. It can allow alternative paths. But it should pre‐
serve something that does not change merely because the user
frowned: a work they can disagree with, a thread they can follow, a
version they can cite.
And it should preserve the ability to withdraw. It should not turn
every hesitation into a signal to intervene. It should not treat every
pause as a defect. It should not regard being closed as a failure of re‐
tention. Sometimes a book has done its job precisely when readers
leave to speak differently with someone, check something, or try out
an idea.
We do not need to save the book from every change. We need to save
our ability to choose the changes that matter. Let us not confuse access
with understanding, comfort with truth, duration with depth, or the
familiarity of a voice with its authority. Let us use a summary when
we need guidance and recognize when we want more than guidance.
The last page offers something simple: another does not automatically
follow. We are not obliged to fill the gap immediately. We can let the
text settle in disagreement with us or close beside us. We can see what
question remains after the author’s voice falls silent.
In a world that always has something to tell us, perhaps this is one
reason the book is still worth opening: at some point, it knows how to
leave us alone with what we have begun to think.

How Sources Were Used
This book is a researched essay for a general audience, not a systemat‐
ic review of the entire literature on reading. Research is used for
bounded claims, while conceptual comparisons, imaginary scenes,
and proposals about the future are arguments made by the text. The
narrative examples and the dialogue with the assistant were construc‐
ted for explanation; they are not field observations or actual tran‐
scripts.
Numbered references allow sources to be identified without excess‐
ively interrupting reading. Publication pages, publicly available study
texts and abstracts, materials by the authors, and communications
from the institutions that conducted the research were consulted. The
positions of Maryanne Wolf and Naomi Baron are presented through
verifiable outlines in materials from the authors and publishers, not
as the result of a full review of the volumes. For the American trend in
reading for pleasure, the source used is UCL’s communication about
the study published in iScience, identified as such.
Meta-analyses bring together studies that differ in participants, texts,
devices, and tasks. An average finding does not mechanically apply to
every reader. Several syntheses may partly include the same studies,
so they should not be counted as wholly independent evidence. A dif‐
ference on a comprehension test does not, by itself, establish a work’s
aesthetic value or the effects of a lifetime of reading. The absence of
an overall statistical difference does not prove that two experiences
are perfectly identical.
The AI research mentioned consists of preprints and is explicitly
labeled. A small observational study describes possible behaviors
without establishing their frequency across the whole population. An
experiment with human supervision does not justify a conclusion
about autonomous systems. Researchers’ affiliation with the de‐
velopers of a tool is relevant to interpretation and to the need for in‐
dependent evaluations, without automatically invalidating the result.

or the whole world. Phones or social networks are not assigned a
causal role that the cited observational study cannot establish.
Predictions about disappearance, hybridization, access, and abund‐
ance are conditional scenarios. Requirements concerning stable ver‐
sions, verifiable sources, confidentiality, and optional help are norm‐
ative proposals. They should be judged by their consequences and
tested in concrete implementations. Confidentiality is discussed as a
principle of design and intellectual freedom, not as individualized leg‐
al advice.
