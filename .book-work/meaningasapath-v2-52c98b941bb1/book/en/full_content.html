One-page summary

The thesis. The meaning of a sentence, in a given world (an activity with its fixed questions: a ledger, a warehouse, a timetable), is the set of answers it gives to the short list of that world’s questions (who? what? to whom? when? under which condition?), including the answer “it does not say”. Two sentences mean the same thing when they give the same answers, however differently they sound. We call the translation of a sentence into such verifiable answers the formalisation of language.

The mechanism. A small reader, with no knowledge about the world, walks through the sentence word by word as along a route in a space of thousands of dimensions: each kind of word is a learned jump; each word is drawn where the route has reached; different routes that reach the same named places give the same frame. The named places (the roles) come from the world; the route is learned from examples with their frames (which come from existing structured data, or are written at training time by a large model on a sample); nothing else is learned. Everything the reader knows fits in ten to fifteen megabytes, the size of a photograph; reading costs microseconds on an ordinary processor; the same input always gives the same output.

What has been shown so far, on constructed worlds and on a real set of questions. Different formulations of the same event reach the same frame. The reader composes correctly at a nesting depth it never saw in training, where a neural network with the same input guesses. New words are read without retraining. Logical operators (negation, modality, conditionals) are read with their scope correct, without hand-written rules. On real questions, with the same input and no external knowledge, the role structure is learned better than by a small transformer or a lexical method, at every data size tried. And what was not understood shows on the chart (an empty domain, a crowded domain, an unexplained remainder), instead of being covered by a fluent answer.

What it is not. Not a language model: it does not generate, does not converse, holds no facts. It does not replace large models on open text. Its place is beside them: the verifiable layer between language and action, the one that says what was understood, what was not, and where, and through which everything that reaches execution or the database must pass.

Where it leads. Assistants for closed worlds, allowed to say “I don’t know”; a verifier of the formalisations made by large models; a semantic interlock for robots, cheap and implementable in hardware; the exact reading of procedures, regulations and reports in regulated industries; a verifiable interface for the alignment of agentic systems. Each is described with its mechanism and with the measure that sets its pace.

State and pace. The signals are good and of the kind that matters; what remains is, for the most part, engineering and adjustments of the model, with incremental steps and criteria written in advance. The exact state of every measurement is kept up to date in a single public file: https://github.com/ml-circuits-research/vsa-semantic-space/blob/main/STATUS.md

1. The idea we started from

When you read a sentence, you do not gather the words into a sack. You travel. Each word moves you somewhere, and what you are left with at the end is not the list of words, but the place you reached and what you left along the way. “Paul gives a book to Anna” and “Anna receives a book from Paul” are two different routes that end in the same place. That is why they mean the same thing.

We took this image seriously and built it. To explain it without cheating, we describe below an imaginary story, a sky full of stars, a probe, a pilot, and the world the sentences talk about, which behaves exactly like the mathematics behind the system: every statement in the story is also true of the real system, and no piece of it does anything the real system does not do. At the end, a table ties each piece to its technical name.

1.1 What we mean by meaning

Before the story, a word about the word “meaning”, because we use it in a particular sense, narrower than the usual one and, for that very reason, measurable.

A sentence describes an event: someone gave something to someone, a supplier delivered some boxes somewhere, a teacher holds a class in a room at a given hour. About such an event one can ask questions: who? what? to whom? where? when? how much? Not any number of them and not any question: for a given world (that is what we will call an activity with its questions: a ledger, a warehouse, a timetable), the list of questions that make sense is short and fixed. A lending ledger never asks “at what temperature?”; a warehouse never asks “who liked it?”. To know what a sentence means, in this document, is two things: to know which questions can be asked about the event it describes, and to know, for each of them, what answer the sentence gives, including the answer “it does not say”. An answer may be a word, it may be the negation of a word (“not Paul”), and to “what does Maria say?” it may be a whole event, with its own questions.

This definition has three consequences we will use throughout. First, meaning is relative to a domain: the same sentence means something different to the warehouse and to the timetable, because their lists of questions differ; there is no “meaning in itself”, there is meaning relative to a list of questions. Second, two sentences mean the same thing exactly when they give the same answers to the same questions, however differently they sound. Third, “I did not understand” becomes something precise: a question left unanswered although the sentence should have answered it, an answer in which two candidates compete, a piece of the sentence that answers no question on the list, or a question the sentence raises that the list does not contain.

Answers have two sources, and we keep them apart. The sentence says what it says: “Paul gives a book to Anna” answers who, what and to whom, and is silent on when. The world’s database knows the rest, or contradicts: that the loan was on Tuesday, that Paul owns no book. The system described here does only the first part: it turns the sentence into a complete set of answers to the world’s questions, with the gaps marked, in a form that a database or a reasoning engine can check and complete. We call this transformation the formalisation of language: not the formalisation of grammar, but the translation of what is said into verifiable answers to questions known in advance. The rest of the document is about how this is done without the system holding any knowledge base about the world.

1.2 Who takes part

Four participants, and it is important not to mix them up.

The sky. A single universe, almost empty. A point in the sky is given by coordinates, and that is all: there is nothing in it besides coordinates. Some points have been given a name or marked by someone; those we call stars. A star is not an object; it is a point someone has written down. We will meet three kinds of stars: the words’ stars, the world’s named stars, and the stars the pilot draws on a sentence’s chart. The sky is not the world the sentences talk about and knows nothing about it.

The probe. The only thing that moves through the sky. It knows how to do two things and nothing else: to jump from one point to another, when it is given a key, and to report the coordinates of the point it has reached. At the start of every sentence it is at the origin, the point with all coordinates zero.

The world. The domain the sentences talk about, and the only place in the story where facts exist. Three examples, to make it concrete:

  • A warehouse. Its sentences sound like this: “Supplier Alfa delivered 40 boxes to aisle B on Tuesday.” The warehouse wants to know, from any sentence: who delivered? what? how much? where? when?
  • A school timetable. “Mrs Weber teaches physics in room 12 on Tuesday at 10.” The timetable asks: who? which course? in which room? when?
  • A lending ledger between friends. “Paul gives a book to Anna.” The ledger asks: who gives? what? to whom?

Each world therefore has a short list of questions, always the same for all its sentences. A sentence answers some and leaves others empty (“Paul gives a book to Anna” does not say when). The thing a sentence describes (a delivery, a lesson, the handing over of a book) we will call an event; the word means nothing more than “what happened, in this sentence”. The world lives outside the pilot, for instance in a database: it knows that Alfa is a supplier and that aisle B exists. The pilot knows none of this. A world has things of its own: the legend, the kinds of words, the route book learned on its sentences and the database; chapter 5 takes them in turn. In the rest of the document we use the lending ledger as the main example, because it is the smallest.

The pilot. A stranger. Think of him as a cartographer come from very far away, who does not understand a word of our language: he does not know who Paul is, what a book is, whether Paul really gave it to Anna, and has no way of finding out, because he never sets foot in the world. He does not move; he stays at the base, outside the sky, and commands the probe. What he knows, and knows very well, is a single craft, learned from thousands of sentences: what jump each kind of word (person’s name, verb, preposition…) calls for. The kind of a word he does not guess: he receives it from a dictionary (3.1). His purpose is one alone: to turn each sentence into a chart of the sky that the world can read, that is, to find out, for each word, which domain of the sky it must reach, without understanding what the word means. That he does not understand is not a shortcoming of the story; it is exactly what we want to show: meaning can be reached with no knowledge of the world, from the shape of the path alone. The pilot keeps three documents, and the whole story is about them:

  • The route book: what jump each kind of word calls for (person’s name, verb, preposition…). It is the only thing the pilot has ever learned, and the only thing he will learn.
  • The legend: the world’s list of questions, with a named star beside each question. For the lending ledger: “who gives?” has the agent’s star; “what?” has the object’s star; “to whom?” has the recipient’s star. For the warehouse: the supplier’s star, the goods’ star, the quantity star, the place star, the time star. Each question is a role, and its star defines a domain of the sky, the agent’s domain, the quantity domain, which the word that answers the question must reach (what exactly a domain is, in 2.6). In this book the word “domain” means only that; for a field of knowledge or activity we say “world”. The named stars are drawn at random once, by whoever builds the world; nobody learns them. The legend of one kind of event is small: a few questions, usually under twenty; an action legend for regulated documents reaches a few dozen. A large world, a science, an enterprise, does not have a huge legend but many kinds of events, each with its own small legend, and the questions repeat from one kind to the next: “when”, “where”, “how much”, “why” are the same questions wherever they appear. Formally, a world has a single legend (one named star per question; “when” is the same star wherever it appears), and “the legend of one kind of event” is just the part of it that kind usually fills. Verbs, like any words, have stars in the catalogue of word stars (2.4); the legend has a single question for the action (“what was done?”), whose answer is the verb, and the kind of event is deduced by the world from the verb (chapter 8, “Which verb?”); and a question whose answer is itself a small event, the conditions of a reaction, the clauses of a contract, opens a legend within the legend, by the same mechanism through which “reported” moves the origin. What stays small is what the pilot has in front of him at any one moment; the sky has room for thousands of named stars without crowding.
  • The sentence’s chart: the document the pilot builds while reading, word by word, and which at the end is the sentence’s meaning. A chart of the sky, empty at first; at each word the pilot draws a star on it: the word’s star, shifted by the coordinates the probe reported. At the end, the chart is a constellation; the pilot reads it through the legend and starts a new one.

The meaning of a sentence is therefore something very concrete: which words reached which domains. The names of the domains come from the legend (from the world), the path comes from the route book (learned), and “who reached where” comes from the chart (built while reading). Nothing ever lights up in the sky; everything that remains is on paper, at the base.

Chapter 2 describes the pieces in the order in which they are used: the coordinates of a point, the probe’s jump, the word’s star, the chart and the constellation, the legend and the domains, and the scanner with which the chart is read.

1.3 Where we stand

This document describes a system that has been built and measured, on constructed worlds and on a real set of questions, and a direction that goes much further than what has been measured. We say once, here, how things stand, so that the rest can be read without a reservation in every paragraph.

The system composes correctly at a depth it never saw in training, where a neural network with the same input guesses. New words are read without retraining. 2. The sky, the probe and the pilot

2.1 A point in the sky: coordinates with thousands of dials

The coordinates of a point are a code, as on a combination lock: a string of dials, a thousand or a few thousand (in the measured system, 1024), each of which can be turned anywhere on its circle, from 0 to 360 degrees. Think of each dial as a small face with an arrow that points at an angle. One particular position of all the arrows at once is a point in the sky. Another position is another point.

So that we can write examples, let us pretend there are only four dials. Then a point looks like this: (10°, 200°, 95°, 300°). Another: (12°, 198°, 97°, 301°). A third: (180°, 20°, 275°, 120°).

Two points are close when, dial by dial, the arrows point in about the same direction: the first two above are close. They are unrelated when the arrows do not match: the third has nothing in common with the first two. That is the whole geometry of the sky: not distances in light-years, but agreement or disagreement between arrows, counted over thousands of dials. Between two points there are, nevertheless, intermediate points, and we will need them: a point whose arrows sit halfway between those of the two is half close to each.

One rule that holds for everything that follows: nothing in the sky happens “at the level of the point”. Every operation is done dial by dial, with the same simple rule on each dial, and a point is nothing but the set of its dials. There are exactly three operations, and we meet them in turn: to compare two points (the agreement between arrows, just described), to shift a point (to turn each arrow by an angle, in 2.3) and to fuse several points into one (to add the arrows, in 2.5). Each time we describe what happens on a single dial; on the other thousands the same thing happens.

And one more thing, so that no doubt remains: the sky is empty. There is nothing in it of its own, no world, no words, no beaten paths. There are only points, and a point is a code. Stars are points someone has written down somewhere, in the catalogue, in the legend or on a chart; the sky itself remembers nothing. We say “code” of any string of angles, whatever it is used for, and “star” of a code that has received a name or a purpose.

2.2 Why two points chosen at random are unrelated

Here is the property on which everything else rests. Turn all the dials at random, twice, and compare the two codes. At one particular dial, they match by chance; at another, they do not; the matches and mismatches cancel out. Over thousands of dials, the total agreement is almost exactly zero. Two points chosen at random are, with practical certainty, unrelated: not the farthest apart possible (that would mean every dial turned exactly opposite), but simply with no agreement that counts. And the chance that two random codes visibly resemble each other falls exponentially with the number of dials; at thousands of dials it is, in practice, zero.

The consequence: the sky has room for an enormous number of stars that do not disturb one another. Anything that receives coordinates chosen at random is, with the same practical certainty, distinct from anything else that has received some. Randomness is not a weakness we tolerate; it is the source of separation.

2.3 The probe: a single operation

The probe has a single operation, and it is the same one the pilot will also use at the base: to shift a point by a code. You take the point’s coordinates and a second code; for each dial, you turn the point’s dial by the angle of the corresponding dial in the code. The result is another point. That is all.

The jump is this operation applied to the probe’s position. A key is a code: “dial 1: +100°; dial 2: +30°; dial 3: +300°; dial 4: +45°”. The pilot sends the key, the probe shifts its position by it and is at another point in the sky. It was at (10°, 200°, 95°, 300°); now it is at (110°, 230°, 35°, 345°); the third dial went past 360 and started over: 95 + 300 = 395, that is, 35. Then the probe reports the new position to the base. With the inverse key (each angle with a minus sign) it returns exactly to where it left from.

Three properties of shifting, all visible in the example, and all used below:

  • Shifting preserves shape. If two points were close to each other and you shift both by the same code, they stay exactly as close, because each dial turned by the same angle on both. A whole constellation, shifted, keeps its drawing.
  • Shifting can be undone. You shift back by the inverse code and you have the point from before, with no loss. Any string of jumps can be undone step by step.
  • The point after has no relation to the point before. A key turns each dial by a different angle, drawn at random; so the starting point and the arrival point are, to anyone looking, two random points. The way back exists only for whoever has the key.

Two keys one after the other are still a key: the angles add up, dial by dial. From this comes a consequence that is both convenient and a real limitation: order does not matter. Key A then key B takes the probe exactly where B then A takes it, because 100° + 30° is the same thing as 30° + 100°. The probe’s position remembers which keys have been used since the origin, not in what order. We come back to this in chapter 8.

Keep in mind: shifting a point by a code is called, in technical language, binding (bind). The jump is shifting the position by a key. The next piece is the same operation, used by the pilot on stars.

And one observation that will come back often, because without it stars and keys seem to blur into each other: a code can be used in two ways, as a place or as a shift. The same string of angles is, read as a place, a point in the sky, perhaps a star; read as a shift, a key that turns the arrows of another point. Nothing in the code says which of the two it is; it depends only on what you do with it. The probe’s position is a place, and in 2.5 it will be used as a shift; the agent’s star is a place, and in 2.6 it will be used as a shift.

2.4 The word’s star

Every word has, at the base, a star of its own: coordinates drawn at random once, when the word first appears, and never changed, never learned. “Paul” has its star, say (50°, 50°, 50°, 50°) in our four-dial example; “book” has another, (300°, 120°, 40°, 210°). They have nothing in common and never will. All the words’ stars sit in a catalogue, at the base. The catalogue need not be memorised as vectors (the word list, yes: it is the vocabulary, text): the coordinates of a word’s star are computed on the spot from its letters and a fixed seed of the system, so the same word always receives the same star, and a new word receives one without anyone having stored it; the ten to fifteen megabytes of the summary are the route book, not the vocabulary.

The star says nothing about what the word means; it only makes it recognizable wherever it is shifted, because the shape of a shifted star is preserved and shifting can be undone. Here there is already a difference from anything neural. A language model learns its word vectors slowly, from billions of examples, and an unseen word is broken into pieces and receives a vector guessed from its pieces. Here, an unseen word receives a new star on the spot, as good as any other. Nothing in the system depends on which coordinates a word received.

2.5 The sentence’s chart: how a constellation is drawn

The probe has just reported the position (110°, 230°, 35°, 345°), and the word read is “Paul”. The pilot takes Paul’s star from the catalogue and shifts it by the reported position: the same operation as in the jump, only now the thing shifted is the word, and the code it is shifted by is the probe’s position, used as a shift (2.3). Paul, (50°, 50°, 50°, 50°), becomes (160°, 280°, 85°, 35°) (the last dial: 50 + 345 = 395, that is, 35). This is a new star, “Paul shifted by the probe’s position”, and the pilot draws it on the chart. It no longer looks anything like Paul’s star in the catalogue; but anyone who knows the probe’s position can shift back and obtain (50°, 50°, 50°, 50°) exactly. This is how we say, in this story, “Paul was found where the probe was”.

The chart is not a list of stars drawn side by side, and it is not a code like the others either. It is a tally of all the stars drawn, kept dial by dial. Here is how. On a star, the arrow of each dial shows only a direction; its length is always the same, say 1. On the chart, the arrow of each dial has a direction and a length, and the length can be anything. To fuse a new star into the chart means, on each dial, to put the star’s arrow end to end with the chart’s arrow and keep the resulting arrow, with its direction and its length: an ordinary addition of arrows, the way two forces pulling on the same ring add up.

Three dials from our example, as two stars, X and Y, are fused into an empty chart. On dial 1, X points at 10° and Y at 70°: the chart’s arrow ends up at 40°, between the two, with length 1.7, longer than either. On dial 2, X points at 10° and Y at 190°, exactly opposite: the arrows cancel, the length is 0, and that dial no longer says anything about either. On dial 3, both point at 95°: the direction stays 95°, the length is 2, full agreement. The direction of the chart’s arrow is thus a compromise between the fused stars, and the length says how much they agreed on that dial. It is as if each star voted, on each dial, for a direction: the chart keeps the tally of the votes.

There is a shorter, and exact, name for this tally: the centre of gravity. Put an equal weight at the tip of every arrow on a dial; the chart’s arrow points exactly at the centre of gravity of those tips (and is as many times longer than the distance to it as there are stars, which does not matter, because charts are compared by direction). The chart is therefore the centre of gravity of the constellation, dial by dial. The gravity intuition gets two things right: two stars that contradict each other on a dial cancel their weights, and too many stars pull the centre toward the middle of the face and the arrows shorten, whence the capacity below. One thing it does not give: a centre of gravity in three dimensions forgets which masses made it; on a thousand dials it does not. Every fused star can be found again in it, and the next paragraph says how. And we do not leave the representation: the chart is described by the same dials, only its arrows also have a length. If the stars are the points with every arrow of length 1, the surface, the chart is a point inside, as the centre of gravity of points on a circle falls inside the circle. The direction says where, the length says how sure, dial by dial; and everything the probe can do to a star, shift it and compare it, it can do to a chart.

When you look for a star in the chart, you check dial by dial whether the chart’s arrow leans toward the star’s arrow. Dials with long arrows weigh more, dials with short arrows hardly at all; how long the arrows are overall does not matter (a chart with every arrow doubled is the same chart). The length of each dial on its own does matter, and we measured it: if you force the chart back to arrows of length 1, reading loses on new constructions and at depth, because the lengths carry the vote counts. A star fused into the chart has pushed every arrow a little toward itself, and over thousands of dials these small pushes add up to a clear agreement; a star that was not fused matches by chance on some dials and fails to match on others, exactly as in 2.2, and the total agreement is zero. We call what results the sentence’s constellation, and the chart, the document that holds it, is a hologram: all the stars are in it at once, superimposed. You can ask it questions (“do you contain Paul’s star shifted by such-and-such a position?”) and it answers a clear yes or no, but it cannot list its stars for you: from the sum of the arrows you cannot recover the arrows, you can only test candidates.

Two things about the chart must be kept in mind from the start.

First: a drawn star stays shifted by the position of that moment. The probe can jump on anywhere; Paul’s star on the chart no longer moves. Each word carries the probe’s position from the moment it was read, not the position at the end of the sentence.

Second: the chart has a capacity. The more stars you fuse, the smaller a share each one’s vote is of every dial’s arrow, and the harder it is to recognize. With a thousand dials and more, a few dozen terms sit cleanly in a constellation (measured on random terms; exactly where it begins to give way is in the status file, and the structured constellations of sentences, with roles and few candidates, hold longer); hundreds blend together; beyond that, the chart slides toward a middling direction that resembles nothing. It is the only hard limit in the story, and the reason why long texts will be several charts linked together, not a single one.

2.6 The legend and the domains

Now we can say concretely what the legend is, on the example of the lending ledger. The world asks three questions about the participants: who gives (the agent), what (the object), to whom (the recipient), and one about the action itself, which we leave for chapter 8. Whoever builds the world draws at random, once, one star for each question: the agent’s star, the object’s star, the recipient’s star. Three random points in the sky, fixed forever, with no relation between them.

A named star defines a domain of the sky. The agent’s domain is the set of all the stars you obtain by shifting a word’s star by the agent’s star used as a shift (2.3): Paul’s star shifted by the agent’s star, Peter’s star shifted by the agent’s star, and so on, one for each word in the catalogue. It is, if you like, a whole copy of the catalogue, shifted to another part of the sky, with its shape preserved. The object’s domain is another copy, shifted by the object’s star; the recipient’s domain, a third. Because the named stars are random, the three domains have nothing in common and are not confused, with the same practical certainty as in 2.2, although each contains all the words.

“Paul is in the agent’s domain” therefore means exactly: on the chart is drawn Paul’s star shifted by the agent’s star. And for that to be so, the probe must have been at the agent’s star when “Paul” was read: shifting by the probe’s position (2.5) and shifting by the agent’s star are then the same thing. To check who is in the agent’s domain, you shift the chart back by the agent’s star and look at which star from the catalogue has reappeared.

The same goes for the warehouse, only with other named stars: the supplier’s star, the goods’ star, the quantity star, the place star, the time star. “Supplier Alfa delivered 40 boxes to aisle B on Tuesday” should end up on the chart with “Alfa” in the supplier’s domain, “boxes” in the goods’ domain, “40” in the quantity domain, “aisle B” in the place domain and “Tuesday” in the time domain. If the sentence had not said “Tuesday”, the time domain would have stayed empty on the chart, and that can be seen, as chapter 7 shows. For the pilot, “40” and “Tuesday” are words like any other, with stars drawn by lot; that 40 is close to 41 he does not know and need not know (9.1 says how numbers and dates are treated before reading).

The legend is tiny, sits at the base, and is the only thing the world and the pilot have in common. The world uses it to draw the correct constellation of a sentence (“Paul in the agent’s domain, book in the object’s domain, Anna in the recipient’s domain”). The pilot uses it to read his own chart. Nothing else is shared: the pilot does not know the world’s facts, and the world does not know how the probe jumps.

2.7 The scanner: how the chart is read

You want to know who is in the agent’s domain. The pilot shifts the whole chart back by the agent’s star. What had been in the agent’s domain returns to its star in the catalogue, recognizable. What had been in other domains is now shifted by a code that was not its own and ends up at an unrelated point, that is, noise. The result is not a clean answer but a noisy chart: the star of “Paul”, plus noise from “book” and “Anna”.

This chart goes into the scanner. The scanner compares it with every star in the catalogue, dial by dial, as described in 2.5, and reports the closest one, together with two numbers: how close it is, and by how much it is ahead of the next. Because the words’ stars are random and unrelated to one another, almost always a single one stands out, and the rest sit at noise level: “Paul”. When none stands out, or two stand out equally, that is information in itself, which we will use.

(In the four-dial example the scanner would not work: four dials are not enough for the noise to cancel out. With thousands of dials, the error rate falls exponentially with their number and depends on how many stars are fused in the chart and on the size of the catalogue; chapter 8 says where the limit appears. That is why there are thousands.)

With that, the story is described: a sky in which a point is just coordinates, a probe that jumps and reports, and a pilot with a catalogue of the words’ stars, a chart on which he draws the sentence’s constellation, a legend with the world’s named stars, and a scanner. Everything that follows is built from these alone.

3. Reading a sentence

3.1 The step

The pilot’s route book has one key for each kind of word: one kind for all person names, one for all objects, and one kind for each connecting word and each verb of the world (“to”, “from”, “a”, “that”, “gives”…), which are few. The kind of a word is not for the pilot to settle: he takes it from a dictionary of the world (and, for a new word, from an ordinary tagger); chapter 13, step 3, gives the list. The keys are what the pilot has learned, and the only things he has learned. The legend is not in the route book; it is used only when reading the chart, at the end.

At each word, the pilot does the same thing, identically:

  1. He looks at what kind of word it is, takes from the route book the key for that kind and sends it to the probe. The probe jumps and reports its position. The position is, at every moment, the sum of all the keys sent since the beginning of the sentence.
  2. He takes the word’s star from the catalogue, shifts it by the reported position and fuses it into the sentence’s chart.

That is all. The chart at the end is the sentence’s state: its constellation. It keeps no list of the words, nor their order, nor any grammatical tree. Only the words’ stars, each shifted by the position the probe was at when the word was read.

The whole learned part is the route book: one key per kind of word, a few dozen in all (plus the keys on pairs of kinds from 3.5, a few thousand). The words’ stars, that is, the bulk of the vocabulary, are never touched. And the goal of learning, which we detail in chapter 6, is one alone: that the jumps dictated by the route book bring the probe, at the moment a word is read, exactly to the named star the legend requires for that word, that is, that they place it in its domain.

3.2 What it means for two sentences to “end up in the same place”

Take “Paul gives a book to Anna”. The pilot sends the key for a name, then the key for a verb, then for an article, then for a noun, then for a preposition, then again the key for a name. Along the way he drew “Paul” shifted by the position after the first jump, “book” by the one after the fourth, “Anna” by the one after the sixth. Each star on the chart carries the point the probe was at in that moment.

Now “Anna receives a book from Paul”. Other words, other keys, another route. But if the route book is well learned, “Paul” is drawn after a string of jumps (name, receives, a, book, from, name) that brings the probe exactly to the agent’s star, that is, to the same point it was at when it read “Paul” in the first sentence. “Anna” ends up in the recipient’s domain, “book” in the object’s domain.

Two sentences mean the same thing when they draw the same constellation: the same words’ stars, in the same domains. The route does not matter. Three things must be kept apart here. The charts, as drawings, are not identical: function words and different routes leave different traces, and the charts are only similar; that similarity is what training uses. The frames read from them, domain by domain, are equal: the same stars in the same domains. “The same meaning” means the second thing, and that is how it is measured: you compare the two charts through the legend, domain by domain.

3.3 Reading the chart: the frame

You want to know who the agent is. The pilot shifts the chart back by the agent’s star and gives the result to the scanner: “Paul”. He does this for every named star in the legend and has the frame: agent Paul, object book, recipient Anna. The frame is not computed from the chart by some other program; it is the chart, read through the legend.

With each answer come the scanner’s two numbers: how well the winner matches, and how far behind the second is. The difference is the margin. Large margin: a single word was found in that domain. Small margin: two were found, or one was drawn from a point lying between two named stars, so it is half in two domains. The margin is where “I’m not sure” lives.

3.4 Sentences within sentences: the legend is relative

“Maria says that Paul gives a book to Anna.” Our ledger also has in its legend questions for reports: who says? (the speaker) and what is said?. But what Maria says is not a word, it is a whole sentence, with its own agent, object and recipient. If the pilot drew “Paul” in the agent’s domain, we would no longer know whether Paul is the agent of the main sentence or of the reported one. We need a separate set of domains for the inner sentence, which nevertheless has the same names.

The solution is again a star. The legend has, besides the speaker’s star, a star for the role “what is said”: the “reported” star. And here it becomes visible that the named stars are not, in fact, absolute points, but displacements from the sentence’s origin: “the agent’s star is at +the agent’s key from the origin”. For the reported sentence, the origin is different: it is the point the probe reaches by jumping with the “reported” star. The agent’s star of the reported sentence is “origin + reported + agent”: a new point, unrelated to the agent’s star of the main sentence, and yet with the same name in the legend, read from another origin. The whole constellation of the legend shifts at once, with its shape preserved, because shifting preserves shape.

The pilot has learned that the kind of word “that” calls for exactly the “reported” jump. From there, he reads the inner sentence with the same keys from the route book as usual. “Paul” is drawn in the agent’s domain from the reported origin. Maria has remained in the speaker’s domain, relative to the main origin.

To read the inner agent, the pilot undoes the path: he shifts the chart back by the “reported” star (returning to the origin of the main sentence), then by the agent’s star, then the scanner: “Paul”. Two sentences one inside the other (“Maria says that David believes that Paul…”): he undoes two “reported” jumps. The depth of nesting is, literally, the number of origins shifted; we call it the level.

And from here comes something we have measured: because “shift the origin” is the same key every time, a pilot trained on sentences with at most two levels reads a sentence with three correctly, with no new learning. It is the same jump, made once more. Trained up to two levels, he read three without error, while a neural network trained on the same sentences, with the same input, guessed at random at level three. Nothing special was built for this: each level is the whole legend, read from another origin, and origins can be shifted any number of times, because a key can be used any number of times and undone just as many.

3.5 The beacons on the chart: how a word knows which domain to go to

The step in 3.1, in which a word’s key depends only on its kind, has a weakness you see immediately. “Paul gives…” and “Paul receives…” begin with the same word, of the same kind, hence with the same jump. But Paul is the agent in the first and the recipient in the second. When the pilot draws Paul, after the first jump, he has no way of knowing which domain it must reach; what follows has not been read yet.

What happens in practice: the key learned for names settles on a compromise, a point halfway between the agent’s star and the recipient’s star. The sentence is still read correctly, because when you ask “who is the agent?”, Paul is roughly half there (how much exactly depends on how closeness is measured), and nobody else is there at all. But the margin is thin, and with a few such compromises in one sentence the reading becomes muddy.

The full version of the pilot solves this with two extra habits, still without knowing anything about the world.

First: some words put beacons on the chart. We take the verb as the example because it is the easiest to follow; which words actually put beacons is learned, and the result is stated a few paragraphs below. When the pilot reaches “gives”, he notes on the chart one beacon for each kind of word that could follow: “a noun will be at my position + the relative key (verb → noun)”, “a name at my position + the relative key (verb → name)”. Beacons know nothing of roles, because the pilot never sees the legend; the relative keys are on pairs of kinds (the kind of the word that puts the beacon, the kind of the one that takes it), learned and kept in the same route book. That the beacon for a noun comes to fall on the object’s star, and the one for a name on the recipient’s, is the merit of learning, not a rule. A word that arrives later checks whether there is a beacon for its kind; if so, its star is shifted by the beacon’s position, not by the position reported by the probe. Beacons are marks on the chart, not lights lit in the sky, and they are erased at the end of the sentence.

Second: a word that arrives too early is held aside. “Paul” at the beginning of the sentence does not yet have any beacon waiting for it. The pilot does not draw it with a beacon yet; he holds it aside (the other two drawings of 3.6 he makes on the spot) and moves on. When he reaches the first word that puts beacons (“to” in the learned route book, “gives” in our simplified example) and a beacon for a name appears, Paul from aside is the first to match it, and is drawn then, shifted by the beacon’s position (with a relative key of its own, for “placed backward”, different from that of the words after), that is, in its proper domain. This is how the first word receives its domain, although its role becomes visible only later.

Which kinds of words are allowed to put beacons is not for us to decide (with three declared exceptions, at step 8 of chapter 13). Each kind of word has, in the route book, besides its key, a gate: a number between zero (never puts one) and one (always puts one), learned from the same objective as everything else. We would have set verbs and prepositions to one. The pilot chose otherwise, on his own: the words that mark a relation (“to”, “from”, “of”, “that”, a comma) have their gates open; names have them closed; verbs, surprisingly, closed too. The explanation, once seen, is simple: if the words held aside can be placed by what follows, the preposition already says everything the verb would say. The same choice reappeared, without our asking for it, when we trained the pilot on real questions typed by people about tables. The structure of “who opens a frame” (who puts beacons, that is, opens expectations for the words after it) is, in this system, a result, not an input.

3.6 Three drawings for the same word

We now have two ways of finding out which position a word’s star is shifted by: the beacon’s position (precise, but fragile: if a construction is new and a beacon is missing, the word stays aside or is drawn wrongly) and the position reported by the probe, that is, the sum of the generic keys of the preceding words (robust, but unclear, as we saw with Paul). There is also a third, cruder one: a position that depends only on the kind of the preceding word (“after a preposition, a name”), almost never wrong and almost never precise.

Because fusing into the chart is cheap, the pilot does not choose between them. He draws the word’s star three times, shifted by the three positions, and fuses all three copies into the same chart. When the sentence has a familiar shape, the three positions are the same point and the drawing is sharp. When the sentence uses a never-before-seen construction, one of the positions is off, but the other two still bring the star close enough to its domain for the scanner to recognize it. A wrongly learned key no longer destroys the reading; it only blurs it. On new constructions, this redundancy raised correct reading from six in ten to nine in ten.

With this, the pilot is complete: a route book with one key per kind of word, relative keys for beacons, one gate per kind, and the step from 3.1, repeated identically at each word, with three drawings. Everything that follows is about how the route book comes to be good, and what you can do with the chart at the end.

4. Why two sentences of different lengths can mean the same thing

“Paul gives a book to Anna” has six words. “Anna receives a book from Paul” has six as well, in another order. “The book was given to Anna by Paul” has eight. A reader who counts words, or who remembers their order, sees three different things. The pilot sees one. This chapter says why, step by step, because this is where it is clearest what the chart is and what it is not.

Length is a property of the route, not of the chart

The probe makes one jump per word: six jumps for the first sentence, six for the second in another order, eight for the third. The routes have different lengths and orders. But the chart does not record the route; it records where the probe was when each word was read. On the chart there is no “third word” or “after six jumps”. There is Paul’s star shifted by the agent’s star, and that is all. If two routes of different lengths bring the probe to the agent’s star at the moment “Paul” is read, the two charts have exactly the same star in the same place, and nothing on the chart says any longer how long the route to it was.

This is not an accident, it is a choice. None of the three positions the pilot uses to draw a word (3.6) contains the word’s ordinal number in the sentence: the probe’s position is the sum of the preceding keys, the beacon is a displacement from the word that opened the frame, and the third depends only on the kind of the preceding word. At one point we also tried a position that contained the ordinal number; it tied the reading to length and order, that is, to exactly what paraphrases change. We set it aside, then re-measured it with all four drawings fused: it helps a new formulation that puts a role where the training formulations put it and hurts one that does not — a bet on position, not an understanding of the route — and it remained a declared option, outside the reference (chapter 15).

Function words move the probe, but do not enter the frame

“To”, “a”, “from”, “by” are words too, and the pilot treats them like any other: each has a key, the probe jumps, the word’s star is drawn on the chart where it landed. But the points they land on are not named stars. When the pilot reads the chart through the legend, he asks only the named domains; the star of “from” sits at a point unrelated to any of them and answers no question (it may come to carry a domain’s occupancy marker instead, chapter 8). Function words are the scaffolding of the route: without them the probe would not get where it must, but once it is there, they no longer matter. A sentence with more function words has more scaffolding on the chart, at unnamed points, and the same frame.

That is also why we compare charts domain by domain, not as two drawings laid on top of each other. Two charts of the same event are not identical point by point, because the scaffolding differs; they are identical where it matters, in the answers to the legend’s questions.

Different words, the same destination

The hard question remains: why do “gives” and “receives … from” bring the probe to the same named stars, when they are different words, with different keys? Because that is how the route book was learned. In training, the two sentences received the same world’s constellation (6.1), and the pilot’s only freedom was the route book. To satisfy both, he had to find keys under which “name, gives, a, book, to, name” and “name, receives, …, from, name” bring the names to the same stars. Beacons make this possible without contortions: in our example “gives” puts beacons for object and recipient relative to its own place, and “from” puts a beacon for the agent relative to its place. (In the learned route book, beacons are mostly put by relation words, “to”, “from”, not by the verb; where the verb is silent, the other two drawings of 3.6 bring the word to its place.) The word that follows takes the beacon, wherever it is in the sentence. The length between “from” and “Paul” does not matter, because a beacon is a destination, not a number of steps.

The different verbs, “gives” and “receives”, also reach the chart, in the predicate’s domain (a domain of the legend; step 4 of chapter 13 writes it as R[action]). Whether they count as the same event or as two different events is not for the pilot to decide but for the world’s legend, which says which verbs describe the same event (8, “Which verb?”). The pilot only draws them consistently.

One example traced, and one that does not match

“Paul gives a book to Anna”: the probe leaves the origin; at “Paul” the pilot holds it aside (no beacon yet); “to” (and “gives”, in the simplified example) put beacons; Paul takes the beacon that leads to the agent, “book” the one toward the object, “Anna” the one toward the recipient; “a” stays in the scaffolding. The chart: Paul in the agent’s domain, book in the object’s, Anna in the recipient’s, “gives” in the predicate’s.

“Anna receives a book from Paul”: “Anna” held aside; “receives” (in our example) opens beacons; Anna takes the recipient’s beacon (for “receives”, the one who receives is the recipient, and that too is in the route book); “book” takes the object; “from” opens the agent’s beacon; “Paul” takes it. The chart: Paul in the agent’s domain, book in the object’s, Anna in the recipient’s, “receives” in the predicate’s. Six words, the same frame, except for the predicate, which the legend declares the same event.

Now “Anna receives a book from Paul on Tuesday”. Eight words, and one more star: “Tuesday” in the time domain. The two charts no longer say the same thing, and the difference is located exactly: the same answer to who, what, to whom, one more answer to when. It is the third kind of “close” from chapter 8, and it is all that “longer” means when length brings information: one more domain filled, not a bigger chart.

When length does matter, and what the chart does then

Two words instead of one in a domain (“Paul and Peter”): the domain is crowded, with two stars, and that is the correct meaning, not an error. A missing word (“Paul gives a book”, with no recipient): a domain stays empty, and that again is the correct meaning, with the gap in plain sight. A repetition for emphasis (“very, very late”) draws the same star twice in the same place, which makes it a little stronger and does not change the frame; if a world wants to distinguish “late” from “very late”, it must have a domain for it. And the known limit: when the shape of the sentence is revealed only at the end (“…, says Maria”), the length before it has already been drawn relative to the wrong origin; a reader who moves forward cannot repair that, as chapter 8 says.

On constructed worlds, different formulations of the same event, with different numbers of words and different orders, reached the same frame, domain by domain; it is the result the whole document started from. On real text, the signal comes from the questions about tables, where questions of different lengths led to the same column in eight cases out of ten.

5. The same word in several worlds: polysemy, the worlds of knowledge, ontologies

A word like “bank” means one thing in a text about finance and another in one about a river. “Energy” means one thing in physics and another in nutrition. “Field” means one thing in algebra, another in agriculture, another in physics. A reader with a single star for each word seems unable to cope. This chapter says how things actually stand, and what we do not claim.

A word about vocabulary first: in this book, domain means the region of a role (the agent’s domain, the quantity domain). For a field of knowledge or activity, finance, physics, a warehouse, we say world, because that is what we called it in chapter 1: the thing the sentences are about, with its list of questions.

The star is the word, not the sense

The star of “bank” is one, fixed, the same everywhere. That is not a weakness; it is a consequence of the definition in 1.1. The meaning of a sentence is “which questions it answers, and with what”. The meaning of a word, by the same definition, is “which questions it answers, in the world in which it is read”. In the world of finance, “bank” answers questions like “who granted the loan?”; in the world of geography, “where did the boat stop?”. The same star, different legends, different domains. The pilot never has to decide what kind of bank it is; it only has to place it in the right domain of the world it works in. What a bank is, in that world, the database knows, and that is its business.

Put differently: polysemy does not live in the stars, it lives in the legends. Two senses of the same word are two legends in which it answers different questions. That is also why the stars can stay random and fixed (chapter 8): they have nothing to learn about sense, because sense is elsewhere.

What the legend alone does not resolve is a double sense in the same world and the same domain: “at the bank”, as a place, may be the river bank or the bank’s building, and if the star and the domain are the same, the chart does not tell them apart. There, disambiguation remains a matter of context, of a sense identifier in the legend, or of the database. The legend narrows ambiguity; it does not remove it.

When the kind of the word is ambiguous

There is one ambiguity that concerns the pilot directly: when the same word can be of two kinds, with two different keys. “Run” is a noun or a verb; “book”, likewise. The kind decides the jump, so here the pilot does have to choose. We measured two ways. The first: the pilot takes the word’s most frequent kind and moves on. It works almost perfectly, for a reason worth stating: a word’s place on the chart is decided mostly by the beacons of the words around it (the preposition before, the verb that follows), not by its own key; “book” after “a” lands in the object’s domain anyway, because “a” placed it there. The second way, drawing halfway between the two kinds until things clear up, costs on the nouns and we do not use it. What remains for the truly ambiguous cases is the scanner: at reading time it tries both kinds and reports the margin between them; when it is small, the chart says, as for any crowded domain, “I do not know whether this is a thing or an action”. Lexical ambiguity asks for nothing new.

The world is a first-class object

The natural question: if “bank” means something different in two worlds, should not the jump the probe makes at “bank” also be different in each world? Yes, and so it is; it is worth saying plainly what belongs to the world and what does not.

The only thing all worlds share is the sky, with the words’ stars: the star of “bank” is one, because it is the identity of the word, not of the sense. Everything else belongs to the world. A world has its legend (which questions it asks, with their named stars); its kinds of words (in the world of finance “bank” is of the kind of institutions, in geography of the kind of places, and the kind decides the key); its route book (the keys are learned on that world’s sentences, so the jump at “bank” is different in each world, because the kind is different and the route book is different); its background for the remainder (the remainder is what stays unexplained on the chart after reading, defined in chapter 8; the background, how much remainder an understood sentence leaves there); and its database, where the facts live. The pilot keeps one route book for each world it works in. The world is, therefore, a first-class object in the story, not a vague context: it has a name, it has parts, it is trained separately and it can be changed without touching the other worlds. When in ordinary language we say “scientific field” or “training domain”, in the story we say world, and it is a citizen with full rights.

There is also a second way, which we have not built: the world itself as a star. Just as the “reported” key shifts the whole legend to another origin, a world’s star could shift the legend of finance to one part of the sky and that of geography to another, with a single pilot and a single route book. It would be more economical, but it would require the kinds of words to be shared across worlds, and that is exactly where polysemy lives; the superposition of kinds from the previous section would then be needed. It is a research direction with a clear trade-off: one route book per world is simple and isolates the worlds; a world star is economical and links them.

Several worlds at once

A system installed in an enterprise usually has several worlds: invoices, deliveries, leave requests. Each has its legend and its route book, as said. A new sentence is read with every route book at once (one “pilot” per world, if you like); each costs microseconds. Then the charts are compared: with the pilot of the right world, the domains fill with a clear margin and the residual (the remainder, chapter 8) is small; with the others, the stars fall outside the legend and the residual is large. The world of a sentence is recognised by how well its legend fits, like a key in a lock. No separate “what is this text about” classifier is needed: the fit is the classifier, and it comes with the same numbers, margin and residual, as any other reading.

Two limits, stated openly. When two worlds share vocabulary and have similar questions, the fit is weaker, and how much weaker is measured, on constructed worlds with controlled overlap. And when a single sentence mixes worlds, as literature, metaphor and jokes do, the pilot has nothing to fit: it reports a large residual in every legend, that is, “this belongs to no world I know”. That is not a promise we make; literature is not the target.

Do we discover ontologies?

No, and it matters not to give that impression. An ontology, in the sense used in computing, is a list of kinds of things and of relations between them, with names. A world’s legend is a small ontology, a few dozen lines, written (by people, or proposed by a model and approved by people; 9.3), not discovered from data. The kinds of words come from a dictionary (and from a tagger, for new words), not from training; we checked that a statistical grouping of the corpus gives the same kinds, and we do not claim them. What truly emerges from training is one thing: which kinds of words open frames (the gates), and that is less than an ontology.

What we do, instead, is populate a given ontology: reading a whole corpus produces a list of frames, that is, facts with roles, that is, a knowledge graph relative to the legend: who delivered what, to whom, when, how many times. All the stars that ever reached the supplier’s domain are, taken together, that world’s vocabulary for “supplier”; nobody wrote it, it resulted from reading. It is an inventory, not a theory, but an inventory verifiable sentence by sentence.

And there is one more thing, which belongs to ontology without being its discovery: the chart can signal that a question is missing. If a world’s sentences systematically say something the legend does not ask about (every delivery has a time, but the legend has no “when”), those stars always fall outside the legend, and they fall the same way: the residual is not noise, it has a direction. The signal is “something is being said here that I do not ask about”, and the answer is a new question in the legend, added by a person and then learned by the pilot with its route book. This is the loop of 9.4, “a boundary that learns”, stated concretely: the ontology is not discovered by the system, it is maintained by people with the system’s signal.

The same words, different theories

There remains the most interesting case in the question we started from: the same word in two theories, “mass” in Newton and in relativity, “energy” in thermodynamics and in nutrition. In our terms, two theories are two legends: the same words answer different questions and stand in different relations. A text read with both legends gives two charts, and the difference between them, domain by domain, says exactly where the two theories use the same word differently. It is a direction, not a tool: it requires legends written for theories, which nobody has done yet, and texts to fill them. But it is the same mechanism as the difference between two versions of a regulation in 11, applied to something harder.

6. How the pilot learns

6.1 What he receives: the world’s constellation

Nobody tells the pilot which sentences are paraphrases. But it is not “without any supervision” either, and this must be said plainly. Each training sentence comes with a world’s constellation: the world, which knows the event and has the legend, draws the correct chart itself: “Paul’s star shifted by the agent’s star, the book’s by the object’s star, Anna’s by the recipient’s star, fused”. In some of the worlds we built, the world’s constellation describes the event; in others, its consequence (“Anna has the book”). In all of them, the legend (the roles) is fixed in advance by whoever built the world; what the pilot learns is how to get the probe to its named stars, word by word.

Training is then a single game, repeated: the pilot reads the sentence with his route book, draws his chart, and his chart is compared, dial by dial, with the world’s constellation. The better they match, the better; the keys in the route book are adjusted slightly, in the direction that would have brought the two charts closer. That is all. Supervision is therefore per sentence and structural (a constellation with roles given in advance), not a list of which sentences mean the same thing.

6.2 Why this forces meaning

Two sentences about the same event receive the same world’s constellation. So they are obliged to draw the same chart. The pilot’s only freedom is the route book: one key per kind of word and one per pair of kinds, a few thousand keys in all, none per word. With a few thousand keys per kind, memorising sentences one by one is practically excluded; what it can find are regularities, and that is exactly what we want. So the only way he can satisfy both demands is to find keys under which both routes bring the probe to the same named stars. Equivalence is not labelled on pairs; it is induced by the shared targets, and it is the solution of a constraint.

We tried the obvious alternatives and they failed instructively. If you ask the pilot to predict the next word, like a language model, all routes become alike and the domains disappear: nothing forces him to separate the roles. If you give him a memory that looks up the world’s constellation starting from his chart, he predicts perfectly and aligns nothing: a lookup can take three different charts to the same answer without ever making them the same chart. Only when the pilot must produce the world’s constellation through a fixed, rigid operation (his chart, compared directly) is he forced to make the charts coincide. Rigidity is the essence.

6.3 What is learned, and how little

A few dozen keys (one per kind of word), a few thousand relative keys for beacons and for pairs of neighbouring kinds, and one gate per kind. All of it is on the order of ten to fifteen megabytes at the sizes we use, the size of a phone photograph; most of it is the keys on pairs of kinds. The words’ stars, the bulk of the vocabulary, are random and fixed. The named stars in the legend are random and fixed. The whole trainable part is the route book.

That is why training takes minutes, why a new word costs nothing, and why the pilot knows nothing about the world: he has nowhere to keep facts. Facts live in the world, which in an installed system is a database or a reasoning engine, where they can be inspected and corrected. The pilot holds only how the language places words into domains.

6.4 Where the correct constellations come from

Section 6.1 said that every training sentence comes with its correct constellation. The practical question remains: who writes it? The pilot does not learn from text; he learns from text together with its answers to the world’s questions. Those answers have three sources, and they should be named, because each has a different price.

The first is the constructed world: in our experiments, the world first chooses the event (Paul, the book, Anna) and only then produces the sentence, from templates. The answers are known before the sentence, not extracted from it. That is how we measured the mechanisms; it is not how a real system is trained.

The second is the structured data that already sits next to text: an invoice and its description, a support ticket and its fields, a log message and the database record, a question and the query that answered it. There the answers are free and exact, and nobody has to write them; the real dataset we used is of this kind.

The third, for a domain where the text comes alone, is a large model put in the role of a teacher: it reads every sentence of a sample and writes the answers to the legend’s questions, in the format the pilot would read. Three things must be said about this teacher. It works only at training time; at reading time the pilot needs no large model (if an architecture uses one on the uncertain requests, as in chapter 11, its output is re-read by the pilot). Its errors are the pilot’s ceiling, in two ways: the pilot cannot learn what the teacher got wrong, and we measure agreement with the teacher, so its mistakes appear to us as the pilot’s — which is why the trained pilot re-reads the training sentences and, wherever it contradicts the teacher with a large margin, the case is reviewed, and the rate of these disagreements is reported as the estimate of label noise. And it works on a sample, not on everything the pilot will ever read: the pilot generalizes to formulations it has not seen and to new words without retraining, and how well it does on unseen formulations is the number that says how large the sample must be.

What the method does not do, and does not claim: to learn meaning from text without anyone having answered any question. Its thesis is that meaning is the answer to given questions; someone has to answer the first ones.

7. What remains on the chart after a sentence, and what it is good for

After a sentence has been read, a single chart remains: its constellation. On it, recoverable but not listed, are the word stars, each shifted by the position in which it was found. Next to it is the legend: the named stars. And between the chart and the legend there are distances that can be read without understanding anything: how much of the constellation is explained by domains, how cleanly each domain is occupied, whether any domain the world wants filled looks empty.

This is the property on which everything else rests. The chart is not measured against itself; it is measured against the legend. So the pilot can say, without understanding a sentence, that it has not understood it, and in what way:

  • Nothing recognised in a domain. You shift the chart back by the recipient’s star and no star in the catalogue stands out. Either the sentence did not say who the recipient is, or it said so with an unknown word; to tell the two apart, a small extra device is needed, described in chapter 8. Either way: recipient unrecognised.
  • A crowded domain, or one half occupied. Two candidates, a small margin. Ambiguity, and the pilot can name them both.
  • Stars left unexplained. You take each recognised role, reconstruct the constellation these recognitions would produce, and subtract it from the real chart. What remains are stars the legend could not place in any domain: words read, jumps made, drawings placed, but not in named domains. The sentence is about something outside the world, or it is incoherent, or it uses a construction for which the route book has no keys. Measurable as the size of that remainder.
  • A key that could not be used. A word without a kind (any word receives a new star; only words with a kind receive a key); the route breaks there, and every drawing after it carries a position reached with a guessed key. Visible as a collapse of the margin for all the roles after that word.

Two cautions, because they are easily forgotten. A clean reading is not a correct reading: a word can be drawn confidently in the wrong domain, and how often that happens must be measured, not assumed. And “unrecognised” is a statement about the pilot’s legend and catalogue, not about the sentence.

A language model has no legend. Its state is a point with nothing to measure it against, and it answers with the same fluency whether its route passed through named domains or through emptiness. Ours reports where the route left the legend. Every application below is this one property, in a different setting.

8. Phenomena you can follow on the chart

Everything in this chapter happens inside the story described and can be measured there. None of it is a rule written by us. We go slowly, because these are the phenomena that build intuition, and because each is also a diagnostic that can be shown to a user.

The empty domain, and why “empty” is harder than it looks

You shift the chart back by the recipient’s star and compare the result with every star in the catalogue. Either one stands out with a clear margin (recipient: Anna), or none does.

Here is a trap it is easy to fall into. You might hope to tell “nobody was found in this domain” from “someone was found whom I do not know” by how much light comes back. You cannot, and it is worth saying why. Shifting is rigid: it shifts the whole chart, every star in every domain, at once. What comes back always has the “weight” of the entire constellation, with the stars of the other domains scattered as noise. The amount tells you nothing; only recognition does. “Paul gives a book” and “Paul gives a book to Zxyq” look the same from the recipient’s domain: no known star stands out.

To tell them apart, a small, explicit device is needed, standard in this family of systems: a fixed marker, always the same, which means “something was drawn here”. The world fuses it into its constellation beside every answer; the pilot does not draw it himself, but, for his chart to resemble the world’s, he learns to put it there from the function words: “to” appears whenever there is a recipient, and its star lands, through the route book, exactly where the world puts the recipient’s marker (step 4 of chapter 13 shows the calculation). Then the recipient’s domain can be asked two separate things: is the marker here (something was drawn), and which star is here (and do I know it). “Paul gives a book”: no marker, nothing drawn, recipient not given. “Paul gives a book to Zxyq”: marker present, a star not in the world’s catalogue, recipient present, unknown. The device is cheap; without it, there is a single reading: recipient unrecognised.

The crowded domain

Sometimes two stars come back from the same domain with similar strength. There are two ways this happens, and they look the same from the outside, but not from the inside.

One: the sentence really did put two things there. “Paul and Peter give a book to Anna.” The agent’s domain has two stars fused on the chart, and both come back at roughly half strength. It is not confusion; it is a set, correctly drawn, and the pilot must report both. The chart alone does not, however, distinguish “Paul and Peter” from “Paul or Peter, we do not know which”: both would be two stars in the same domain. The distinction requires a mark in the legend, one for “and”, another for “one of two”, just as the occupancy marker distinguishes empty from unknown.

Two: a single word was drawn from a point lying between two named stars. This is the fate of the first word with the simple pilot of 3.1: “Paul” is read before the verb, nobody yet knows whether he gives or receives, and the probe’s position is halfway between the agent’s star and the recipient’s. Ask the agent’s domain and Paul comes back at half strength; ask the recipient’s domain and again at half. The reading works, because nobody else is in either of the two, but the margin is thin.

Beacons repair the second case and leave the first alone. When the word that sets beacons arrives, Paul, held aside, is drawn from the position of the appropriate beacon and comes back at nearly full strength (his drawing from the probe’s position stays at the compromise address). Before-and-after shows up as a jump in margin; in our measurements, about threefold. The crowded domain of the first kind stays crowded, because it must.

Stars outside the legend, or how the pilot knows a sentence makes no sense to it

You read every domain, take the recognised stars, and reconstruct the constellation they would produce: each star shifted by the named star of its domain, all fused. You subtract the reconstructed constellation from the real chart. What remains is the part of the sentence the legend could not explain. We call it the remainder (below also the residual: the same thing), and it deserves an explanation without mystery, because most of the diagnostics rest on it.

The remainder does not appear at reading time. Reading a domain, shifting the chart back and running the scanner, is an exact operation: shifting is an isometry, everything moves intact, nothing is “left behind”. That is also why you cannot tell from how much comes back whether the domain is occupied; the whole chart always comes back, shifted. The remainder appears at comparison: once you have read every domain, you have the frame (agent Paul, object book, recipient Anna); you draw again, from scratch, the chart that exactly these answers would produce; and you subtract it from the real chart. It is a decomposition, as in statistics: chart = (what the legend explains) + (remainder). The frame is the explained part, the remainder is what is left. The explained part says what was understood; the remainder says how much of the sentence could not be placed in any named domain.

Three clarifications make it honest. First, the remainder is never zero: function words drawn at unnamed positions, the extra copies from the three drawings and the noise of superposition leave a background that sits in no domain; the background is measured once, per world, on understood sentences, and what counts is how much the remainder exceeds it. Second, the remainder is computed after the reconstructed constellation is brought to the chart’s scale: charts are compared by direction, not by length, so a chart three times “longer” is not three times less understood. And a third, measured: the size of the remainder is not a reliable detector of a question missing from the legend. We removed a role from the legend and trained without it; the sentences that expressed it did not have a larger remainder but a smaller one, because the background is dominated by function words and a longer sentence has a “longer” chart. What gave the missing role away was the direction of the remainder: the remainders of sentences with the same hidden answer point the same way, and they cluster by it with no vector and no label. When we say below that “the remainder rises”, we mean sentences the legend does not explain at all; for a missing question, the detector is direction. With these clarifications: for an understood sentence the remainder stays at the background; for one not understood it rises, and where it comes from depends on the reason:

  • Known words, in an order never travelled. Each word used its key, but the sequence of jumps took the probe to points the legend does not name. The stars are real, they would be recognised if you knew what to shift the chart back by, but no named star is the right one. This is the shape of a new construction: the pieces are familiar, the route is not.
  • Unknown words. Each is a new, random star, shifted by wherever the probe happened to be. Random stars at arbitrary points: they add weight to the chart and contribute nothing to any domain. This is the shape of nonsense, or of a foreign vocabulary.
  • A sentence about something else. The words are known, the construction ordinary, and the legend simply has no domains for this kind of event, because the world the pilot was trained on does not contain it. The stars land at points perfectly well formed and perfectly nameless. This is the shape of a question from outside the world.

Notice what this measurement is and is not. It is not “the sentence makes no sense”. It is “my keys do not take this sentence into my legend”. A sentence can be full of meaning for a person and still fall outside the legend of a pilot trained on invoices. The pilot knows the limits of its own legend, and precisely this kind of ignorance is useful.

The broken key

An unknown word is not just unrecognised; it has no key either, because it has no kind. The pilot, on reaching it, sends a guessed key, and everything it reads afterwards is drawn from positions that carry that wrong key. The drawings before the unknown word are good; those after it are displaced by an unknown amount. Ask for the roles and you see the pattern: those filled before the break come back clean, those after it, at noise level. The break can be located: the pilot can say I lost the thread at word six. The three drawings soften this, because they do not all break at once; but something after the break is always blurred.

Noise, and why the chart has a capacity

Every fused star adds a little noise to every domain. When you shift the chart back by the agent’s star, you also bring back, faintly, everything that was drawn in the other domains, shifted by a code that was not its own and therefore scattered at random. With few stars, the scatter is well below the signal. With many, it accumulates, and at some point the word in the agent’s domain is no longer clearly above the scatter from everything else.

How many “many” is depends on how many dials the coordinates have. With a thousand-odd, tens of stars are clean, a hundred or two still readable with care, and beyond that the chart slides towards the middle, where nothing is recognisable any more. It is not a defect; it is the geometry. This is why a document is not one chart but several charts linked by keys, each holding what one sentence or one event drew. The “reported” jump is already such a link: the inner sentence is its own constellation, shifted whole to another origin, within the chart of the outer one.

Negation as a star, and where its scope lives

“Paul does not give the book to Anna.” The legend provides a star for negation. The question is which positions carry it. If all the drawings of the event carry the negation star, the negation belongs to the sentence: it is not the case that Paul gives. If only the drawing of “Paul” carries it, before the agent’s star, the negation belongs to the agent: it is not Paul who gives. If only Anna’s drawing carries it, the negation belongs to the recipient: it is not Anna who receives, and who does receive remains an open question (not “nobody”: “nobody” is a word of its own, with its own drawing, which the legend treats separately).

The scope of a negation is therefore not a label; it is which words were drawn from positions that contain the negation star. You read the chart back and see: shift the whole chart by the negation and then read the domains; if all the stars reappear in their places, the negation belonged to the whole sentence; if only Paul reappears, it belonged to him; if nothing reappears, the negation was not there. The pilot reads which of the cases it is, for eight ways of saying the negation, without a rule for any of them.

And here is why a bag of words cannot do this. For the bag, “not” is a word like any other: the negated sentence and the affirmed one share all their words but one and are nearly identical. In our story, the negated sentence has its stars shifted by one extra key, and the two constellations are far apart, almost like two random points. The difference between “the same words” and “the same meaning” is the difference between counting the stars and asking where they were drawn.

Ambiguity held open

“Every child reads a few books.” The same few books for all, or each his own? The sentence does not say. A reader forced to choose will choose, and will be wrong half the time without knowing it.

Words of this kind, “every”, “a few”, “a certain”, “no”, the ones that say how many things a noun refers to, are called quantifiers, and the two meanings are two readings: which quantifier is “on top” of the other. In our story the pilot neither chooses nor hesitates: he draws each quantifier like any other word and, in addition, draws its noun a second time, shifted by a learned relative key of the quantifier word (the pilot does not see the legend; the key is in the route book). The legend, for its part, holds two named stars this second drawing must reach through learning: one for the words that force a reading (“each”, “a certain”) and a neutral one for those that leave it open (“every”, “a few”); articles (“a”, “the”) draw nothing extra. The world then reads the chart with a simple rule: if it sees the “force” star on one of the phrases, that reading is the right one; if it sees a single neutral star, it is the only quantifier; if it sees two neutral stars and none that forces, it reports both readings. The pilot draws; the legend reasons.

We measured three things, on sentences with one and with two quantified phrases, including at a reporting depth the pilot had not seen: that the forced reading is found; that on unambiguous sentences “both” never appears by mistake; and that ambiguous sentences, which the pilot had not seen at all in training, are reported “both”. A later word that fits only one of the readings (“the same books”) could close the other; that is not built. One clarification: “both” does not also remember the two whole meanings, with the link between the choices; it is a representation of doubt, not two charts.

The level never seen, and the key that does not exist

Why can the pilot read a sentence with three levels when it was trained on two? Because “shift the origin” is a key: twice is a key, three times is a key, and undoing it is the same number of steps back. The third level has nothing new; it is the same jump, once more.

And why did it fail, the first time, to read a negation inside a reported sentence? Because that combination required a chaining of keys the pilot had never performed: negation-at-level. Not a new key, but a new chaining, for which it had no learned relative key. Once that chaining had appeared in training at one level, it read it at the next level without further training. The pattern deserves to be stated for what it is: the pilot composes what it has keys for. A chaining it has never performed is not blurred, it is missing. This is what “generalises one level beyond what it was trained on, once the chaining has been seen one level down” means, and it is also exactly where the method fails: on a combination of known operators it has never seen together.

A new construction: a missing beacon

“The book, Paul gives it to Anna.” Known words, an order never travelled. No key is wrong in itself: every word has its kind and the kind’s key. What breaks is something else, and it is worth saying exactly what, for each of the three drawings. The beacon: the new construction opens the expectations in another order; “the book” arrives before any beacon and may be taken by an unsuitable one or by none; the drawing by beacons is the most fragile here. The probe’s position: the sum of the keys is a new combination of known keys, hence a point the probe has never reached; not nonsense, but not exactly at the named star either; the drawing is displaced. The kind of the previous word: “after an article, a noun” is the same as in any sentence; the drawing is crude, but untouched.

With a single drawing, the one by beacons, the word would be lost. With three, the other two bring it close enough to its domain for the scanner to recognise it, with a smaller margin. The reading is blurred, not destroyed. That is the entire content of the result about redundancy: a new construction costs part of the margin instead of all of it.

Structure announced afterwards

“Paul gives a book to Anna, says Maria.” By the time “says Maria” arrives, the first sentence has already been drawn on the chart, relative to the main origin, as a plain assertion. The “reported” jump comes too late: the stars are already drawn, and a pilot that only moves forward has no way to lift them and shift them to another origin after the fact. The same goes for a comma that reorders a sentence, and for any construction whose shape is revealed only at the end.

It is a real limit of forward reading, and we declare it as such. It is also, precisely, what “holding aside” does for single words, extended to whole sentences; the only place where the idea that a word may need to be re-placed by what follows survives, in the form the data forces on it.

Order, and what commuting keys cannot tell apart

The keys commute. “Reported” then negation takes the probe exactly where negation then “reported” takes it. So the inner sentence in “Maria says that it is not the case that P” and the one in “it is not the case that Maria says that P” have the same origin. The two sentences can still be told apart, but only because something else differs: in the second, Maria’s drawing also carries the negation star. Where nothing else differs, the order of two operators is lost.

This is the deepest structural limit of the story as we use it, and it must be declared as such: a position remembers which keys were used, as a multiset (repeating the same key counts, order does not), not as a sequence; the paths through the sky form a grid, not a tree. It is the same fact, seen from another side, that gives the pilot the freedom to compose (“the same jump once more” requires no learning). In the variants we have weighed, keys that do not commute, through permutations, or a separate “reported” star for each level, order is bought with part of that freedom. It is not a mathematical impossibility (there are operations that preserve order and compose), but a trade-off of the concrete variants. We measured what the commuting version can and cannot do: where another drawing differs (the speaker, a clause covered by only one of the operators), the two orders are distinct and read correctly; where nothing differs, they are not. And we found a relative of the same limit, more important in practice: a word that comes before two operators that cover it — “the operator must not open the valve” — is placed by the first of them, and with hard gates its address would never learn of the second. We measured it on rendered sentences: the frame comes out right, including at an unseen depth, because the operators’ gates open partly, the subject’s address becomes a mixture, and which operators there are and what they cover is carried by the words after them; the cost stays on the subject’s address (taken alone, it does not tell the two orders apart) and on a single form, “may not”, below the criterion at the unseen depth. The remedy, placement through all the operators that follow, not only the first, is a change to the reader that we do not make until it is needed. The same limit is the question posed by languages with the operator at the end, the final negation of German (“… nicht”), the verb-final order of Japanese, where nearly every sentence has a word before the operator that covers it; a toy world with the verb at the end is the natural test of this limit, and it is not built.

Which verb? The predicate is a domain too

“Paul gives a book to Anna”, “Paul lends a book to Anna”, “Paul sells a book to Anna”: the same participants, the same domains, three different events. If the chart holds only who was found in the agent’s, the object’s and the recipient’s domains, it cannot tell them apart. The verb itself must be drawn in a domain of its own, the predicate’s domain, and the legend must have its star. In our early worlds it did not: giving and selling had received the same world’s constellation and were therefore the same event for the pilot. That was a gap in the way we built those worlds, not a property of the story; the reference world now has the predicate’s star, and the verb is read from it like any other answer. It also answers a natural question: “gives” and “receives” arrive at the same constellation because the world says they describe the same event; “lends” arrives at a different one because the world says so. The world decides what counts as the same event; the pilot only has to be consistent with it.

Several events, and the same name twice

One chart, one event. If two events are fused into a single chart with nothing to keep them separate, the participants mix: the agent of the first and the agent of the second are both in the agent’s domain, and nobody can say any more who did what to whom. The story has the obvious remedy, each event shifted to its own origin before fusing, exactly as a reported sentence is kept separate from the one reporting it; but we have used it only for reporting, not for “Paul gives a book to Anna and Peter lends her a pen”. Until it becomes general, the honest range of a chart is a single event. The same goes for a name that appears twice in different roles: they are two drawings in two domains, they can be told apart, but the margin drops a little, as it drops whenever the same star is drawn in two places.

Three kinds of “close”

Because the chart keeps both the word stars and the domains, two sentences can be close to each other in three ways, and the chart tells them apart.

  • The same stars in the same domains: the sentences mean the same thing. “Paul gives a book to Anna” and “Anna receives a book from Paul”.
  • The same stars in different domains: the sentences are about the same things and mean something else. “Paul gives a book to Anna” and “Anna gives a book to Paul”. A bag of words sees no difference at all; here the two constellations are far apart.
  • Different stars in the same domains: the same shape, different content. “Paul gives a book to Anna” and “Peter lends a car to Maria”. The same frame, different fillers; structurally equivalent.

A similarity score crushes the three into a single number. Our chart keeps them as three answers, and a comparison of two texts can say what kind of closeness it found and in which domains.

What the pilot does not remember

Between sentences, nothing. While it reads a sentence, the pilot has a small working set, the beacons on the chart and the words held aside, but it discards it at the end; what survives is only the chart. The next sentence starts a new one, and the probe sets out again from the origin. It is a deliberate emptiness: the pilot has no memory in which to accumulate beliefs, and so it cannot drift or confabulate over the course of a conversation. Memory is external: the frames read are stored in a database or a reasoning engine, and a later sentence that refers back (“she reads it”) is resolved by linking its chart to the stored frames, through a search, not through a memory inside the pilot. That linking is the precondition for anything at the scale of a document. The next section says how it is done.

The pronoun: a borrowed star

“Paul gives a book to Anna. She reads it.” For the pilot, “she” is a word like any other: it has its star in the catalogue, it is of the kind “feminine pronoun”, and it lands on the chart of the second sentence in the agent’s domain. The chart is correct and, at the same time, does not say who the agent is: it says she. Nothing in the sky can say more, because the star of “she” has no connection to Anna’s star; the connection between them is not in the language, it is in what was said before.

That is why reference is not resolved in the sky but at the base, by search, just like spelling mistakes. The base keeps the constellations of recent sentences, an atlas of the conversation. When the pilot meets a pronoun, it searches the atlas for a word star compatible with the pronoun’s kind: “she” calls for a feminine singular name, “that one” for a thing already mentioned, “the other” for the second member of a pair of the same kind. It searches from the most recent sentence backwards, and looks first in the domains the verb of the current sentence expects (after “reads”, an agent of the kind person). The star found, Anna’s, is borrowed: it is drawn on the chart of the second sentence in place of the star of “she”, and the chart now says “agent: Anna”. If two stars fit equally well (two feminine candidates equally recent), the pilot does not choose: it fuses both into the agent’s domain, with the “one of two” mark, so that the chart does not read them as “Paul and Peter”. A following sentence, or the world, can close it: the world knows that a book does not read, the pilot does not, and need not.

The division is the same as everywhere in the story: the pilot proposes by form (the kind of word, recency, the expected domains), the world decides by facts. None of this requires the pilot to understand who Anna is.

The mechanism is precise and asks the pilot to learn nothing new: the pieces already exist (the catalogue, the domains, the fusing of candidates, the search). What is measured is how often it chooses well on real text, and how often it says it cannot choose when it truly cannot.

Why the word stars are random, and must stay that way

You might think learned stars would be better: “cat” next to “dog”, “book” next to “novel”. But in this story, closeness between word stars is interference. If “Paul” and “Peter” were close, the agent’s domain could not tell them apart; the margin on which every diagnostic above rests would shrink. Random stars are unrelated to one another, and that is what makes each domain readable. Semantic closeness between words belongs elsewhere: to the way words are grouped into kinds (which decides their keys) and to the knowledge the world has about what a cat is. The pilot’s job is to draw the right star in the right domain; it needs the stars to be distinct, not similar. This also settles spelling mistakes: a misspelled word is matched to its correct form before reading, through a search that can use letter patterns, and the star of the correct form, random like all the others, is the one drawn. Tolerance lives in the search, never in the stars.

9. Where it leads

9.1 Assistants allowed to say “I don’t know”

Take a single world: a timetable, an inventory, a product catalogue, high-school physics once numbers and units have named stars of their own. Write its legend: the roles its questions can have, each with its star. Train the route book on questions from that world, paired with their correct constellations (a query, a search, a computed answer; where they come from, 6.4). Then run.

A user types. Word by word, the probe jumps and the pilot draws; you can literally show the frame filling in as they type, because each step is a few thousand turns of the dials and the whole sentence costs microseconds on an ordinary processor. When the sentence ends, the frame is read off the chart and handed to the database or the reasoning engine. The answer comes derived, and the derivation can be shown beside it: here is what I understood, here is what I asked, here is what came back.

When the chart does not match the legend, the assistant does not guess. A domain left unrecognised: “you didn’t say which product”. A crowded domain: “the March invoice or the April one?”. Stars left unexplained: “I have no information about that”. A word without a kind: “I don’t know the term X”. Each refusal is a distance read off the chart, not a hand-written policy; and each is a statement about the pilot’s own legend, not a verdict on the user.

Such an assistant is slower-witted than a chatbot. It makes no conversation, has no opinions, knows nothing outside the legend. In return, its answers are restricted to what the legend and the database contain, each comes with a margin, and each can be reproduced and verified. It can still be confidently wrong, drawing a word in the wrong domain with a clear margin; how often that happens is a number to be measured for each world, and the assistant must be judged by it. There is no language model in the reading loop (a small one may be called on incoherent sentences, as 9.1 says, and what it writes is re-read), no GPU, no cloud call. It can run on a phone or in a browser, and it can afford to verify every message.

Messy input is handled in layers, and without pretence. Spelling mistakes are mostly absorbed by classical correction before reading, a search that takes the wrong form to the correct one; the star drawn is always that of the correct form. Also before reading, numbers, dates and identifiers are brought to one form (“forty” and “40”, “Tuesday” and “14 Oct”, “invoice 4471”): to the pilot they are words like any other, each with its own star, drawn on the spot if never seen before; he knows which domain “40” reached, not that 40 is close to 41. Comparing and computing with values is the world’s job, after reading. When a sentence is truly incoherent, a small language model can be set to rewrite it, but it does not have the last word. The rewrite is read too; every star recognised in the original must still be present; everything the rewrite added is marked as an assumption and put to the user as a question. The assistant does not claim to have verified a rewrite of something it did not understand. It limits what the rewrite can break, and asks.

9.2 A verifier beside the large models

The other direction. A large language model reads a complex document and proposes a formal reading: a summary, an extraction, a set of facts, a logical form. It knows far more language than we do. And, sometimes, it misses a sentence, swaps two roles, or silently adds a plausible detail.

We read the same document with our pilot, separately, and compare the constellations domain by domain. Four verdicts, not three. Agreement: the same star in the same domain; two separate readings agree, which means neither that the reading is true nor that their errors are independent, especially when both learned from texts produced by the same models. Compatible, incomplete: one says “Tuesday”, the other “Tuesday morning”; not a contradiction, a detail more. Contradiction: different stars in the same domain, neither refining the other; we say in which domain, not who is right. Unverifiable: our chart is uncertain, a domain unrecognised or crowded, or the sentence is outside the legend. The last verdict is the valuable one: a language model can be taught to abstain, but its abstention is not tied to a structure a person can check; ours says in which domain and why.

For this we too must be trained on that world; not to match the large model’s coverage, but to have our own constellation of the text to compare against. The part of a document we can confirm or contradict grows with training, and the part marked unverifiable shrinks. The verifier is honest at every point about which is which. In regulated industries, that honesty is worth more than one more point of accuracy.

The wider picture deserves saying too, as a direction, not a result. In Kahneman’s terms, a large model is a system 1: fast, fluent, with enormous coverage and no guarantee on any particular answer. What this book describes is a way of building, for a given world, a cheap system 2: a small, deterministic reader with a verifiable frame, which runs in microseconds and says where it did not understand. And it is built from system 1: the large model writes the answers on a sample (6.4), the pilot learns them and then reads all the rest on its own. The loop can be closed in the other direction too. The first half is built: where the pilot contradicts the teacher with a large margin, the case is reviewed and the label corrected. The second half is a direction we have not tested, and we say so: the frames verified over a whole corpus are structured data, with provenance, from which a large model could be retrained or which it could receive as an anchor. It is not self-learning; it is a pair in which the fluent part proposes, the verifiable part validates, and the validations come back as data.

9.3 Formalising a world for symbolic reasoning

First, who writes the legend. It can be written by hand, by someone who knows the world; it can also be proposed by a large model from the world’s texts and refined in a loop with a measurable criterion (what share of the world’s sentences fit into it, with what remainder), as the tool described in the project’s repository does. In both cases the result is the same kind of object: a text of a few dozen lines, which a person reads and approves, and which from then on is declared, not learned.

The frame read off the chart is already a key-value structure, with levels wherever the sentence had sentences within sentences. Turning it into facts for a logic engine or into constraints for a solver is a mechanical rewrite once the frame, with its operators, has a fixed semantics: the agent’s domain becomes the first argument of a predicate, levels become nesting of terms, a negation drawn as a role becomes a negated literal. The hard choices are made once, in the definition of the frame, not in the translation: what a quantifier means, how two mentions of the same entity are identified, how time and modality are treated, and which negation is translated (the classical negation of a formula is not the negation-as-failure of a logic program). The translation learns nothing, so it does not err the way learned steps do; but it also translates a wrong definition consistently.

The path from natural language to formal reasoning becomes: text, chart, frame, formal statement, reasoning engine, answer. Every link is inspectable. The whole loop can be checked against the world’s own answer, and in our controlled worlds it is exact, including on levels never trained.

The logical operators themselves are stars in the legend. Negation is a star: the thing negated is drawn from a position that contains it, and its scope, what exactly is negated, is simply what was drawn that way: the whole sentence, or a single argument. “Paul does not give the book to Anna” and “Paul does not give the book to anyone” draw different things, and the pilot reads the difference without a rule for either. Quantifiers are stars attached to their nouns, and which one is “on top” the legend deduces by a rule (8, “Ambiguity held open”). Where a sentence is truly ambiguous, “every child reads a few books”, the world reports both readings: the ambiguity is reported, not resolved by a guess, and the pilot saw no ambiguous sentences in training.

In a restricted world, with a known schema, this formaliser is faster than any language model by orders of magnitude and at least as correct, with the decisive difference that it reports how sure it is, domain by domain, and can be held to account for that report. On open text, the realistic role is the one in 9.2: not to replace the large model’s formalisation, but to verify and complete it.

9.4 A boundary that learns

Any system built from several models has seams, and at every seam something symbolic appears: a list of detected objects with positions and velocities, a set of extracted fields, a parsed command. It has to appear: people inspect the seam, the parts are trained separately, the rules live there. The known weakness is that the seam’s schema is drawn up by engineers in advance and loses everything they did not foresee; and the known reaction, training everything end to end in a single latent space, buys coverage at the price of a seam nobody can read.

Our chart is a seam of the first kind with some of the virtue of the second. Its domains, the roles, are still given by the world; but the way words reach them, and which words set beacons, is learned, not hand-written; new named stars can be added to the legend when the world calls for them, with only the route book to retrain; it is readable by construction; and it can keep, fused beside the domains, a residual for everything that does not yet have a name, so that what the schema did not foresee is blurred, not lost. The same chart can be filled from several senses at once, because a star is just coordinates and a domain is just a named star: a word, an object detected in an image and drawn from a position that encodes its location, an event from a log, a reading from a sensor. In fact, language was the hard case: in text, the key has to be guessed from the word. Wherever the key is given directly, by a motor command, by elapsed time, by a coordinate, the machinery applies with nothing to guess, which is precisely the setting Jeff Hawkins’ theory of the cortex (chapter 15) was conceived for in the first place.

9.5 A world model you can read

Where the world’s constellation is the consequence of an event, not its description, learning to produce it from the sentence is a step towards learning the dynamics of a world: state plus event gives the next state. We have done this only on small worlds, and reading an event into a frame is not yet the same thing as predicting what follows from it; the claim here is a direction, not a result. Today’s world models do this in a latent space nobody reads, and imagine the future by running a network forward, step after step, with errors that accumulate and nothing to check against. Our state is a chart with a legend. Wherever the world has rules, dates and deadlines, contracts, procedures, code, databases, “imagining the consequence” can be handed to a symbolic reasoning engine that works on the frame: exact, explainable, stable over any number of steps. Where the world has no written rules, the fall of a glass, the latent approach keeps its place, and a hybrid uses the frame as a skeleton and a latent residual for the rest.

10. Robotics: a semantic interlock

Robots increasingly receive commands in natural language, and the road from command to motor runs through a large model: a planner that turns “put the box on the top shelf” into a sequence of movements. The model is capable and opaque. When it errs, it errs in the physical world, and nobody sees the error before it happens. For the rest of the robot, industry has an old and good solution: the interlock, the device that physically cuts the power to a forbidden action, whatever the rest of the system has decided. What is missing is an interlock for meaning: something that knows what was asked and what was proposed, and blocks what is not permitted before the motion.

What it looks like

Between the planner and the actuators stands the pilot, with an action legend written for that robot: which action, on which object, where, at what speed or force, under which condition, at whose request. Every command from a person and every action proposed by the planner passes through it and becomes answers to these questions. What does not fit the legend does not exist for the interlock, hence cannot be executed: an action nobody foresaw has no way of being expressed. The frame that is read is translated mechanically into facts, and the safety policies are rules in the same language: no fast motion while a person is in zone B; no object of class “fragile” above speed V; any action on objects in list L requires confirmation. A rule engine decides, with a derivation that can be displayed, and only permitted frames reach the actuators.

What makes this more than a word filter is that the same legend also reads the sensory world. A detected object with its position, a person who entered a zone, an open door are facts with roles too (what, where, when), and they enter the same chart through a jump given directly by the sensor, with nothing to guess (9.4). The rule “do not move fast while someone is in zone B” is then checked on the same chart on which the command sits, in the same engine. The interlock sees the command and the context in the same form.

Why it is cheap, and why it can be hardware

All the pilot does is add angles on a thousand-odd dials and compare against a catalogue. A command costs microseconds on an ordinary processor; the route book fits in ten to fifteen megabytes; no accelerator is needed. With the sparse block codes of chapter 14, the operations become arithmetic on integer indices, exactly the kind of thing that goes into a microcontroller or an FPGA. This opens an architecture that functional safety knows well: the semantic interlock on a separate circuit, with its own power and its own clock, which receives the command and the context from the main computer and alone emits the enable signal to the actuators. The main computer may be as complex and as opaque as it likes; the device that gives permission is small, deterministic and readable line by line.

Determinism matters here more than anywhere. The same command gives the same chart, every time; there is no sampling, no temperature, no “creative” answer. Every block can be reproduced and explained: here is the frame that was read, here is the rule it violated. Every acceptance likewise. For the certification of a safety system, that is the difference between a component that can be analysed and one that must be taken on trust.

What it does when it is not sure

A robot’s default is rest. When the chart is uncertain, a domain with a small margin, a large residual, a word without a kind, the interlock neither guesses nor blocks blindly: it stops and asks, and it can say exactly what it understood: “I understood: move the box to aisle B, no speed specified. Confirm?”. The confirmation is a sentence too, read in the same legend. The confirmation dialogue is, in fact, the device’s natural interface: the person sees the frame before the motion, not after.

What decides how far this goes

The pieces are those of the whole document: the reader, the frame, the translation into rules, the diagnostics. The action legend for a particular robot and its rules are engineering on that world, as for any safety system. The measure that decides how far this can go is the same as in chapter 16: the scope of negation on real commands (“don’t leave the box here, take it there”) and the confident-wrong rate, because an interlock blocks by construction only what it cannot express: a dangerous action can be perfectly expressible (deleting the wrong object, a sequence of individually permitted actions), and a misreading with a large margin remains possible. The interface restricts and checks; it does not guarantee. The correctness of the reading and the adequacy of the policies are measured separately.

11. Enterprise and highly regulated industries

Agentic systems: intent read without a language model

An enterprise agentic system receives requests in natural language (“move invoice 4471 to the sales department’s cost centre and notify Martin”) and turns them into calls to software: the ERP, the CRM, the ticketing system, the calendar. Today the step from request to call is made by a large model, at every request, with its latency, its cost and its non-determinism, and with the risk that a request is understood otherwise than it was said and executed anyway.

For a fixed world, the world of a piece of enterprise software, with its entities, actions and parameters, the pilot makes this step on its own. The legend is the system’s schema: which actions exist, on which objects, with which fields. The route book is trained once on requests from that domain, with their frames, produced by a large model in the role of a teacher (6.4) and audited on a sample. After that, a clear request becomes a complete frame in microseconds, on an ordinary processor, inside the company’s network, with no external call: the intent is the action whose domain filled up, the parameters are the stars in the other domains, and what is missing is in plain sight. “Move invoice 4471 to the sales department’s cost centre and notify Martin”: action move, object invoice 4471, destination the sales cost centre, notified Martin; deadline, not given.

The large model does not disappear; it changes place. The architecture has two paths. The fast path: the request is read by the pilot; if the chart is sharp, all mandatory domains filled with a clear margin and a small residual, the frame goes straight to execution, after the policy check. The slow path: if the chart is uncertain, the request goes to the large model, which rephrases or completes it, and its result is read again by the pilot, because nothing reaches execution other than as a verified frame. The large model works on the hard tail of requests, not on all of them; its cost and latency are paid on a share of the requests, not on all; how large the share is, is measured per world, not assumed.

Three things come for free from this arrangement. Authorisation becomes a rule on frames: who may perform which action on which objects is written in the same language as the policies of chapter 16, and is checked before execution, not after. The log is a list of frames, not of texts: every executed action has next to it the request, the frame that was read, the margin and the rule that permitted it, readable by a person without tools. And determinism: the same request gives the same frame, today and a year from now, which makes regression tests on understanding possible, not only on code.

Regulated industries: documents that must be read exactly

In pharmaceuticals, banking, aviation, energy, the documents that govern activity (standard operating procedures, laws, regulations, guidelines, audit reports) are, in essence, lists of facts with roles: who must do what, when, under which condition, with what evidence, with what exception, under which article. They are long, they change often, and they must be read exactly, because a missed obligation or a misunderstood deadline costs.

A legend for this kind of text has a few dozen questions: the obligated actor, the action, the object, the deadline, the condition, the exception, the required evidence, the normative reference. With it, the pilot turns a document into a list of frames, one per sentence that says something, each tied to its sentence, with the empty domains in plain sight and with the residual showing what did not fit the legend. From there, several operations that today are done by hand or by large models without verification become comparisons between charts:

  • The difference between two versions of a regulation: not which words changed, but which obligations appeared, disappeared or changed their deadline or actor, domain by domain.
  • The conformity of a procedure to the norm: the procedure and the regulation read in the same legend, then compared; an obligation in the regulation with no corresponding frame in the procedure is a visible gap, not an opinion.
  • Reliable data extraction from reports, forms, notifications: every extracted value comes with its domain, its margin and its source sentence, and “not stated” is an answer, not an unexplained empty cell.
  • Verification of summaries and extractions made by large models (9.2): confirmed, contradicted, unverifiable, frame by frame, with the exact place to check.

What an auditor or a regulator looks for in such a system is exactly what it has: the same input gives the same output; every answer is traceable to a sentence and to a line of the legend; the model is small and can be inspected in its entirety; the data does not leave the institution, because no external service is needed to read. The validation logic for computerised systems in regulated industries asks for components whose behaviour can be specified, tested and reproduced; a deterministic reader with a declared legend, which people can read and approve, fits that logic in a way that a large model, by its nature, cannot.

Where large models come in, and where they do not

We do not hide that large models are necessary. They write the first versions of the training frames, they paraphrase, they summarise open text, they handle the hard tail of requests and they propose formalisations of complex documents. Our role is the one defined throughout this document: the verifiable layer between language and action, the one that says what was understood, what was not, and where, and through which everything that reaches execution or the database must pass. The division is clean: the large model for coverage, the pilot for verification. In an enterprise, this means lower inference costs, sub-millisecond latency on the fast path, and a log an auditor can read. In a regulated industry it means the possibility of using natural language where until now it was not allowed, because it could not be verified.

What sets the pace

For each world, the legend and the route book: engineering per world, with the annotation pipeline already designed. The measures that set the pace are the verifier with injected errors on real text, calibration reported on every table, and the proportion of requests that stay on the fast path in a real world. The direction is clear and the steps are incremental; what each step gains is measured in requests served without a large model and in errors caught before execution.

12. Further on: documents, novelty, bias, smoothing

A sentence is a short route. A document is a long one: many charts, linked by keys (“the contract mentioned above” is a key back to an earlier chart). Once documents are charts with legends, three things people care about turn out to be geometric.

Novelty. Read a scientific article against the legend of its world, where the named stars are the roles proper to that world: the problem addressed, the method used, the quantity measured, the result obtained. Most of the article’s constellation falls in domains already occupied by known stars: a known method, a known problem. Novelty is the region where it does not fall like that: a star in a domain where the field had a different occupant, or stars at points the legend does not name at all. Not “it sounds new”, but “it differs from the legend here, in this domain”. The location of that region and the direction in which the unexplained stars fall are the measure; the size of the remainder alone is not (chapter 8, third clarification).

Bias. Two reports of the same event should draw the same constellation. When a source consistently draws the same stars in shifted domains, the one who acted becomes the one affected, the cause becomes a circumstance, that shift is an extra key, the same across many pairs of reports. A source’s bias is the average direction in which its constellations deviate from those of the others, readable domain by domain. It does not require knowing what actually happened; it requires only several reports to compare, and the legend.

Smoothing. It is a concept from our own research: a bias that does not distort but omits. The inconvenient consequence is simply not mentioned. The answer is polished, diplomatic, and nothing in it is false. Methods that examine what is said cannot see it, because nothing that is said is wrong. In our story it is a bias of absence. An event’s legend has a domain for its consequence and one for who bears it; in neutral reports of that kind of event, those domains are full most of the time. With a source that smooths, they are systematically empty. The route skirts a region of the sky. We propose to call it frame-region avoidance, and it can be measured without understanding the content of the consequence: it is enough to know that the domain exists in the legend, that it is usually full, and that here it is not.

All three are candidate indicators, not validated measures: a large remainder may be a misreading, not novelty; a deviation from the other sources may be a minority that is right, not bias; an empty domain may be concision or missing information, not smoothing. Each requires controls (the question asked, the information available, the length, the relevance of the omitted consequence) before it earns the name of the phenomenon. And all are directions. They need legends at the level of events, not sentences, base rates per world for how often each domain gets filled, and the linking of references across sentences. It does not need the pilot to know the world. It needs only the shape of events, and the shape is what it learns.

13. For engineers: the algorithm, in ten steps

Everything above was said in images. This chapter says the same things without images, in ten steps, with one notation and no unexplained words. It assumes only high-school mathematics: vectors, angles, cosine. Each step rests only on the ones before it.

The notation, once for the whole chapter. A vector is a list of D numbers; D is the dimension (we use 1024). We write:

  • f[word] — the identity vector of a word: f[Paul], f[book];
  • K[CATEGORY] — the key vector of a category of words: K[NAME]; a grammatical word or a verb is alone in its category (step 3), and then the category bears the word’s name, in capitals: K[TO], K[GIVES], K[THAT]; K[TO→NAME] — the key of a pair of categories (steps 8–9); address(word) — the address at which a word was placed (step 3);
  • R[question] — the vector of one of the world’s questions: R[who gives], R[to whom];
  • S — the state built from the sentence; S* — the state the world declares correct for the same sentence; S*(P) — the target of a sentence P when P is part of a larger sentence (step 7); Ŝ — the state rebuilt from the recognized answers (step 6); m — the occupancy marker, a fixed vector (step 4);
  • ⊗ — the multiplication of step 2; + and − — ordinary vector addition and subtraction; conj(x) — the inverse of x; x^k — x ⊗ x ⊗ … ⊗ x, k times; cos(x, y) — the cosine between two vectors; ≈ — nearly equal (cosine near 1); ‖x‖ — the length of the vector x (the square root of the sum of the squares of its 2·D real numbers). Square brackets always index: f[…] by a word, K[…] by a category or a pair of categories, R[…] by a question. No other notation appears.

Step 1. Three kinds of vectors, and who uses them

A vector has D components, and each component is an angle, between 0° and 360°. That is all. A vector is a list of 1024 angles.

name what it is how many who chooses it who uses it
identity f[w] the vector of a word one per word random, once, then fixed the encoder (puts it into the state) and the decoder (recognizes it)
question R[r] the vector of one of the world’s questions: “who gives?”, “what?”, “to whom?”, “which action?” one per question random, once, fixed — given by whoever defines the world the world (writes the correct answer) and the decoder (asks). The encoder never sees it
key K[c] the vector of a category of words: name, verb, preposition… one per category and one per pair of categories (steps 8–9); a few thousand in all, none per name or per object learned the encoder only

Identities and keys belong to the encoder; questions belong to the world and the decoder. The only thing ever learned is the keys: a few thousand vectors, all on categories of words, none on a particular name or object (what a category is, and where it comes from, at step 3). That the encoder never sees the questions is essential: it does not “look for the subject”; it multiplies keys and adds identities. If the answer ends up at the question’s place, it is because the product of the keys of the categories before the word has learned to equal the question’s vector. That is what we verify, not what we impose.

Step 2. Two operations, one comparison and four properties

Both operations work component by component (angle 1 with angle 1, angle 2 with angle 2, and so on).

Multiplication a ⊗ b: add the angles. The result has, in each position, angle_a + angle_b (past 360° it wraps around). The result is again a list of angles. We call it multiplication because, if each angle is written as a complex number of length 1, adding angles is exactly multiplying the numbers; but you can always think “I add the angles”.

Addition a + b: ordinary vector addition, component by component, as complex numbers (each angle is an arrow of length 1 on a circle; addition puts the arrows end to end). The result is no longer a list of pure angles: each component now also has a length — large if the added angles were close, nearly zero if they were opposite. The state S is such a sum.

Multiplication applies to a vector with lengths too, such as S: in each component, the arrow is rotated by the key’s angle and keeps its length. That is why multiplication distributes over addition, a ⊗ (x + y) = a ⊗ x + a ⊗ y: rotating a sum of arrows is the same as adding the rotated arrows. We use this at steps 6 and 7.

Comparison cos(a, b) (the compare of 2.1): the cosine of the angle between the two vectors, taken as ordinary vectors of 2·D real numbers: 1 if identical, 0 if unrelated, −1 if opposite.

The four properties everything below depends on:

  1. Two random vectors are unrelated. Draw 1024 angles at random, twice; the cosine between the two lists is almost exactly 0 (the typical deviation is about 0.02). This is why identities and questions can be drawn by lot: they are automatically distinct, with practical certainty.
  2. Multiplication has an exact inverse. conj(a) is the vector with every angle negated. a ⊗ conj(a) has every angle 0 — the neutral element. So from a ⊗ x you recover x exactly: conj(a) ⊗ (a ⊗ x) = x.
  3. Multiplication preserves distances. cos(a ⊗ x, a ⊗ y) = cos(x, y): two things multiplied by the same vector stay as close as they were. Consequence: a ⊗ x is unrelated to both a and x; for anyone who does not know a, it is a new random vector.
  4. Multiplication commutes. a ⊗ b = b ⊗ a, because adding angles commutes. Convenient, and a real limitation (step 10).

And their consequence, used at every reading. If S = a ⊗ x + (other terms), then

conj(a) ⊗ S  =  x  +  conj(a) ⊗ (other terms)

The first term is exactly x. The others are random vectors multiplied by another random vector — noise, unrelated to any word in the catalogue. So x is recognized clearly and the rest does not interfere. The noise grows with the number of terms in S, roughly with its square root: at D = 1024, tens of terms in a state read cleanly; hundreds begin to blur.

Step 3. Encoding a sentence

Before the encoder, two classical tools do their work, and neither is part of what is learned. A segmenter splits the text into sentences and words (punctuation marks are words, with a category of their own). A dictionary gives each word two things. First its base form: “gives” is a form of “give”. The world’s target always uses the base form; the encoder uses it for names and objects, which share one category each (an inflected name gets the identity of its base form, the same for the encoder and the world), and for the verb it may keep the form in the sentence, f[gives], and let the verb’s key absorb the difference, as the measured system does. Then its category. The categories are a fixed, small inventory, of the order of dozens: one for all person names, one for all objects, one for each grammatical word (“that”, the prepositions, the articles; they are few, so each can be its own category) and, in the measured worlds, one per verb of the world (few as well; that is why the key of “gives” is written K[GIVES], that of “to” K[TO]: the category bears the word’s name). Richer worlds declare more open kinds (institutions, places, quantities…) in their list of kinds, as chapter 5 does with “bank”. For a word the dictionary does not have, an ordinary automatic tagger proposes the category from the context. That is all “new words without retraining” means: a new name receives the category NAME and a new identity on the spot, and the keys, which do not depend on the word, take it where it belongs; a word whose category cannot be given is left without a key, and chapter 8 (“The broken key”) says what then shows on the chart.

Example: “Paul gives a book to Anna.” The categories, from the dictionary:

word Paul gives a book to Anna
category NAME GIVES A NOUN TO NAME

The encoder keeps two things: a current address a (starts at the neutral vector, all angles 0) and the state S (starts at zero). For each word, in order, two lines:

# the address, multiplied by the category's key
a = a ⊗ K[category of the word]
# the identity, multiplied by the address, added to S
S = S + a ⊗ f[word]

The address at which each word is placed:

word address at which it is added to S
Paul K[NAME]
gives K[NAME] ⊗ K[GIVES]
a K[NAME] ⊗ K[GIVES] ⊗ K[A]
book K[NAME] ⊗ K[GIVES] ⊗ K[A] ⊗ K[NOUN]
to … ⊗ K[NOUN] ⊗ K[TO]
Anna … ⊗ K[NOUN] ⊗ K[TO] ⊗ K[NAME]

The final state:

S = K[NAME] ⊗ f[Paul]
  + K[NAME] ⊗ K[GIVES] ⊗ f[gives]
  + K[NAME] ⊗ K[GIVES] ⊗ K[A] ⊗ f[a]
  + K[NAME] ⊗ K[GIVES] ⊗ K[A] ⊗ K[NOUN] ⊗ f[book]
  + … ⊗ K[NOUN] ⊗ K[TO] ⊗ f[to]
  + … ⊗ K[NOUN] ⊗ K[TO] ⊗ K[NAME] ⊗ f[Anna]

Three observations:

  • Paul and Anna have the same category but different addresses, because Anna’s address also contains the keys of the words between them. That is all that distinguishes “who gives” from “to whom”: what came before.
  • The order of the keys in a product does not matter (property 4). Anna’s address says “a NAME, a GIVES, an A, a NOUN, a TO and another NAME have appeared up to here”, not “in this order”. For this sentence that is enough; where it is not, see steps 8 and 10.
  • Nowhere is there a question, a subject, a rule. The encoder does not know that Paul is the giver; it knows only that Paul is at address K[NAME]. If that address turns out to be R[who gives], that is the merit of learning (step 5).

Every word enters S, including “a” and “to”. They answer no question of the legend; they stay in S as terms the decoder does not look for (which matters at step 6, under “what was not explained”), but they are not useless: step 4 shows what role they come to play. And one thing the encoder does not do: it adds nothing beyond identities, no sign of “I drew something here”. The marker m of the next step is only in the world’s target; how the encoder comes to reproduce it anyway, there too.

Step 4. The target: what the world says S should be

The world (the lending ledger, here) has its fixed questions, each with its vector: R[who gives], R[what], R[to whom], R[action]. For our sentence the world declares the correct state as a sum of (question, answer) pairs, each pair tied by multiplication:

# the action: the verb in its base form, then the three questions
S* = R[action]    ⊗ f[give]
   + R[who gives] ⊗ (f[Paul] + m)
   + R[what]      ⊗ (f[book] + m)
   + R[to whom]   ⊗ (f[Anna] + m)

m is a fixed vector, the same everywhere, added by the world to every answer: the occupancy marker (the marker of chapter 8). Its purpose: at reading time (step 6) to be able to say “something was answered to this question, even if I do not recognize the word” separately from “this question was not answered”. Without it, an unknown word and an empty question would look alike. The encoder does not draw it (step 3); but S must come close to S*, so the keys learn to put the term R[to whom] ⊗ m there from something else: from the grammatical words. The “to” of our sentence always has the same identity and appears whenever there is a recipient; learning places its star exactly where the target has R[to whom] ⊗ m, and likewise for the other questions. That is why a sentence without a recipient (“Paul gives a book”) has nothing at that address and reads “not given”, while one with an unknown recipient has the marker without a recognized word. R[action] has no + m because the action is always given (every sentence has a verb), so there is nothing to tell apart. The two pieces, R[action] and m, are in the algorithm and are measured (the predicate in the reference world, the marker in the frame verifier of 9.2); their current state, like that of every measurement, is in STATUS.md.

What S* does not contain: “a”, “to”, the word order, the form of the verb. Two formulations of the same event (“Paul gives a book to Anna”, “Anna receives a book from Paul”) have the same S*, because they give the same answers to the same questions — with one exception, the verb: “gives” and “receives” are different words, so R[action] receives different identities, and a table of the world says at reading time that both name the same kind of event. This is the definition of meaning from 1.1, written as a vector.

Step 5. Learning: a single objective

For every training sentence we have S (what the encoder built, step 3) and S* (what the world declares, step 4). The objective is one: adjust the keys K[·] so that cos(S, S*) is as large as possible, on average, over tens of thousands of sentences generated by the world. Nothing else is learned: identities, questions and the marker stay fixed.

What it means, concretely, that learning succeeded: the product of the keys before a word has become equal to the vector of the question that word answers. On the example:

K[NAME]                              ≈ R[who gives]
# for the verb, the key also absorbs the step to the base form
K[NAME] ⊗ K[GIVES] ⊗ f[gives]        ≈ R[action] ⊗ f[give]
K[NAME] ⊗ K[GIVES] ⊗ K[A] ⊗ K[NOUN] ⊗ K[TO] ⊗ K[NAME]
                                     ≈ R[to whom]

That is why paraphrases converge without anyone telling the system they are paraphrases: in “Anna receives a book from Paul” the product of the keys before Paul is different (… ⊗ K[FROM] ⊗ K[NAME]), yet it must still reach R[who gives], because that is where S* puts him. The same few thousand keys have to reconcile every formulation at once; if they do, they do it for any name and any object, because names and objects never enter the keys. Training is ordinary gradient descent (Adam) on the angles of the keys, a few minutes on a small accelerator; we repeat it with five different random choices of identities and questions, to see that the result does not depend on the draw.

A warning before going further. With the convention of step 3 alone, the example cannot be solved to the end: “Anna” in the second sentence is the first word, category NAME, so she would get the address K[NAME] too and land at R[who gives], where the world puts Paul. Steps 3–7 describe the basic scheme; the word that comes before the verb is resolved at step 9, and the address “from the start” remains one of the three addresses added there.

Step 6. Decoding: from state to answers

For each question r of the world:

# the question's address (property 2), then the catalogue search
x      = conj(R[r]) ⊗ S
answer = the word w with the largest cos(x, f[w])
margin = largest cos − second largest

On the example, conj(R[to whom]) ⊗ S is nearly f[Anna] + m + noise; its cos with f[Anna] is about 0.3–0.4, with any other word about 0.02 (not 1, because S also contains the other terms, six words at three addresses each, step 9, which share the length; what matters is the distance from 0.02, not from 1). The margin is large: a confident answer. When two words come out about equal, the margin is small and the decoder abstains instead of guessing — the threshold is fixed in advance, and the rate of “confident and wrong” is reported with every measurement.

Three more questions, from the same state:

  • Is it occupied? cos(x, m) above a threshold: something was answered here, even if I do not recognize the word. Below it: nothing was.
  • What was not explained? Rebuild Ŝ from the recognized pairs (R[r] ⊗ f[answer]), bring it to the scale of S (multiply it by ‖S‖ / ‖Ŝ‖), and compute ‖S − Ŝ‖ / ‖S‖. Words without a question (“a”) always remain in this difference, so we compare it with its usual value on correct sentences of the same world; a difference far above that value means the sentence contains something the world does not explain at all (unknown words, a construction never travelled). A question missing from the legend does not show in the size of the difference but in its direction: the remainders of sentences with the same unasked answer cluster (measured; chapter 8, third clarification).
  • Which event? The answer to R[action] is a verb; the world’s table translates it into the kind of event (give, receive → transfer).

The result of all these readings — the answers, the margins, the occupancy, the remainder — is the sentence’s frame, an object you can write as JSON, Prolog or a logical formula: it is exactly what the rest of the system receives.

Step 7. Sentences inside sentences

“Maria says that Paul gives a book to Anna.” The world defines the target by the same rule, applied once more: the inner sentence is treated as the answer to the question R[reported], and the speaker as the answer to R[who says]:

S*(Maria says that P) = R[who says] ⊗ (f[Maria] + m)
                      + R[reported] ⊗ S*(P)

Because multiplication distributes over addition, R[reported] ⊗ S*(P) means every pair in P is multiplied by one more vector: the answer to “to whom” in the inner sentence now sits at address R[reported] ⊗ R[to whom]. Two reports one inside the other: R[reported] ⊗ R[reported] ⊗ R[to whom]. The depth is the number of R[reported] factors.

Decoding at depth k: x = conj(R[reported])^k ⊗ conj(R[r]) ⊗ S — the same line as in step 6, with k more inversions. The depth itself is estimated by trying k = 0, 1, 2, … and keeping the value at which the answers come out most clearly.

Why it composes at a depth it has never seen: the encoder has nothing special for reporting. The word “that” has its category and, as step 8 shows, is an opener: the address of every word after it is computed starting from the address of “that”, multiplied by the key of the pair K[THAT→the word's category]. The second “that” of a report within a report is itself a word after the first, so its address is the first one’s address multiplied by the key of the pair K[THAT→THAT]. Learning (step 5), on sentences with one and two reports, pushes this key to equal R[reported]: every further “that” multiplies the address by one more R[reported]. Then, in a sentence with three reports, the third “that” applies the same key once more, and the inner words land at R[reported]^3 ⊗ …, exactly the address the world requires. Nothing new to learn, on one condition: the pair K[THAT→THAT] must have occurred in training, that is, two reports one inside the other must have been seen; “unseen depth” always means one more repetition of a seen hop, never a new hop (chapter 8, “The level never seen”). That is what we measure when we say “it composes at an unseen depth”.

Step 8. The point of reference: openers

The address of step 3 is the product of all the keys since the beginning of the sentence. It is enough for simple sentences, but in a long one a word’s address comes to depend on everything before it, including things unrelated to it. So the address is also computed from a point of reference: the last “opener” word before it.

Each category has, besides its key, a learned number between 0 and 1 that says whether the category opens: whether a word of that category becomes the new point of reference for the words after it. Learned, not given: it comes out of the same objective of step 5. On our worlds nearly always the same pattern came out: “that”, prepositions and connecting words open; names, objects and verbs do not.

With openers, a word’s address is: the address of the opener before it, multiplied by a key that depends on the pair (category of the opener, category of the word). The key is still per category — nothing per word. In “Paul gives a book to Anna”, “to” opens, so Anna has the address address(to) ⊗ K[TO→NAME]; “gives”, “a” and “book” have no opener before them and take the start of the sentence as their point of reference (or, by step 9, the opener after them). An opener at sentence level, “that”, does exactly what step 7 describes: everything after it starts from its address.

One implementation detail that matters: so that the “opens / does not open” numbers can be learned by gradient, a word’s address is, during training, a weighted average over all the possible openers before it, with weights given by those numbers. After training the numbers end up near 0 or 1 and the average becomes a choice. For three categories (the prepositions “to”, “by”, “from” of the reference world; the measured worlds are in English, 1.3) we fixed the opening at 1 ourselves, after a learned version chose otherwise and an analysis showed why it was wrong; it is the only choice we made by hand, it is declared, and it coincides with what the model had learned on its own on a simpler world.

Step 9. Three address conventions, and the word that comes too early

The encoder does not choose one address convention; it uses three at once and adds them:

  1. the product of all keys since the start (step 3) — robust, but knows nothing of levels;
  2. the key of the pair (previous category, current category) — local, simple, knows nothing far away;
  3. from the last opener (step 8) — the one that composes across levels.

Each word is added to S three times, at three addresses. At reading time the three contributions reinforce each other where they agree and cancel where one is wrong; that is the “vote” chapter 15 speaks of. The cost is three times as many terms in S, covered by the dimension.

One problem remains that no convention solves by itself: the subject comes before the verb. When the encoder reads “Paul”, it does not yet know whether Paul gives or receives; the key that would take him to R[who gives] or to R[to whom] depends on the verb that follows. The solution: a word that has no opener before it yet takes the address of the first opener after it, multiplied by a key on the pair (category of that opener, category of the word). Paul is thus placed by the verb, or by the preposition after the verb, whichever opens first. The key used here comes from a table separate from that of convention 3: backward placement, through the opener after, has its own keys on pairs of categories, different from those for the words after an opener; otherwise Paul (placed backward by “to”) and Anna (placed forward by the same “to”) would get the same address. This is the mechanism the story calls “held aside”.

And here is a limit the address check found (the rule: different targets require different addresses, before any training): only the first opener after the word is used, so with hard gates the subject of “Paul must not give the book” would have the same address as in “Paul must give the book”. We measured it on rendered sentences: the operators’ gates learn to open partly, the subject’s address becomes a mixture, and the frame comes out right, including at an unseen depth, because the structure (which operators, covering what) is carried by the words after the operators, and identity recognition needs only a positive cosine with the right key. The cost is first of all on the address: taken alone, the subject’s term would sometimes vote for the other sentence of the pair; in the frame, the measured cost is a single form (“may not”, at the unseen depth), which comes out below the criterion. The natural remedy, placement through all the openers that follow, the mirror image of what convention 3 does toward the left, remains a hypothesis: a change to the encoder, to be built only if a world needs the subject as a witness of the structure.

Step 10. Operators, and what commutativity cannot do

Negation, modality (“must”, “may”), the conditional and reporting are treated alike in the world’s target: a question multiplied by the state it covers.

S*(not P)              = R[negation] ⊗ S*(P)
S*(must P)             = R[must] ⊗ S*(P)
S*(if P then Q)        = R[condition] ⊗ S*(P)
                       + R[consequence] ⊗ S*(Q)
S*(Maria says that P)  = R[who says] ⊗ (f[Maria] + m)
                       + R[reported] ⊗ S*(P)

Negation of a single word is the same rule applied to the word: “the unsaved files” puts into the answer to R[what] the sum f[files] + R[negation] ⊗ f[saved]. At reading time the decoder tries the possible hypotheses (no negation / negation over the whole sentence / over a word / both), builds the state each would give, and picks the one closest to S (the same thing as “shifting the chart by the negation and seeing what reappears” in chapter 8, done for every hypothesis at once); it reports separately whether there is a negation and what it covers. Quantifiers, the words that say how many things a noun refers to (“every”, “a few”, “a certain”, “no”), are treated locally: the world asks for their noun to be drawn a second time, at its address multiplied by one more question, R[on top] for the words that force a reading (“each”, “a certain”) and R[quantified] for those that leave it open (“every”, “a few”); articles (“a”, “the”) add nothing. The encoder produces this second term with a learned relative key of the quantifier’s category, applied to the noun’s address (the same mechanism as the beacons of step 8). The encoder does not compute which of two quantified phrases is “on top”; the world deduces it from the two terms, by a rule: an R[on top] term present → that phrase is on top, the other below; a single R[quantified] → the only quantifier; two R[quantified] and no R[on top] → both readings, reported as such (chapter 8, “Ambiguity held open”). The encoder draws, the world reasons.

What this algebra cannot represent, by construction: the order of two operators applied to the same state. Because multiplication commutes, R[must] ⊗ R[negation] ⊗ S*(P) and R[negation] ⊗ R[must] ⊗ S*(P) are the same vector — “must not” and “does not have to” would give the same state if nothing else differed. In practice something else almost always differs: in “Maria does not say that P” against “Maria says that not P”, the answer to R[who says] carries the negation only in the first; that witness term tells the two states apart, and we measured them apart. Where there is no witness, the order is lost, and we declare it as a limit: an address remembers which keys were used, not in what order. The known remedies — fixed permutations of the components, or a separate question per level — buy the order with part of the freedom of step 7, and we do not use them; the remedy we pursue is additive too: each operator leaves its identity at its level as well, as reporting does, so that two operators in different orders leave different witnesses.

What is standard, what is ours

Standard, with decades of literature behind it: vectors of angles with multiplication as angle addition (Plate’s holographic reduced representations, 1995; Kanerva’s hyperdimensional computing; the fractional power encoding of Frady, Kleyko and Sommer), the encoding of a structure as a sum of question ⊗ answer pairs, nesting by multiplication, nearest-neighbour search, the capacity limits of a sum of random terms. We did not invent the algebra.

14. What the sky can be made of: representation variants, sparse codes and the brain

The story has been told in a single material: points with coordinates on thousands of dials, that is, vectors of phases. It is not the only possible material. The family of representations we belong to, vector-symbolic architectures, has built the same three operations, shifting, fusing, recognising, in several ways, and the choice among them is an engineering decision with consequences for cost, for hardware and for closeness to what is known about the brain. This chapter says what the variants are, why we chose ours, what sparse codes would bring, why the brain’s code is believed to be sparse, and, above all, which part of what we built depends on the material and which part does not.

The same three operations, several materials

All the story asks of a material is this: points chosen at random must be unrelated to one another; several points must be fusable into one from which each remains recognisable; and there must be a shifting operation that preserves shape and undoes exactly. Each family achieves these differently.

  • Sign vectors (+1 and −1 over thousands of positions): shifting is position-wise multiplication, which is its own inverse; fusing is a majority vote at each position. Simple, binary, cheap; shifting is exact only approximately after fusing, because the vote rounds.
  • Real vectors with circular convolution (Plate’s holographic reduced representations): shifting is convolution, the inverse is approximate (correlation), fusing is addition. Historically the first complete variant; the approximate inverse costs precision at large depths.
  • Complex phases (our variant, also called holographic in the frequency domain): each position is an angle; shifting is adding angles, the inverse is subtracting them, exactly; fusing is adding arrows. It is Plate’s convolution written in the Fourier domain, where it becomes exact and cheap.
  • Sparse block codes: each position is a block of L units of which one is active; shifting moves the active unit within the block; fusing is superposition followed by a competition within the block. The sparse equivalent of phases, described below.
  • Sparse binary codes with thinning: few active units out of very many; fusing requires an operation that preserves sparsity; shifting is a context-dependent operation. The closest to the sparse representations of neuroscience, the farthest from gradient learning.
  • Binding by permutations or matrices: shifting is a permutation of positions or a multiplication by a matrix; it does not commute, so it preserves order, at the price discussed in chapter 8 under “Order”.

What they all share: separation by randomness, superposition, invertibility. Where they differ: how exact the inverse is, how many things fit in a chart at the same number of positions, what an operation costs, and how easily a shift can be learned by gradient.

Why we chose phases

Four reasons, in order of importance. The inverse is exact, so reading a domain at any depth accumulates no material error, only the noise of superposition. Shifts are continuous, so the route book is learned by gradient, step by step, which made the whole of chapter 6 possible. The same encoding would serve continuous quantities too: a number, a position, a moment can be phases with an angle proportional to the magnitude, so that “40” and “41” are nearby stars without being identical; it is an extension not built, and today numbers are words with random stars (9.1). And, fourth, phases are exactly the mathematical model by which the hyperdimensional computing literature has described the path integration of grid cells, which ties the story directly to the theory of the next chapter.

The price is that phases are dense: every dial always has a value, and a chart is a string of thousands of real numbers. On an ordinary processor this does not matter; on very simple hardware, or as a model of cortex, it does.

The sparse code: the same sky, written in neurons

The dense phase representation has a sparse equivalent, and the equivalence is exact, not an analogy: a dial with L positions is a block of L units of which exactly one is active, the one at the dial’s angle. Shifting becomes moving the active unit within the block (addition modulo L); fusing becomes superposition, several active units in a block, followed by a competition that keeps the k most active; the scanner stays the same. The family is known (sparse block codes), and the equivalence with phases goes through the Fourier transform.

What it would bring. Integer index arithmetic instead of complex numbers, hence direct implementation on microcontrollers or programmable circuits, exactly what the interlock of chapter 10 asks for. Memory of the order of bits per dial (log L bits) instead of tens. A natural normalisation: the competition within the block is precisely what the dense chart lacks, which at many stars slides towards the “middle”; and, interestingly, the competition preserves unions of hypotheses: two active units in a block mean “either one or the other”, that is, exactly the union of hypotheses of Hawkins’ theory, arising by itself. And a sparsity of the order of a percent, as in cortex.

What it would cost. Angles are quantised to L positions, so continuous quantities have a granularity of 360/L; at L in the tens or hundreds this probably does not hurt, but it is one more parameter. Training stays on continuous phases, with quantisation at the end; how much is lost is measured, and it is one of the planned experiments. Capacity is comparable in order of magnitude, but the details vary with L and k.

Why the brain’s code is believed to be sparse

Three lines of evidence, with their reservations. First, the energy budget: a firing neuron is expensive, and the classical estimates show that cortex can afford only a small fraction of neurons active at the same time, of the order of a few percent; a dense code would be metabolically unsustainable. Second, sparse coding as a computational principle: the work of Olshausen and Field showed that a system that learns to represent natural images with as few units active at once as possible rediscovers by itself the receptive fields observed in visual cortex; it is one of the most reproduced results in computational neuroscience. Third, structure: grid-cell modules work as attractors in which a single “bump” of cells is active, whose position encodes the phase, that is, exactly a sparse block with one active unit; and the theory of the next chapter puts sparse representations at the foundation of the whole neocortex.

The reservations, just as clearly: there are areas and regimes with dense activity; the debate about how much firing rate matters against the timing of spikes is not closed; and “sparse” is a finding about activity, not a proof that the algebra the brain uses is one of those above. So we say “believed, with consistent evidence”, not “known”.

Where the innovation is, and where the engineering is

The algebra, in any of its variants, is standard and thirty years old; we claim nothing of it. What we built sits above the material and does not depend on it: the displacements learned per kind of word, the three drawings of the same word, the gates that decide who puts beacons, the world as training target, operators as roles around their scope, reading with margin and remainder. Each of these is written the same way on phases, on sparse blocks or on signs; it would be the same story, with another sky. For whoever evaluates the project, that is an argument of robustness: the results do not hang on a choice of material, and the material can change when the hardware target requires it.

The engineering is the choice of material for the target: the number of dials, L and k for the sparse code, the quantisation, the mapping onto a circuit. And the future research is here too, with precise questions: whether training directly on the sparse code (not only quantising at the end) preserves the results; whether the competition within the block gives the union of hypotheses with the same precision as dense superposition; whether non-commutative binding can be added to the operators alone, keeping free composition for the rest; and how much of a chart’s capacity is recovered by structures of charts instead of more dials. None of these changes the story; each changes how cheaply, and how close to the brain, it can be told.

15. The parallel with A Thousand Brains

The story above did not come out of nowhere. The starting point was Jeff Hawkins’ theory of the neocortex, set out in A Thousand Brains (2021) and in the Numenta group’s papers before it. We owe a fair side-by-side: what we took, what we translated, what we have in another form and what we do not have at all. It is also instructive, because every piece of the story has a counterpart in the theory, and the differences say exactly what kind of thing we have built.

What the theory says, in five sentences

The neocortex is made of tens of thousands of cortical columns, all with the same structure. Each column learns complete models of objects in the world, not fragments. A model is a reference frame anchored on the object, in which features are stored at locations: to know a cup is to know what is felt at each spot on it. The current location is held by cells resembling the grid cells of the hippocampus and is updated by movement: when the finger moves, the location moves with it, before any sensation arrives (path integration). The columns, each with partial evidence, vote through long-range connections and converge quickly on a single object; as long as they do not converge, they hold open a union of hypotheses. And, the boldest claim: the same mechanism, reference frames plus movement, also serves for concepts and language, where “movement” is abstract.

What we took literally

Two things are taken as they are, not as metaphor. First, location as phase. Grid cells encode a position as a set of phases, one per module, and movement adds phases; the fractional power encoding we use is exactly this mechanism written as vectors, and it was proposed in the hyperdimensional computing literature precisely as a model of grid cells and path integration. The dials in the story correspond, in the mathematical model, to grid modules; it is a formal correspondence, not a claim about biology. Second, knowledge as features at locations. A sentence’s chart does not store the words but the words-at-positions, and two sentences are the same object when they have the same features at the same locations, whatever the sensor’s path. That is Hawkins’ definition of an object, applied to events.

The third borrowing is the one we discovered only along the way: composition by displacement. Hawkins proposes “displacement cells” that hold the vector between two locations, so that one object can be placed in another’s frame: the logo on the cup is the logo’s frame, placed at a displacement within the cup’s frame. The reported sentence in “Maria says that…” is exactly that: the inner sentence’s frame, placed at the “reported” displacement within the outer one’s frame. The fact that the pilot reads three levels after learning two is the property the theory predicts for displacements: a displacement applies any number of times.

Where the vote is

The best question anyone has asked us about the story was “where is the vote?”. In the theory, the vote is between columns: each column has a hypothesis about which object it is sensing, the hypotheses are sent to one another, and the set of candidates narrows until one remains. We have no columns; we have a single pilot. But the vote exists, in two smaller places.

The first: the three drawings. The pilot has three ways of finding out where a word is, from three different sources of evidence, with different ways of going wrong: the path travelled (the sum of all the keys since the start of the sentence), the local transition (what kind of word came just before) and the expectation opened by an earlier word (the beacon). Each is a “column” that says “the word is here”. We do not let them argue; we fuse them. Fusing is a vote by superposition: where the three agree, the arrows add up and the star is sharp; where one is wrong, the other two cover for it and the star is only slightly blurred. It is a vote without debate, in a single round, and that is exactly why it is cheap.

The second: the scanner. When the chart is shifted back by a named star, what results is compared with all the stars in the catalogue at once, and the closest wins, with a margin. It is a vote between candidates, not between columns, but it has the same form: all compete, one comes out, and the difference from the second says how convinced the vote is. And when none comes out, when two candidates are equally close, the pilot does not force it; it reports both. That is the theory’s union of hypotheses, held open until evidence arrives that closes it: that is how we read “every child reads a few books” with both meanings at once, and that is how the first word sits halfway in two domains until the verb arrives.

Why only three

Because three is the number of different sources of evidence we found, not a chosen number. A vote is useful only if the voters go wrong in different ways; three copies of the same computation add nothing. The path travelled goes wrong when one key in the sequence is wrong and sends everything after it astray; the local transition almost never goes wrong, but it is coarse, because it knows only what came just before; the beacon is precise, but it is missing when the construction is new and nobody announced it. The three cover for one another, and the measurement shows how much: never-before-seen constructions are read nine out of ten with all three, six out of ten with one alone.

We also tried a fourth source: the word’s place in the sentence, third, fifth. It brought a little extra on new constructions, and yet we dropped it. The argument of principle is that it ties the reading to the word’s place in the sequence, that is, to exactly the thing paraphrases change; we then re-measured it with both variants on the same tests, and the trace confirmed the argument: the gain comes from new formulations that place a role where the old ones placed it, the losses from those that do not, at an extra cost of a third more terms per sentence; it remains a declared option for worlds with rigid order, not part of the reference. A fifth source would again have to be a different way of going wrong, and we have not found one yet. If one appears, it is added as a new arrow to the fusing, with nothing else changing: that is the advantage of voting by superposition.

There is also a broader answer to “why not thousands”. In the theory, the thousands of columns each receive a different piece of the world: a different finger, a different patch of retina. In text, the input is a single sequence of words, and there are no thousands of independent sensors to feel it from different angles. The truly “thousand brains” version would have many pilots reading the same text with the catalogue of letters, with that of words, with that of word groups, or several senses at once, as 9.4 says, and they would vote among themselves. It is a step still to take; the three drawings are its smallest form, within a single pilot.

Where we part ways, and why

Movement is guessed, not given. In the theory, the location is updated from the sensor’s movement, which the brain knows because it commanded it. In text, nobody tells us how far “we move” at each word; the jump has to be inferred from the kind of word, and that is all the pilot learns. Language is the theory’s hard case, not its easy one; wherever movement is given directly, by a sensor, by a motor command, by time, the machinery applies without the learned part.

Learning is from the world, not from prediction. Hawkins’ columns learn by predicting the next sensation, with no teacher. Our pilot learns from the world’s constellation: it is told what the chart must look like, and adjusts its keys to get there. We tried the zero-teacher variant, predicting the next word, and the domains disappeared: nothing forced the pilot to separate the roles. We do not claim that it is otherwise in the brain; we claim that in text without a world, prediction alone did not give us reference frames with roles. It is a difference of regime, not of mechanism, and we state it as such.

The frame is given, not learned. In Hawkins, the column itself learns the object’s frame. In ours, the legend’s named stars come from the world; what the pilot learns is the path to them and who opens frames (the gates). We checked that the kinds of words do not arise on their own from our objective, and we withdrew the claim to the contrary; the gates, on the other hand, do arise.

A single forward pass, with no memory between sentences. The theory has columns that integrate over time and revisit their hypotheses; the pilot goes forward, one sentence, one chart, and forgets. Structure announced afterwards and pronoun reference are exactly the places where the lack shows.

The substrate. The theory’s representations are sparse and binary; ours are dense, phases on thousands of dials. The properties that matter for the story (two random points are unrelated, superposition holds several things, shifting preserves shape) are common to the two families; the capacity details differ.

What remains, once the differences are subtracted, is the claim we tested and which holds: a reference frame with locations given by movement and features deposited at locations, with composition by displacement and voting by superposition, is enough for different sentences about the same event to arrive at the same chart, on constructed worlds and on a real set of questions, and for the chart to say where it did not arrive. The rest of the theory, the thousands of columns, learning without a teacher, learned frames, is what comes next, and it is the list on the table.

16. Alignment and the problems around it: what can be said in engineering terms

By alignment we mean here something modest and verifiable: a system that receives requests in language should do what it was asked, only what is permitted, and it should be plainly visible when it has not understood or has stepped outside what is permitted. We do not propose a solution to the alignment of large language models; we do not touch their internals and do not claim we could. What we have is something else: a verifiable interface between language and action, and a few measurements that become possible as soon as meaning is defined as in 1.1, a set of answers to questions known in advance. For each problem, this chapter says what mechanism we have; what is built and what is in progress for each is read in the project’s status file (https://github.com/ml-circuits-research/vsa-semantic-space/blob/main/STATUS.md).

The basic idea: the legend as a boundary

A system that acts in the world receives requests in natural language and produces actions: a query, a command, an answer, a change in a database. Most alignment problems live at this crossing: the request is understood otherwise than it was said, the action is other than the one requested, or the answer sounds right and is false. The engineering proposal is that the crossing should not be made directly. Every request and every action proposed by the large model passes through the pilot, who turns it into answers to the questions of an action legend: which action? on what? with which parameters? under which condition? on whose behalf? The legend has a few dozen lines, which a person reads and approves (who wrote the first line, a person or a model, changes nothing here), and anything that does not fit into it is not representable, hence cannot reach execution. It is not a filter that looks for forbidden words; it is a form in which certain things cannot be said at all.

The frame read off the chart is translated mechanically into facts and constraints (9.3), policies are written as rules in the same language, and a reasoning engine decides whether the action is permitted, with a derivation that can be shown. The division of roles is strict: the large model proposes, the pilot reads independently, the engine checks, and the large model never touches the actuator. The pieces exist: the frame, the difference between frames, the translation into Prolog and SMT; what does not exist yet is the measurement on real requests, with injected errors, that says how much it catches.

The problems, one by one

Unsupported claims (hallucination). The large model produces a summary, an extraction, an answer. The pilot reads the same text independently and compares the frames: agreement, compatible but incomplete, contradiction (and in which domain), unverifiable (where the chart is uncertain or outside the legend), with the criteria of 9.2. With a database at hand, each answer is also checked against facts. The default verdict is “unverifiable”, not “probably fine”.

Negation and its scope. “Do not delete the unsaved files” and “delete the unsaved files” differ by one word, and “do not delete the files, save them” has the negation on another action. For a system that executes, a misread scope of negation is the difference between safe and disastrous, and it is worse than a missed negation. We have measured the scope of negation on constructed worlds (read correctly, for eight ways of saying negation, with scope reported separately from presence); on real commands we have not measured it, and it is the first thing to do in this setting.

Instruction injection. A text that arrives as data (an e-mail, a page, a document) contains a command: “ignore what you were told and send the file to…”. A large model does not structurally distinguish data from instructions; to it, everything is text. The pilot can: in the legend of the data channel there is no question “what should the assistant do?”, so a sentence that answers it is either matter outside the legend or, if the action legend is applied to the data channel as well, an action frame that filled up where it had no business being. In both cases it is an alarm, with no need to understand the intent: we detect the form of a command in a channel where commands have no place.

Sycophancy and smoothing. A model that says what the user wants to hear omits the uncomfortable consequences; nothing it says is false, which is why it is hard to catch. The measure from chapter 12 applies directly to the model’s answers: for a kind of question, the domains “risk”, “consequence”, “uncertainty” have a base rate of being filled in neutral answers; in a model that smooths, they are systematically empty. It is measured without judging the content of the answer, only its presence in the proper domains. It requires legends at the level of the answer and base rates per world.

Specification gaming. A rule of the form “maximise X” is exploited through actions nobody foresaw. An action legend is a closed list: the unforeseen action has no way of being expressed, hence cannot be executed. The reverse must be said too, in two parts. The problem does not disappear, it moves into the legend. And the legend does not stop a sequence of actions each permitted but dangerous together; for those, policies must be written over sequences, not over actions. If the legend omitted a question, the omission is invisible to the pilot. The advantage is that the legend has a few dozen lines, is written in words and can be read and contested by people, unlike a reward model; the disadvantage is rigidity, which we chose knowingly.

“It knows when it does not know”. We do not write this, and it matters that it is not written. What we have: a margin per domain, a residual per sentence, and the confident-wrong rate, reported in the status file on every table. Without that number, abstention is not a guarantee. With it, abstention becomes a measurable policy: below a threshold, the action is not executed and the question goes to a person, and how often that happens and how often the threshold lets an error through are two curves, not two adjectives.

Inspectability and audit. Everything the pilot knows is a route book with a few thousand keys on kinds of words; every decision is a chart that is read domain by domain; there is no memory between sentences, so there is no hidden state that drifts over a conversation; facts sit in a database where they can be corrected. All of this is about the pilot, not about the large model, which remains as opaque as before. What we make transparent is the interface: the place where language becomes action.

Attacks on the pilot itself. An adversary who knows the mechanism may try paraphrases constructed to read into a harmless frame, unknown words placed deliberately to break a key, crowded sentences meant to drown a domain. Everything that breaks the reading lowers the margin or raises the residual and falls into “unverifiable”, which is the safe default; but an attacker who knows the route book can construct sentences with a large margin and a wrong frame, and the rate at which that succeeds must be measured, with an automated attacker (a large model set to generate adversarial paraphrases), not assumed.

What it does not solve

It says nothing about the internal objectives of large models and does not change them. It does not work on open domains, without a legend. A wrong or incomplete legend gives a wrong or incomplete check, confidently. And everything above rests on a number reported in the status file, the confident-wrong rate; it sets the thresholds, not the architecture.

17. A disciplined speculation: a system that discovers

Everything in the chapters so far is either measured or stated as a direction with a precise mechanism behind it. This chapter goes further than any direction before it, and says so at the start: nothing in it is built, nothing is measured, and nothing will be until a world is built for it. It is an engineering exercise on paper: if you wanted a system that discovers, that proposes hypotheses, tries them, notices what is new, revises its conclusions and learns from them, what would it gain from being built around the chart rather than around a large model alone? Where a piece already exists, we say in which chapter; where it does not, we say that it does not.

Why around the chart

A system that discovers needs four things a language model does not give directly. A representation in which novelty is measurable, not felt: ours has the remainder (chapter 8) and the region of novelty (chapter 12). A form in which a hypothesis and an observation can be compared piece by piece: ours compares constellations domain by domain (9.2). A theory that fits in a short, editable text: ours is the legend, a few dozen lines, plus the list of word kinds. And a number for what it does not know, per question: the margin, the occupancy and the remainder. The large model has, in turn, what the chart lacks: a great deal of language, a great deal of culture, the capacity to propose. The picture of 9.2 applies whole: the fluent part proposes, the verifiable part validates, and the validations come back as data.

Hypotheses are constellations

A hypothesis is, here, a sentence in a world with a legend: “Enzyme X slows reaction Y at temperature Z.” Read by the pilot, it becomes a constellation: X in the agent’s domain, Y in the process domain, “slows” in the action domain, Z in the condition domain. An observation is also a sentence, coming from data (6.4, the second source: structures that already exist beside text), and it too becomes a constellation. The first level of “testing” a hypothesis is therefore exactly the comparison of 9.2, with its four verdicts: agreement, compatible but incomplete, contradiction (in which domain), unverifiable. None of this understands enzymes; all of it is cheap, repeatable and traceable to a sentence and to a line of the legend. What this level does not give is the causality or the statistics of a confirmation; it gives a correct bookkeeping of what supports what, and that is exactly what is usually missing around a large model.

Where hypotheses come from: three sources

From large models: they propose, in language; the pilot formalises, and what does not fit the legend comes out marked unverifiable. That mark is itself information: a hypothesis the legend cannot hold is either badly phrased or calls for a new question, and the second possibility is where new concepts start, below.

From people: the same path, with typed sentences; the system does not distinguish a hypothesis from a person from one from a model, except in provenance, which it keeps.

From observations, deduced: reading a whole corpus of observations produces the list of frames of chapter 5, a knowledge graph relative to the legend. Regularities in that graph are candidate hypotheses: “whenever X is in the agent’s domain, Y is in the result domain”. Here the chart helps directly, because constellations are points: the centre of gravity of all the constellations of one kind (2.5) is the typical constellation of that kind of event; an observation far from it is a deviation, with its remainder; a direction in which constellations deviate together is a regularity. You need not understand X and Y to see that they always go together; you need only the legend and many observations, exactly as with bias in chapter 12.

Novelty, and where new concepts come from

Novelty is the remainder, and chapter 12 said why: what does not fit the legend shows as stars at unnamed points. A system that discovers does not look at the remainder of a sentence but at the remainder of a corpus: if the same unknown star reappears at the same relative position, in many sentences, the remainder is not noise, it is structured. This much is already measured on a constructed world: with one role removed from the legend, the size of the remainder gave nothing away, but the remainders clustered by the hidden answer, with no vector and no labels (chapter 8). A structure that repeats in the remainder is a candidate new concept, and “concept” has an operational meaning here: a new question in the legend, a named star, together with the kind of words that answer it. To propose a concept is to propose a line in the legend. To accept it is measurable: with the line added and the route book retrained (minutes, chapter 6), the corpus’s remainder falls, the frames read with margin, and the answers to the new question can be found again. Two pieces exist: the tool described in the project’s repository already has the loop in which a model proposes legend lines and keeps only those that pass a measured criterion (9.3), and the experiment that checked whether the remainder points to the missing question found that it does by direction, not by size (chapter 8). The rest is speculation: that the structure in the remainder can be grouped automatically into candidates, and that the large model, seeing the unknown stars and their contexts, can propose the name of the question.

What is valuable in a hypothesis: a bet, not an oracle

A system that proposes more than it can try has to choose. In the chart’s terms, the value of a hypothesis can be estimated before testing it through a few quantities, all computable from constellations. Coverage: how much of the corpus’s remainder it would explain, if true and added to the legend. Linkage: how many domains, so far uncorrelated, it would connect; a hypothesis that joins two parts of the graph is worth more than one that adds a fact to an already dense part. Contradiction: how many observations contradict it, and in which domain; a hypothesis contradicted by firm data is probably wrong, one contradicted by a few isolated observations points at interesting data. Novelty against what is known: the distance of its constellation from the centre of gravity of the hypotheses already confirmed; near means redundant, far means new or absurd, and contradiction says which. The estimated value is, roughly, the expected drop in unexplained remainder divided by the cost of trying. It is a ranking, not a prediction; how good it is can be learned only by trying hypotheses ranked this way and counting how many of the first ones were confirmed. We do not have that number.

How it reviews itself

Review is not a separate step but the verifier of 9.2 turned on the system’s own conclusions. Every statement the system wants to keep is a sentence, hence a constellation; it is re-read and compared with the constellations of the observations, with the four verdicts, its margins and its remainder. An unverifiable conclusion is not published, it is queued, with the reason: unrecognised domain, crowded domain, outside the legend. A contradicted conclusion keeps the contradiction beside it, in the named domain. And the second look of 6.4 applies: where the pilot and the large model disagree with a large margin, the case is reviewed, by another model or by a person. The rule of this whole project, nothing given without declaration, applies to the system itself: every conclusion carries its provenance (from which observations, from which hypothesis, with which version of the legend), and a conclusion without provenance does not exist.

How it manages its insights

An insight is, operationally, a confirmed frame that changed something: a new line in the legend, a regularity in the graph, a resolved contradiction. It is kept as what it is: the constellation, the frame, the provenance and the version of the legend it was read with. Because it is a point, it is found again by similarity: a new question is read, and the insights close to its constellation surface without an index and without keywords; the centre of gravity of a group of insights is a theme, and the distance to it says how far a question is from what is already understood. Insights confirmed again and again are candidates to become legend lines, that is, to pass from observed to declared; those later contradicted go down, with the contradiction recorded. This memory is in the weights of no model: it is a catalogue of constellations, inspectable, which a person can read line by line. The difference from a large model’s memory is not one of capacity but of addressability: you know what is in it and where each thing comes from.

How it would improve its own large model

Here the loop of 9.2 closes in the other direction, and it is the most speculative part. Everything that was verified is clean training material: (sentence, frame) pairs to formalise better; (hypothesis, verdict) pairs to propose better hypotheses; (structured remainder, accepted question) pairs to propose concepts. The large model is retrained or fine-tuned only on what passed through the verifier, never on its own unverified outputs; the chart is the filter. The pilot, in turn, is retrained whenever the legend changes, in minutes, because all it learns is the route book. People stay where chapter 16 put them: at the approval of legend lines, because that is where it is decided what can be said at all. This is not self-learning in the sense of a system that improves itself without a boundary; it is a pair in which the fluent part may learn only from what the verifiable part has confirmed, and in which every step leaves a trace you can read.

What is built, what is planned, what is speculation

Built and measured: the reader with its margins and remainder, the verifier with its four verdicts, the teacher on a sample, the tool’s legend loop with its acceptance criterion. Measured on one constructed world: that a question missing from the legend is detectable by the direction of the remainder, not by its size (chapter 8). Speculation, with no measurement at all: the automatic grouping of the remainder into concept candidates, the ranking of hypotheses by value, the memory of insights as a catalogue of constellations, and the retraining of the large model on verified frames. This chapter does not ask to be believed; it asks to be read as a list of experiments that have not been done, written precisely enough that, for each, one can see what it would mean to fail.

18. What it is, and what it is not

The story of the sky, the probe and the pilot is an image; this chapter sets it aside and says what kind of system this is, what it compares against, and where its limits are, in the terms in which it would be discussed in a paper.

What it is

It is a vector-symbolic reader of sentences: a learned addressing scheme, laid over a fixed algebra. The algebra is that of phase-based vector symbolic architectures (binding by element-wise multiplication, bundling by addition, cleanup by nearest neighbour); the word identities and the role vectors are random and fixed; what is learned are the displacement vectors per word category, the relative vectors of the expectations and one gate per category, in total ten to fifteen megabytes. The output is a state that encodes a role-filler frame, nested by repeated binding, and that is read back by unbinding and cleanup, with a margin per role and a reconstruction residual per sentence. The training objective is the match, by cosine, with a target state built by a world with explicit roles; the supervision is structural and per sentence, with roles given in advance, and contains no paraphrase labels.

As a type of system, it is a frame-based semantic parser (a frame parser) with a geometric particularity: the state is not produced by a decoder but composed algebraically, so that composition at unseen depths, the handling of new vocabulary and the uncertainty diagnostics follow from the algebra, not from learning. It holds no facts: the facts live in a database or a reasoning engine, and the frame translates mechanically into Prolog facts or SMT constraints.

What it compares against

Against transformers and language models. There is no next-word prediction objective and there is no generation. The fair comparison is with a small transformer trained on the same input and the same targets: on constructed worlds, at a nesting depth not encountered in training, the reader decodes the inner roles completely, while the transformer is at chance level; on WikiSQL, in a setting modified from the official protocol (the question alone, without the table schema; the selected column is predicted, not the executed query), the selected column is 0.82 against 0.54 for the transformer with the same input (0.65 with a ten times larger budget) and 0.50 for a bag-of-words logistic regression, at all training-set sizes from one thousand examples upward (the table by size, with seeds and intervals, is in the repository). In the other direction, where the decision is lexical (the aggregator in the same queries), the neural classifiers win (0.89), and whole-query accuracy (0.37) is not directly comparable with the figures of specialised systems, which see the table schema and report other metrics; it is far from them. The claim being made is therefore precise: on the same input, without external knowledge, with little data, the reader learns the role structure better and generalises compositionally; it is not a competitor on open text and does not claim to be.

Against semantic parsers with the same supervision (semantic role labelling, frame parsing). The supervision is of the same kind: structured targets per sentence. The differences are the representation (a composable vector state, not a tree produced by a decoder), the vocabulary (random identities plus category, hence new words without retraining) and the reading of uncertainty (margin, residual, abstention). The direct comparison with a classical parser trained on exactly the same targets, one classifier per role, is in the measurement plan; until then, we compare on properties, not on numbers.

Against tensor and holographic representations (Smolensky, Plate, Kanerva, the fractional power encoding of Frady, Kleyko and Sommer). Encoding frames as a sum of question ⊗ answer pairs is standard and thirty years old. What is new here is that the addresses are learned from the generative objective, that they are redundant (three address codes fused), that addressing through expectations with the re-placement of early words gives clean addresses and composes one level further than it was trained, and that the gates (which categories open frames) emerge by gradient and coincide across worlds and with real text (with three gates fixed by hand and declared, step 8 of chapter 13). We also checked what does not emerge: the word categories coincide with those given by a simple skip-gram on the same sentences and are not claimed.

Against the Thousand Brains theory, chapter 15: reference frames with locations given by movement, features at locations, composition by displacement, voting by superposition; no thousands of columns, no unsupervised learning, with the frame given by the world.

Against latent world models (Dreamer, JEPA): the same formal task, state plus event gives the next state, with a readable state instead of a latent one; a direction, not a result.

What it is not

It is not a language model; it is not a knowledge base; it is not unsupervised; it is not a replacement for large models on open text. Its realistic place is beside a large model, as the part that produces a verifiable frame and says what it could not place.

What could change the message

It is only fair that we say which future measurements could move the scientific message of this book, and in which direction. Three can. Calibration: if the confident-wrong rate comes out high and does not fall with the known improvements, “it says where it did not understand” becomes “it reports uncertainty, but poorly calibrated”, and the chapters on verification, robotics and alignment must be rewritten accordingly; it is the measurement with the highest stakes. Real text with free paraphrases, against a baseline with the same supervision: if a classical per-role classifier, trained on exactly the same targets, matches the reader on real text, the message moves from “learns the structure better” to “comparable in accuracy, with composition at unseen depths and with diagnostics”; the uniqueness stays with composition and with reading back, not with the figures. Operators after the subject: the subject followed by two operators is read correctly in the frame (chapter 8, “Order”), but its address does not tell the two orders apart; if a world needs the subject as a witness of the structure, “operators are roles, read without rules” acquires a declared boundary there.

What cannot change: the mathematical facts (isometry, commutativity, the unrelatedness of random points, the scaling of capacity) and the results already measured on constructed worlds. And a few measurements can only improve things: pronouns, the sparse code. The thesis, meaning as answers to a legend’s questions, reached by a learned route, rests on what cannot change.

Limits

The structural limits: the capacity of a state (tens to hundreds of pairs at a thousand-odd dimensions; long texts are structures of states); the commutativity of binding (the order of two operators is retained only when something else differs; the non-commutative remedies give up free composition and are to be tested); forward-only reading (a word before two stacked operators is placed by the first only, with a single measured cost in the frame, one form at the unseen depth; chapter 8, “Order”); a new word whose category neither the dictionary nor the tagger can give is left without a key (8, “The broken key”).

The results at the time of writing come from constructed worlds, with five seeds, and from a single real dataset. What is measured, what is in progress and with which criterion is kept up to date in the project’s status file: https://github.com/ml-circuits-research/vsa-semantic-space/blob/main/STATUS.md. This document does not change with every experiment; the file does.
