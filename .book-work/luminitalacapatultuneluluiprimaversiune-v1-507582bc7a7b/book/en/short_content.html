Is There Light at the End of the Tunnel?

Why the exit resembles the entrance

The book begins with a recurring danger: institutions created to end dependence can recreate it in another form. Equality may shelter a governing caste; economic freedom may buy political influence; democratic choice may empower those who would close future choices. The author treats the initiator’s experience of communism as testimony and a source of questions, not a representative sample. Empirical results, theoretical arguments, proposed mechanisms, and institutional designs have different evidential status. The aim is not a flawless political formula, but a civilization able to notice and correct its own failures.

Power changes its costume

A political antinomy arises when two defensible demands undermine each other if pursued without limits or supporting institutions. Security can require discretion, while discretion protects abuse. Privilege need not consist of money: office, control of careers, access to information, and symbolic standing may convert into one another. The author offers this conversion as a hypothesis to investigate, not a universal law. The experience of a bureaucratic caste under communism illustrates why leveling incomes does not by itself level power. Conversely, that failure does not show that progressive taxation, public services, or cooperatives require party monopoly.

Merit, markets, and correction

Competence deserves recognition, but civic status is not a prize for performance. A specialist can merit autonomy over a task without gaining authority over everyone else’s life. Markets use dispersed knowledge that planners struggle to gather; prices still reflect purchasing power and rules, not all social value. Protection can allow people without inherited wealth to take risks, while repeated rescues may weaken discipline. Capitalism gives many real freedoms, yet legal permission to compete does not equal a fair probability of success. When winners can rewrite the rules, reward converts into political power. The response is to preserve correction and entry, not simply praise or condemn a system.

Freedom against itself

A person may be free to speak while public attention is fragmented, drowned in volume, or shaped by dependence. The book distinguishes these mechanisms from censorship in its strict sense. Studies of algorithmic feeds have different findings across platforms and interventions, so one universal story would exceed the evidence. Collective decisions face another limit: Arrow’s theorem and Sen’s example illuminate tensions among preference aggregation, rights, and social choice; neither abolishes democracy. Experts can identify what is feasible, but they cannot decide all common ends. Deliberation needs a decision point, followed by reasons, appeal, and review.

Resentment and democratic defense

The book separates different elites rather than treating wealth, expertise, and political office as one class. Frustrated expectations can become resentment, but economic hardship does not define anyone’s culture or civic character. Political entrepreneurs can redirect grievance toward domination; understanding the grievance does not license threats or violence. Democracy must defend the future possibility of opposition without appointing an unchecked police of opinions. Rights protect even uninformed or disagreeable citizens because they limit the power to classify people as unworthy. Civic education should develop verification and the ability to lose a decision without surrendering the right to contest it.

Demography without destiny

The uncomfortable hypothesis about fertility, inherited traits, and democratic decline is reconstructed as a chain that requires evidence at every link. A polygenic score associated with education is not civic virtue; heritability within a population does not automatically explain differences between groups. The cited within-family and education studies complicate simple stories of genetic fate. Religion and culture are not genotypes, and poverty is not a genetic test. The defensible concern is institutional and intergenerational: whether children gain capabilities and whether exclusion and hostility are reproduced. Policy should be assessed by observable effects while protecting equal rights and voluntary reproductive care.

The promise and cost of decentralization

The proposal of constitutional cosmolocalism combines knowledge that circulates globally with more widely distributed economic capacities and rights that do not depend on local goodwill. No “ism” supplies every answer: libertarian, socialist, and classical liberal arguments each identify real dangers and leave others unresolved. Commons need governance; local communities can become oppressive; physical limits constrain production near home. A decentralized order also needs common defense, but the force needed for defense can restore the center it sought to limit. The book therefore asks who controls coercion, who can challenge it, and how emergency powers expire.

AI, income, and a life beyond work

Artificial intelligence does not abolish political economy. It can distribute expertise or concentrate the infrastructure on which expertise depends. A basic income can make refusal possible, yet it can also stabilize a world in which most people own nothing decisive and have little voice. Automation may free people from necessity while removing sources of rhythm, recognition, and purpose. The question is not what authorities should do “with” people whose labor is no longer needed, as though people were disposable inputs. The book asks how material security, ownership, services, political voice, and diverse sources of meaning can coexist without a ministry prescribing the good life.

A constitution open to correction

Even fairer distribution leaves tensions between transparency and privacy, efficiency and resilience, family care and equal opportunity, present needs and future claims. The closing constitutional proposal places limits on state and community ownership of the person, measures concentration along several axes, preserves alternatives in critical infrastructure, and makes important decisions attributable and appealable. Minimum income must not purchase silence; exceptional powers must expire; competence should be selected rather than crowned. These clauses are a proposal to test against adversaries, not a proof of a perfect order. The book’s “light” is the capacity to reverse accumulated domination and learn from error without destroying society.
