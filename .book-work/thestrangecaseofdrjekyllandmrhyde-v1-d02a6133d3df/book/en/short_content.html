The Strange Case of Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde

Ten-minute edition

Robert Louis Stevenson’s novella begins as a lawyer’s inquiry into an unsettling stranger and ends with a doctor’s account of an experiment that breaks his life apart. This edition also includes a modern companion perspective on Stevenson, Victorian society, and the story’s interlocking narrative threads.

About Robert Louis Stevenson

The companion sketch traces Stevenson’s Edinburgh upbringing, his move from engineering toward literature, his travels, and his interest in moral conflict. It places Jekyll and Hyde alongside his adventure writing while emphasizing identity and divided impulses. Read the full section.

Historical and Cultural Context

The companion reads the novella against Victorian respectability, class expectations, scientific change, and Gothic fiction. It argues that public propriety can conceal desires and fears that private life cannot easily resolve. These are interpretive lenses supplied by the modern perspective, rather than a replacement for Stevenson’s story. Read the full section.

Plot Summary by Narrative Threads

The companion follows Utterson’s investigation, Jekyll’s growing loss of control, Lanyon’s encounter with the transformation, and the final confession. It explains how these accounts reveal the central mystery at different times. The original novella preserves that sequence of discovery for the reader. Read the full section.

Story of the Door

During a Sunday walk, Enfield tells Utterson how an alarming man trampled a child and paid compensation with a cheque connected to a respectable household. The neglected door and the man’s elusive appearance trouble Utterson, who recognizes a link to his friend Dr. Jekyll’s will. Read the full chapter.

Search for Mr. Hyde

Utterson waits near the door and meets Edward Hyde. Hyde’s manner and appearance deepen his unease, although he cannot name what is wrong. Fearing blackmail, Utterson visits Jekyll’s home and learns that Hyde has a key and unusual freedom to enter. Read the full chapter.

Dr. Jekyll Was Quite at Ease

At a dinner, Utterson asks Jekyll about Hyde and the troubling will. Jekyll refuses a full explanation but insists he can be rid of Hyde when he chooses. He asks Utterson to protect Hyde’s interests if anything happens to him. Read the full chapter.

The Carew Murder Case

A maid witnesses Hyde attack Sir Danvers Carew. Utterson helps police identify Hyde’s rooms, but Hyde has disappeared. The violence turns Utterson’s private worry into an urgent question about Jekyll’s connection to a fugitive murderer. Read the full chapter.

Incident of the Letter

Jekyll says he is finished with Hyde and shows Utterson a letter apparently written by him. Yet the document’s provenance and handwriting raise fresh doubts. Utterson must choose how much to investigate and how much to trust his friend. Read the full chapter.

Incident of Dr. Lanyon

Jekyll briefly returns to sociability, then shuts himself away. Lanyon, another old friend, suffers a sudden shock and refuses to speak of Jekyll. His sealed account is to be opened only after Jekyll’s death or disappearance. Read the full chapter.

Incident at the Window

Utterson and Enfield encounter Jekyll at his window. A moment of conversation turns abruptly to terror, and the window closes. The brief scene shows Jekyll’s isolation without yet explaining its cause. Read the full chapter.

The Last Night

Jekyll’s servant Poole calls Utterson to the house, convinced that the person locked in the laboratory is not his master. Together they force the door. What they find ends the search for Hyde but leaves the deeper story to Jekyll’s papers. Read the full chapter.

Dr. Lanyon’s Narrative

Lanyon’s letter describes an urgent errand for Jekyll and a nighttime visitor who performs an impossible change before his eyes. The event overturns Lanyon’s understanding of his friend and of what science can explain. Read the full chapter.

Henry Jekyll’s Full Statement of the Case

Jekyll explains why he tried to separate the competing sides of his nature, how the potion made Hyde possible, and why the transformation escaped his control. His confession links the earlier incidents and traces the cost of trying to live without accepting moral responsibility for every part of himself. Read the full chapter.
