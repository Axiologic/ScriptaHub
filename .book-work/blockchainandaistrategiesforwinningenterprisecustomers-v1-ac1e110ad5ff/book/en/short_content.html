Blockchain and AI: Strategies for Winning Enterprise Customers

Ten-Minute Edition

This report asks how a blockchain or AI startup can win large enterprise customers before it has a finished product. Its answer begins with direct customer relationships, then develops a professional nonprofit community as a way to educate prospective clients and sustain early work. It also examines accelerators, training, European research grants, and the practical disciplines needed to turn a pilot into a viable business. The figures and funding arrangements in the report are proposals and examples, not guarantees.

Start with customers who can fund the work

The introduction describes a crowded market in which supply can exceed demand for high-profile enterprise clients. Pursuing organizations with no financial capacity may push a startup into a fiercely competitive market for scarce opportunities. The author therefore emphasizes distinctive value, an understanding of customers' specific needs, and relationships with industry leaders. The report draws on the author's experience of forming a startup for large clients rather than presenting a technical survey of blockchain or AI.

Chapter one: sell the problem-solving process first

A young company need not wait for a finished product to speak with potential clients. Networking, meetings, and presentations can reveal enterprise problems and lead to tailored proposals. A customer-funded pilot or minimum viable product can then be tested and refined with the organization paying for it. The report argues that this collaboration builds trust and a market reputation while reducing the risk of making a polished product that nobody buys.

Nor must the startup begin from zero. Open-source resources, prototypes, demonstrations, wireframes, and a capable team can make a proposed solution concrete enough for early discussions. The order matters: validate the problem, adapt available components, and build in response to demonstrated demand. To place this approach in context, the chapter cites Eric Ries on rapid MVP iteration, Clayton Christensen on unmet needs and Jobs to Be Done, Ash Maurya on problem validation, Steve Blank on customer development, Bill Aulet on a structured startup process, and Geoffrey Moore on the transition beyond early adopters. These references support an iterative, customer-driven strategy rather than a claim that one framework alone will secure an enterprise sale.

Chapter two: an educational nonprofit community

The second chapter proposes a professional nonprofit community around emerging technologies. Members would exchange knowledge, collaborate on research and product development, and pay annual fees that help sustain the organization while its commercial offerings mature. Workshops, courses, and discussions would teach potential clients about AI and blockchain and reveal the practical challenges they face. This is both a learning channel and a possible path toward later commercial projects.

The proposed launch begins with two or three prominent members whose participation lends credibility, especially in the pharmaceutical sector. As an illustration, the report imagines 100 members paying €2,000 or €5,000 each year. It does not establish that such a membership base already exists. The sales appeal initially targets individuals within prospective client organizations: networking, professional development, early insight into technology trends, and peer collaboration can help them advocate internally for their employer to pay the fee. The startup, in this model, offers a useful community before it has a product to sell.

Chapter three: promotion and funding

Accelerators and incubators may provide early mentorship, funding, visibility, and introductions to investors or enterprise clients. Training sessions and workshops can create both revenue and relationships. An online specialist community, including pharmaceutical professionals, can deepen discussion about applications and generate qualified leads, provided the startup invests in content and moderation.

The chapter also considers European research grants. It describes possible programs covering up to 70% of development costs, or up to 100% for more theoretical research, and outlines a public-private partnership in which private partners contribute half the project value through money or staff time while the EU matches their contribution. A startup or small consortium may gain funding; larger partners can share research costs and reach skills held by startups and academic institutions. The precise percentages are examples from the report and depend on the applicable program. The strategic point is to form a consortium around a genuine research problem and complementary capabilities.

Chapter four: practices for building the enterprise

The final chapter gathers the operating practices behind these proposals. Customer interviews should define real problems before a minimum viable product is built. Rapid tests and feedback should drive iteration, keeping the solution aligned with changing demand. Strategic partnerships and consortia can supply expertise, credibility, and market reach. Jobs to Be Done helps a team understand why a customer chooses a product, while experienced mentors and structured entrepreneurship frameworks help guide development and market entry.

The chapter returns to the educational community, accelerators, and thought leadership as ways to earn trust and make contacts. It also recommends European grants, continuing market feedback, and attention to the difficult passage from early adopters to mainstream customers. Security, data protection, and regulatory compliance matter especially for enterprise AI and blockchain work. A culture that permits experimentation, decisions informed by customer and market data, scalable offerings, recurring revenue, and sustained professional networking round out the list. Together, these practices connect sales, product design, funding, and organizational discipline.

A combined route to enterprise customers

The conclusion brings the route together: direct client relationships can lead to jointly refined pilots; a nonprofit professional community can fund education and build influence; accelerators, training, and online discussion can broaden access to partners; and research consortia, including potential Innovative Health Initiative programs, can supplement private investment. The book's central claim is practical rather than technological: a startup improves its chances by discovering an enterprise problem, earning trust around it, and financing development with interested partners.
