Wuthering Heights

Emily Brontë

1. A stranger on the moors

Mr. Lockwood rents Thrushcross Grange and visits his landlord, Heathcliff, at Wuthering Heights. The household seems hostile and difficult to understand: people are related in ways Lockwood cannot yet sort out, while ordinary hospitality gives way to arguments and a struggle with the dogs. A snowstorm traps him there. In a room associated with Catherine Earnshaw, he finds old writing and has a frightening dream of a voice at the window. Heathcliff’s response suggests that Catherine’s absence still rules the house. Lockwood returns to the Grange ill, and the housekeeper Nelly Dean begins the long account that explains the people he has met.

2. Catherine and the foundling

Nelly’s story begins years earlier, when Mr. Earnshaw brings a poor child home from Liverpool and names him Heathcliff. Catherine Earnshaw grows close to him, while her brother Hindley resents him. After their father dies, Hindley’s power makes Heathcliff’s place in the family harsher. Catherine and Heathcliff share the freedom of the moors, yet a visit to the more polished Linton household changes Catherine’s prospects. Edgar Linton offers affection, social position, and a future that her companion cannot easily offer. Catherine confides to Nelly that she intends to marry Edgar while declaring her deep bond with Heathcliff. He overhears only part of that confession and leaves. The wound lies in what each person feels and in what they fail to hear.

3. Return, marriage, and revenge

Heathcliff returns after several years with money and a changed manner. His arrival unsettles Catherine’s marriage to Edgar. It also gives him means to answer old humiliations through property and dependence. Hindley’s gambling weakens his hold on Wuthering Heights, and Heathcliff draws Edgar’s sister Isabella toward him despite warnings about his cruelty. Their marriage soon becomes a form of captivity for her. Catherine’s divided loyalties turn into illness, confrontation, and grief. Her reunion with Heathcliff before her death does not repair what their choices have broken. Nelly tells these events from within the households, and the reader must reckon with what she sees clearly, what she misjudges, and what others keep from her.

4. Children inherit the quarrel

Isabella escapes from Heathcliff and later raises their son, Linton, away from him. Catherine and Edgar’s daughter, also named Catherine, grows up protected at Thrushcross Grange. Hindley dies, and Heathcliff gains Wuthering Heights, while Hindley’s son Hareton grows under the influence of a man who wants the next generation to suffer for the last. These children do not begin with the same history as their parents, but the adults’ choices shape where they can live and whom they can trust. As young Catherine becomes curious about the neighboring house and its people, she discovers how much has been withheld from her. The novel shifts from one damaged bond to the fate of those forced to live after it.

5. A marriage arranged through fear

After Isabella’s death, her frail son Linton is brought under Heathcliff’s control. Heathcliff presses for a connection between him and young Catherine because a marriage can help him take Thrushcross Grange as well as Wuthering Heights. Visits, letters, illness, and Nelly’s changing ability to intervene create an increasingly narrow path for Catherine. Edgar’s own declining health makes time important. Heathcliff’s plan is not simply a private act of anger; it uses inheritance law, parental authority, and restricted movement to turn people into instruments. Catherine’s concern for Linton becomes a vulnerability he exploits. The novel asks readers to see the violence of coercion even when it is carried through agreements, households, and legal claims.

6. The two houses under one power

Heathcliff confines Catherine and Nelly at Wuthering Heights until Catherine agrees to marry Linton. Edgar dies after his daughter reaches him, and Linton soon dies as well. Heathcliff’s command over both estates appears complete. Catherine is left in a household where grief and anger harden her toward Hareton, who has himself been denied education and affection. Lockwood’s first bewildering visits now acquire their meaning: he encountered people living in the aftermath of these choices. Nelly’s account also shows how power works through daily arrangements, not only dramatic confrontations. Who may leave, read, learn, or speak kindly becomes part of the struggle over the houses.

7. Another way to live

When Lockwood returns, he learns that Catherine and Hareton have begun to meet one another differently. Her mockery gives way to apology and patient help with his reading; his resentment makes room for trust. Their growing friendship is neither an easy erasure of past harm nor a repetition of the earlier Catherine and Heathcliff. It comes from small choices made in the present. Heathcliff, meanwhile, becomes increasingly absorbed by memories and by the resemblance he sees around him. The drive to complete revenge loses its force, though he has not become a simple figure of repentance. The younger pair’s relationship offers a limited but real break in the inheritance of cruelty.

8. The quiet earth

Heathcliff dies at Wuthering Heights, and Catherine and Hareton plan a shared future. Lockwood walks to the graves of Catherine, Edgar, and Heathcliff. The ending does not settle every question raised by passion, injury, or the stories people tell about the dead. It lets the landscape hold several lives at once. Brontë’s novel has moved through layered narration, changing property, social rank, confinement, and desires that could not be made orderly. Its final contrast is between Heathcliff’s long fixation and the younger couple’s capacity to learn from one another. Readers can return to the complete text for the shifting voices and details that a short account cannot contain.
