A verifier would compare the summary's claims with accepted records. If a claim's status is stronger than the source's, it would flag an unjustified promotion. This kind of distinction is reusable across documents and may be a good target for library learning.
Two Links That Must Not Be Confused
The first link is translating text into a representation. The second is computing over that representation. A solver can impeccably prove that a conclusion follows from extracted rules while the extraction itself is wrong. Evaluation must measure both links.
For an initial experiment, we can generate documents from known structures and vary their expression. This supplies an exact reference for the relations studied. Later, authentic documents must be used with independent annotation and room for evaluator disagreement.
Synthetic data help isolate the mechanism, but may remain too close to the generator's language. Success on them does not automatically establish usefulness on real documents. The two stages must be reported separately.
Quality Criteria
Accuracy on relevant questions is one criterion, not the only one. Coverage, exception preservation, faithful attribution, source localization and justified abstention also matter. A very short summary may correctly answer three known questions yet become useless for the rest of the task.
Some evaluation questions must be hidden from the generator. Human assessment of usefulness must also be included without confusing it with stylistic preference. A reader may prefer a fluent text that omits precisely the important warning.
Cost must be compared with a reasonable baseline: an LLM with source access, search and similar checks. If MRP receives an additional verifier and memory denied to the alternative, the experiment does not isolate the architecture.
Usefulness and Limits
The application could be valuable for technical manuals, product documentation, internal procedures and research syntheses. Interpreter libraries would preserve recurring distinctions: condition versus conclusion, hypothesis versus reported fact, current state versus history, primary source versus indirect citation.
In domains with legal or medical consequences, this architecture should be studied as documentary support under specialist oversight, rather than presented as an autonomous authority. A proof about a summary's structure is not professional validation of its recommendations.
The intended advantage is reducing identifiable errors and verification costs across repeated documents. It is not a promise that every natural meaning can be fully formalized. A useful technology can have explicit limits and solve only certain types of distinctions very well.

Research and Discovery
Three Different Activities
A research agent can generate hypotheses, select experiments and evaluate results. These activities are not identical. An interesting hypothesis may be false; a well-chosen experiment may yield a negative result; a correct analysis may lead to no new discovery.
MRP could organize their relationship through interpreters with different roles. Some construct candidate models. Others calculate predictions. Others identify tests separating models or check whether a conclusion exceeds the data. Each role's success must be evaluated against its own target.
I would not define novelty through a model's internal surprise. A claim may be rare in the agent's representation and well known in the literature. Conversely, a new idea may be expressed in very ordinary terms. Evaluating scientific priority requires external sources and a delineated contribution.
Use Case: Two Mechanisms Compatible with the Data
Assume a simulated system in which two models explain the same passive observations. In the first, X and Y have a common cause. In the second, X influences Y. The example in chapter 7 shows that observing X and Y may not separate these mechanisms, while an intervention can.
A predictive interpreter identifies the models' agreement on existing data. An intervention interpreter calculates which experiments produce different outcomes. The metacontroller compares experiment costs with the value of separation. After observation, the model library is updated.
In a microworld, the evaluator knows the real mechanism. We can measure how many experiments identification requires and their total cost. In a real laboratory, assumptions about interventions, instruments and errors become part of the problem. Learning a Discovery Procedure
A more interesting contribution than solving one case would be learning a reusable procedure. For example, when two models differ only in one relation, the agent seeks an intervention that blocks the alternative explanation. The procedure must be formulated under conditions where this reasoning is valid.
The same pattern cannot be mechanically transferred to every system. The intervention may change other variables, be impossible or have delayed effects. A reusable interpreter should request these conditions rather than assume them.
The research library would therefore store both experiment templates and their preconditions. Learning would mean not merely “which experiment worked,” but “in which class of situations does that experiment identify the relevant difference?”
Compression, Explanation and Discovery
A shorter model may compress observations. This is useful but insufficient for causal explanation. An interpolating formula may predict existing examples without capturing intervention mechanisms. A reusable concept must be evaluated on new consequences.
I would require three distinct tests for a hypothesis: fit to known data, predictions under unseen conditions and, where causal meaning is claimed, predictions about appropriate interventions. Absence of the third test does not invalidate a predictive model, but limits its interpretation.
MRP can make these limits visible in the epistemic register. A result can be an excellent predictor while remaining unsupported as a mechanistic explanation. This separation helps researchers precisely because it prevents a tacit leap from performance to ontology.
Use Case: Reviewing a Literature
The agent receives a bounded corpus of articles and a research question. One interpreter extracts relevant claims, another identifies study methods and conditions, and another separates independent confirmations from citations of the same evidence.
An incident occurs when two results appear incompatible. The agent may discover differences in populations, measurements or definitions. The new distinction does not automatically resolve the dispute, but may reformulate the question so the conflict becomes investigable.
Evaluation would measure attribution correctness, identification of conditions and the usefulness of proposed questions for experts. I would not use the number of self-declared “new ideas” as the main metric. It is too easy to increase through rewording or unverifiable hypotheses.
The Relationship with the Human Researcher
The researcher establishes important problems, ethical limits and relevance criteria. The agent can help identify overlooked distinctions and explore their consequences. This assumes no rigid division between human creativity and automated computation; it requires explicit responsibilities and evidence.
An initial system should produce hypothesis dossiers: model, assumptions, data used, discriminating predictions, proposed experiments and reasons the result would matter. Its value can be evaluated before any spectacular discovery through time saved and methodological errors detected.
The application is ambitious but can be built gradually. The first realistic target is an agent making research more verifiable and choosing experiments better within a narrow domain. It is not a machine guaranteeing novelty or replacing scientific-community validation.
CHAPTER 22
Education and Interactive Agents
Education as Diagnosing Distinctions
A pupil or student may apply a rule to ordinary examples yet confuse two situations requiring different treatment. An MRP educational system would try to identify this collision in responses and propose contrastive examples. This is a design direction, not an empirical conclusion about pedagogical effectiveness.
For example, a student may believe order never matters in composition. The system offers commutative numerical operations and noncommutative state operations. The question is not whether the student memorizes an exception, but whether they recognize which property of the operations justifies or prohibits changing their order.
The agent would preserve an explicit model of hypotheses about the student's difficulty. A wrong answer may arise from misunderstanding the concept, an arithmetic error or ambiguous wording. It must not automatically become a permanent label of the person's ability.
Use Case: Interpreting a Condition
A learner confuses “if A, then B” with “if B, then A.” The system generates situations where the original implication is true but its converse false. It then changes the examples' domain to see whether the distinction transfers.
The pedagogical interpreter proposes an explanation and a set of questions. A separate evaluator measures progress on unseen problems. Memory should preserve which explanations were tried and under which conditions they helped, rather than reduce the person to a single score.
MRP's value would lie in selecting pedagogical interventions appropriate to the collision type. But the system must be compared with well-designed teaching methods and an adaptive version without induction of new interpreters. Usefulness cannot be inferred solely from the model's elegance.
What Must Not Be Optimized
Maximizing correct answers immediately after an explanation may favor memorization. Maximizing time spent in the application may favor dependence on assistance. An educational experiment should track retention, transfer and autonomy under conditions approved for participants.
Human evaluation requires an appropriate protocol and data protection. Before that, the mechanism can be tested with simulated agents having known errors. Those results would validate diagnosis within the model, not demonstrate real pedagogical effects.
The architecture must allow the teacher or learner to challenge the diagnosis. A technical explanation of a “missing distinction” does not justify reducing a complex human difficulty to a single presumed internal representation.
Agents That Observe and Act
An interactive agent can choose to look, measure, ask or execute an operation. In these situations, interpreters receive not only textual symbols but also images, sensor values or tool results. The MRP model does not exclude these inputs, but must declare which processing procedures are supplied.
A simple example is a simulated device that may be “on,” “faulty” or “pending.” The same indicator light can appear in several states. An image-only interpreter cannot separate the situations. An additional measurement or test command may produce the necessary distinction.
Here, learning an interpreter and choosing an observation become complementary. The new program can specify how to combine the indicator with the test response. But without performing the test, the program obtains no information about the current state. This preserves the separation between computation and data acquisition.
Use Case: A Workflow Assistant
An assistant can manage steps in an administrative or technical process: request, verification, approval, execution. Initially, it receives explicit states and simulated operations. The goal is to select the next permitted action and detect missing information.
A collision occurs if two requests have identical visible fields but one received an approval that was later revoked. The agent must preserve relevant history or consult the authoritative source. Another collision may arise from the difference between “approved” and “executed.”
A useful system would learn reusable checks for these distinctions without automatically executing actions outside its mandate. Evaluation would measure incorrect steps, unnecessary information requests, abstentions and total cost. Speed is insufficient if the agent skips mandatory conditions.
