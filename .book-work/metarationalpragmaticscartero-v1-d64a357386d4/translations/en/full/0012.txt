]. Linking a representation to observations and interventions provides operational grounding, but does not by definition resolve every philosophical question about meaning. The distinction between an engineering contribution and a complete theory of mind must be preserved.
At the same time, the absence of a complete theory does not prevent testing concrete capabilities. We can prove that a representation loses a distinction and show that a new program recovers it from the available data. This is precise progress, even if it does not answer every question about understanding.
Limits of Computability and Data
There is no general procedure that decides all interesting semantic properties of arbitrary programs; computability imposes limits, and complexity practically limits many decidable problems [
Barak
]. Induction and verification must therefore work in restricted languages, with approximations or the possibility of reaching no conclusion.
No more expressive language can create missing empirical information about the current world. No finite library guarantees sufficiency for all future questions. These limits do not require abandoning the program; they require explicit domains and criteria for extension.
A system may be right not to learn a new interpreter. If the task is unique, information is missing or cost exceeds benefit, abstention may be the best option. Constant adaptation is not an end in itself.
What Is Worth Retaining
The strongest conceptual contribution is the shift in attention from representations viewed as static objects to distinctions constructed through procedures. This formulation's mathematical contribution is modest but clear: we can define sufficiency, collisions, distances and some limits of refinement. The elementary results presented here are not claimed as new theorems in the specialist literature.
The possible engineering contribution is an environment in which these objects are executable, versioned and evaluated. The empirical contribution remains to be established: whether such an environment learns and transfers more efficiently than well-built alternatives.
In its most defensible form, Meta-Rational Pragmatics does not claim to have found the ultimate form of knowledge. It proposes a working question: what relevant distinction can the agent not yet make, what procedure would make it possible, and what evidence shows that it is worth retaining? A real technology can begin right here.

APPENDIX A
Elementary Mathematical Results
The results below are instructional proofs for the model used in this book. They are not claimed as original theorems. They show exactly where a guarantee arises and which assumptions cannot be removed. We use finite domains when this avoids difficulties with measurability or the existence of an effective representation.
A1. The Limit of a Representation That Confounds Labels
Consider N examples, each with a correct label, and a representation R. We partition the examples into classes according to their R value. A predictor that sees only R must use the same response distribution for every example in a class.
Within a class, the best deterministic answer is the most frequent label. Randomization cannot achieve a higher average accuracy either: the weighted average of label frequencies does not exceed the maximum frequency. The maximum number of correct answers in the class is therefore the frequency of its most common label.
Minimum empirical error = 1 − (sum of the majority-label frequencies in each class) / N.
For two equally probable histories with the same representation and opposite labels, the minimum error is 0.5. The bound applies to a predictor that receives no other information. If we give it the example's identity or the original history, we have changed the experiment.
A2. Sufficiency and Response Factorization
Let f be the correct answer to a deterministic question and R a representation on a finite domain. A function g such that f(h) = g(R(h)) for every h exists if and only if all histories with the same R value have the same answer f.
The forward direction is immediate: the same input to g produces the same result. For the reverse direction, we define g at each R value by its class's common answer. The condition guarantees that the definition is unambiguous.
The result asserts the existence of a function, not its efficiency. The table g may be enormous. For infinite domains, the mathematical existence of a factorization does not automatically imply a computable procedure or one accessible to the agent.
A3. Refinement Does Not Worsen Optimal Risk Without Costs
Suppose the new representation preserves the old one and adds D: Rnew(h) = (R(h), D(h)). Any old predictor can be simulated on the new representation by ignoring component D. Therefore, the lowest possible risk on the new representation cannot be greater than the lowest possible risk on the old one.
The conclusion concerns the optimal predictor within a sufficiently broad class. A concrete algorithm trained on finite data may perform worse after a component is added. Cost, estimation difficulty or overfitting risk may increase. The theorem does not guarantee progress by a real system after every refinement.
A4. The Number of Strict Refinements in a Finite Sample
A partition of N objects has at most N classes. If we start with K classes and each strict refinement increases the number of classes by at least one, there can be at most N − K strict refinements, provided we do not subsequently merge classes.
The proof is a counting argument. After m refinements we have at least K + m classes; since K + m cannot exceed N, we obtain m ≤ N − K. The bound says nothing about the cost of finding each refinement. Nor does it say that the final partition generalizes: separating every example may amount only to memorization.
A5. The Test Pseudometric
For every test t, total variation satisfies TV(P1, P3) ≤ TV(P1, P2) + TV(P2, P3). Each term on the right is at most the corresponding supremum over all tests. Taking the supremum on the left as well, we obtain dT(h1, h3) ≤ dT(h1, h2) + dT(h2, h3).
Nonnegativity and symmetry are inherited directly. The distance can be zero between distinct histories because the tests do not separate them. On classes of histories at zero distance, the function becomes a metric. Adding tests cannot decrease this supremum-defined distance, but may increase the cost of estimating it.
A6. The 2e Bound for Choosing an Action
Assume a finite set of actions. The estimated value of each action differs from its true value by at most e. Let a* denote a truly optimal action and â an action with the highest estimate. The symbol â is simply a name for the choice based on the estimate.
The true value of a* is at most its estimate plus e. By the definition of the choice, its estimate does not exceed the estimate of â. The estimate of â is at most the true value of â plus e. Combining these three relations, the true value of a* is at most the true value of â plus 2e.
The result requires error control for every action compared. A small average error, without control of the selected action, does not provide the same guarantee. For long plans, what the estimated value of each policy represents must be justified separately.
A7. Zero Errors Does Not Mean Zero Error Rate
Assume a fixed candidate, independent tests and the same true error probability r on every test. The probability of observing zero errors in n tests is (1 − r) to the power n. We choose the bound r so that this probability is 0.05. This gives r = 1 − 0.05 to the power 1/n.
For n = 100, the bound is approximately 0.0295, or 2.95%. Observing zero errors permits this one-sided 95% confidence bound under the stated model, not the conclusion that error is impossible. For large n, the approximation 3/n is useful because the negative natural logarithm of 0.05 is close to three.
The frequentist interpretation concerns the procedure's coverage over repetitions of the experiment, not a 95% probability assigned to the fixed parameter without a Bayesian model. The bound does not apply unchanged when the candidate was selected after trying many variants on the same tests.
A8. A Simple Control for a Finite Family of Candidates
We have K candidates fixed before evaluation, where K is a positive integer, and e and d are numbers strictly between zero and one. For any candidate with a true error rate of at least e, the probability of passing n independent tests without error is at most (1 − e) to the power n, which is at most exp(−n × e).
The probability that at least one of the K bad candidates passes is at most the sum of these probabilities: K × exp(−n × e). Independence between candidates is not necessary for this union bound. For the bound to be at most d, it suffices that n be at least [ln(K) + ln(1/d)] / e.
This is a limited guarantee: the class must be fixed independently of the evaluation set, the tests must respect the model, and the criterion is passing without errors. An adaptive search that changes the class without limit in response to results requires another analysis. The formula explains why the number of attempts matters; it does not by itself solve all of MRP validation.
APPENDIX B
Exercises with Commented Solutions
B1. Set or Sequence?
Problem: an agent stores only the set of operations “add 1” and “multiply by 2.” Starting from zero, can it determine the result of any two-operation sequence in which each appears once?
Solution: no. Addition followed by multiplication produces two; multiplication followed by addition produces one. The set is the same, but the result differs. A sufficient representation must preserve the order or other information from which the result can be computed. The number of operations does not repair the collision because it is two in both cases.
B2. What Needs Refining?
Problem: the log preserves the correct order, but the interpreter selects the first event. Is a new representation necessary?
Solution: not for this cause. The necessary information exists. The selection operation must be corrected. A system that adds a new representation may accidentally repair the result, but would miss the diagnosis and introduce unnecessary complexity.
B3. Noise or Collision?
Problem: the same representation sometimes produces success and sometimes failure. Has its insufficiency been demonstrated?
Solution: no. If the process is stochastic, both outcomes may come from the same distribution. Repetitions or a model showing that two situations with the same representation induce different distributions are necessary. A single disagreement between labels does not distinguish variability from lost information.
B4. The Missing Relation
Problem: x and y each take values between zero and ten, and the agent must prove that x − y is zero. What additional information is sufficient?
Solution: the relation x = y is sufficient. Narrowing the intervals separately may help in particular cases, but does not generally capture the dependency. The example shows why a relational representation can matter more than greater numerical precision for each variable considered separately.
B5. When Is Learning Worthwhile?
Problem: constructing and checking an interpreter costs 1,000 ms. The old cost is 40 ms per task, and the new cost, including selection, is 10 ms. How many uses are needed for a strict gain?
Solution: the saving is 30 ms per use. More than 1,000/30 uses are needed, hence 34. At 34, the old cost is 1,360 ms and the new cost 1,340 ms. The result assumes that accuracy remains comparable and that no additional invalidation costs arise.
