PREFACE
A Hypothesis That Must Be Able
to Lose
A technology of understanding does not need a definitive definition of the
mind to begin. It needs situations in which an agent confuses things it
should distinguish, operations through which it can correct that confu‐
sion, and independent criteria for checking whether it has progressed.
This book develops Meta-Rational Pragmatics, or MRP, starting from that
requirement.
The central intuition is that an agent's understanding does not reside ex‐
clusively in a repository of representations. It also depends on the proced‐
ures through which the agent constructs representations, uses them, com‐
pares them with experience and replaces them. Some procedures inter‐
pret a text; others track a sequence of events, estimate an outcome, verify
a proof or produce a new procedure. In this sense, the agent accumulates
not only answers but ways of making certain differences visible.
The book turns this intuition into an engineering proposal: an adaptive
family of interpreters controlled through tests, counterexamples, costs
and evidence. The expression “executable distinction” names a difference
a program can compute and whose relevance can be checked. For ex‐
ample, the order in which permission is granted and revoked is an ex‐
ecutable distinction. The convincing tone of an explanation is not, by it‐
self, evidence that the agent preserved it.
The work is organized as a progressive course. It introduces mathematic‐
al objects before using them; explains equivalence relations before the
learned automaton; separates probability from utility before metareason‐
ing; and introduces distances and convexity before cones. Readers need
not know abstract interpretation, predictive representations or program
synthesis. Elementary familiarity with programs and tables is useful, but
the examples do not require reading any particular programming lan‐
guage.
MRP is presented neither as a validated theory of human cognition nor as
a product already built. Established elements are attributed to the literat‐
ure. Elementary mathematical results are proved within the limits of
their assumptions. Architectural components are proposals. Numerical
4


examples are teaching calculations, not prototype measurements. Applic‐
ations are analyzed as testing opportunities, not fulfilled commercial
promises.

PART I / The Question and the Language
CHAPTER 01
Understanding as an
Engineering Problem
From Impression to Capability
Suppose an assistant receives two records: “access granted, then revoked”
and “access revoked, then granted.” It identifies the same people, re‐
sources and two operations in both records. Yet if the system's rule is that
the last operation prevails, the two situations do not permit the same de‐
cision. A rich vocabulary about access does not compensate for losing the
order.
This example establishes a narrower question than “does the agent really
understand?” The question is whether the agent has the representation
and procedure needed to answer a family of questions correctly. We will
call this property operational understanding. The term does not resolve
disputes about consciousness, intentionality or subjective experience; it
selects a dimension that can be investigated through systems that are
built and compared.
The difference between the two questions matters methodologically. For
the first, identical behaviors can receive different philosophical interpret‐
ations. For the second, we can construct examples with known answers,
modify the available information and observe which component enables
progress. This narrowing does not make the problem trivial. Discovering
what must be represented without the designer supplying every distinc‐
tion is precisely the difficult problem.
Three Different Theses
The first thesis says that any intelligent behavior can be described
through a family of interpreters. This is plausible as an organizing per‐
spective, but too permissive to function alone as an empirical hypothesis.
Any sufficiently complex program can be retrospectively divided into
modules and described this way.
6


The second thesis says that certain errors arise because the available rep‐
resentation identifies situations requiring different outcomes. This thesis
is precise and admits local proofs. If two inputs are transformed into ex‐
actly the same value and a subsequent mechanism sees only that value,
the mechanism cannot respond differently based on information already
lost.
The third thesis is MRP's wager: in certain problem families, an agent that
detects such collisions and learns reusable interpreters will achieve a bet‐
ter trade-off between error, cost and adaptability than comparable altern‐
atives. This does not follow logically from the first two theses. It requires a
concrete architecture and experiments that include every cost.
These three levels must not be mixed. A proof about lost information does
not establish a product's superiority. A useful prototype in a microworld
does not establish a universal theory of the mind. Conversely, a debatable
philosophical claim does not prevent testing the engineering mechanism.
Representation and Interpretation
A representation is a structure the agent can store or manipulate: a num‐
ber, table, vector, graph, expression or program. An interpreter is a pro‐
cedure that assigns operational consequences to such structures: answers,
transformations, predictions or actions. The two are connected but not
identical.
An event table does not produce the current state by itself. A rule for or‐
dering and updating is required. The same rule applied to a table lacking
timestamps or order indicators may become unusable. The capability be‐
longs to the representation–operation pair, not either half in isolation.
The symbol grounding problem, formulated by Harnad, asks how sym‐
bols can be connected to something beyond other symbols [Harnad1990].
MRP does not claim to settle this philosophical problem. It addresses it
practically through verifiable links between representations, sources,
sensors and the effects of actions. An identifier in a report becomes opera‐
tional when it can be traced to the observation or object it refers to.
This grounding can be indirect. An agent analyzing documents need not
have its own sensors for every claim. But it must distinguish “I observed,”
“the source states,” “it follows under the model's assumptions” and “I pro‐
pose as a possibility.” These distinctions belong to the architecture, rather
than being mere polite phrases.
7


Pragmatic Does Not Mean Opportunistic
In this book, pragmatics concerns using an interpretation within a con‐
text, for a purpose and under constraints. It does not mean a proposition
becomes true if it is convenient. A system that learns to obtain user ap‐
proval through false answers may succeed under a poorly defined reward
and fail epistemically.
For this reason, the design must separate three evaluations. Correctness
checks whether the result follows the assumed rules. Empirical adequacy
checks whether predictions match observations. Utility checks whether
the decision serves the task's legitimate purpose. A single score may be
useful in a well-bounded experiment, but must not hide the differences
between these evaluations.
“Meta-rational” names the choice and development of a way of reasoning.
An agent may decide that a numerical comparison is sufficient, that time
must be tracked or that an observation is missing. It is not a magical logic
above all other logics. It is the management of finite cognitive and compu‐
tational resources.
A Working Definition
MRP will designate a mechanism through which an agent constructs, se‐
lects, composes and revises interpreters using verifiable consequences
and declared costs. MRP progress occurs when this reorganization pro‐
duces competence on new cases, rather than merely correcting the case
that triggered it.
The definition leaves implementations open. Interpreters may be symbol‐
ic, numerical, neural or hybrid. Some may be supplied by the designer;
others may be learned. To avoid confusion, every experiment will separ‐
ately report what was provided, what was induced and what was merely
selected. This accounting is the first condition of a defensible result.

CHAPTER 02
A Minimal Mathematical
Vocabulary
Sets, Functions, Relations
A set is a collection of objects treated as distinct elements. In our example,
the set of possible events may contain granting and revoking access. A se‐
quence differs from a set: it preserves order and usually repetitions. The se‐
quence “grant, revoke” is not the sequence “revoke, grant,” although the sets
of their elements are identical.
A function associates a single result with every admissible input. The nota‐
tion R(h) means the result of representation function R applied to history h.
If R preserves only the set of events, the two histories have the same image.
A partial function is not defined for every input; for example, “the last
event” does not exist for an empty sequence unless an additional conven‐
tion is introduced.
A relation need not provide a unique result. The relation “depends on” can
connect a result to multiple sources. The relation “is compatible with” can
connect an observation to several models. Distinguishing functions from re‐
lations allows us to preserve ambiguities without prematurely turning them
into an arbitrary choice.
Composition executes one transformation after another. If E extracts the
events and R reduces them to a state, R(E(text)) denotes the entire path.
Composition is valid only if the first operation's result is an admissible input
to the second. This mathematically simple condition will become an import‐
ant contract between interpreters.
Equivalence and Partition
An equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. Reflexivity
requires every object to be equivalent to itself. Symmetry requires equival‐
ence to work in both directions. Transitivity requires that if the first object is
equivalent to the second and the second to the third, the first is equivalent
to the third.
9


Such a relation divides the domain into disjoint classes called equivalence
classes. The collection of classes is a partition. Grouping histories by the res‐
ult R(h) automatically yields a partition: the same R value means the same
class. A representation can therefore be understood as a way of specifying
which differences we ignore.
One partition refines another when it separates some classes without mer‐
ging elements already separated. For example, classifying documents by
type and year refines classification by type alone. It can preserve old in‐
formation and add new information. This does not mean it will automatic‐
ally produce a better algorithm: it may increase cost and require more es‐
timation data.
We will sometimes use the term “quotient space.” It denotes the set of equi‐
valence classes, not a special geometric space. We replace each concrete his‐
tory with the class of all histories considered equivalent. Geometry, if any,
must subsequently be added through a distance or another structure.
Orders and Graphs
A partial order allows some pairs to be compared without requiring every
pair to be comparable. An interpreter preserving event order and another
preserving documentary sources may be incomparable: each conserves in‐
formation the other ignores. There need not be a single sequence of repres‐
entations from “bad” to “good.”
A graph consists of nodes and links. In MRP, nodes may be operations or res‐
ults; links may represent dependencies. If a computation must first receive
two results, the graph specifies that condition. A directed graph without
cycles allows operations to be ordered so that each follows its dependencies.
This is called a topological ordering.
A cycle is not necessarily an error. It may describe repeating an update until
stabilization. But a rule must explain what repeats, which state changes and
when the process stops. A circular drawing is not yet an executable al‐
gorithm.
Predicate and Invariant
A predicate is a property that can be true or false of an object within the
chosen model. “Every event has an order index” is a predicate on a table. An
invariant is a property that must remain true throughout an execution or a
class of transformations.
10


For an MRP library, one possible invariant is that every accepted result pre‐
serves the identifiers of the sources used. This does not mean the sources
are true; it means the result's origin is not lost. Another invariant may re‐
quire an operation declared read-only not to modify external state.
A proof starts from assumptions and uses explicit rules to obtain a conclu‐
sion. A test executes one or more cases. A proof may cover every case in the
model; a test does not acquire that power merely by being automated. In
turn, a proof does not by itself check whether the model correctly describes
the real world.
Cost and Size
A problem's size may be the number of events, the number of candidate in‐
terpreters or the maximum length of programs being searched. A linear pro‐
cedure visits a number of elements proportional to the size. An exponential
one may double the search space with every unit increase. These differences
explain why a logically correct idea can be impractical.
Computability and complexity distinguish the existence of an algorithm
from the resources needed to execute it. Introductory treatments of theoret‐
ical computer science develop this distinction systematically [Barak]. For
MRP, it translates into a requirement: every induction mechanism must
have search, time and memory limits, even if its conceptual description is
very general.

CHAPTER 03
Programs, Semantics,
Interpretation
What Semantics Establishes
Syntax determines which expressions are well formed. Semantics determ‐
ines how they are interpreted. In a programming language, it may de‐
scribe state transitions, an execution's result or the properties commands
preserve. Semantics must therefore not be reduced to a static dictionary
of meanings. Abstract interpretation explicitly offers multiple semantics
and relations between them [Cousot1977].
Consider a command that adds one to a number. Its semantics must spe‐
cify whether the number is an unbounded mathematical integer or an in‐
teger represented by a fixed number of bits. At the maximum represent‐
able value, the answers may differ. Simply saying “it understands addi‐
tion” conceals an essential modeling choice.
Operational semantics describes execution steps. Denotational semantics
associates a program with a mathematical object describing its behavior.
Axiomatic semantics expresses relationships between input conditions
and output properties. These perspectives can complement one another.
MRP does not replace them with a broader term; it treats them as tools
available to the agent.
What Pragmatics Adds
A semantic rule does not always decide by itself whether it is worth ap‐
plying, which information is missing or which goal must be satisfied. Here
we introduce the pragmatic level: choosing the interpretation regime, rel‐
evant context and stopping criterion. Once chosen, the interpretation's
rules must remain stable enough for verification.
We can call an interpretation whose domain and obligations are fixed for
an execution “stabilized semantics.” This is a convention internal to the
book, not a universally accepted classification in the philosophy of lan‐
guage. It enables a useful principle: the agent may change interpretation
between revision episodes, but cannot tacitly change the meaning of rules
in the middle of a proof.
12


For example, a system may switch from mathematical integers to 32-bit
arithmetic. This is not merely an optimization if it changes results. It
changes the model; old conclusions must be reexamined wherever the
difference matters.
The Interpreter as an Object
In the proposed model, an interpreter receives input, context, goal and in‐
ternal state. It produces a result, a new internal state and evidence. Here
“evidence” will denote verifiable support: sources, tests, computational
traces or proofs, rather than merely the agent's expressed conviction.
Interpret(input, context, goal, state) = (result, newState, evidence).
This expression is a conceptual interface, not a complete algorithm. Its
fields must be restricted for each concrete interpreter. An order analyzer
may require a table with unique indices. A verifier may require a formula
in a precise logic. A generator may accept text, but its output will initially
have proposal status.
Context must not become a place to hide unlimited information. If the se‐
lector can consult the full text while the baseline predictor receives only a
summary, the comparison measures more than interpreter quality. Every
experiment will declare exactly what each component can read.
Contracts and Composition
A contract specifies preconditions, promised results and permitted effects.
Hoare-style logic expresses the relationship between a precondition, a
command and a postcondition. Partial correctness says that if execution
terminates and the precondition held, the postcondition holds; termina‐
tion requires a separate obligation [SoftwareFoundations].
Consider an operation selecting a resource's last event. Its contract may
require a nonempty history, a total ordering and unambiguous resource
identification. The postcondition says the returned event belongs to the
resource and is followed by no other event for that resource. This post‐
condition can be checked independently of how the operation was imple‐
mented.
Composing two interpreters requires more than matching formats. If the
first returns “the last known event,” the second cannot assume “the last
event that occurred in the world” without assuming the history is com‐
plete. This is an example of semantic incompatibility in an apparently
technically correct interface.
13


Probabilistic interpreters require more cautious contracts. “It frequently
produced correct answers” is not a universal logical postcondition. We
can describe the tested domain, evaluation distribution and performance
uncertainty. Composing probabilistic components does not turn those res‐
ults into certainty.
Learning as an Interpreter
A learning procedure interprets data through a family of models and an
adjustment criterion. It produces parameters, rules or programs. It can
therefore be included in the same interface schema. Yet this does not
make every operation identical: changing a temporary result differs from
changing the library on which future tasks will depend.
We will separate working state from consolidated state. A new hypothesis
can exist in an experimental branch without becoming an accepted rule.
Promoting it requires tests and an explicit decision. This separation lets
the agent explore without treating exploration as truth already acquired.
What Remains Stable
An architecture capable of modifying itself needs a core stable enough to
determine which modifications have occurred. The core may be small:
version management, execution rules, evidence types and cost account‐
ing. It need not contain every domain concept in advance.
MRP becomes interesting if its core enables useful interpreters to develop
without rewriting the entire system. Manually supplied components are
legitimate, but must be declared separately from learned ones.

CHAPTER 04
Probability, Decision, Truth
Uncertainty Is Not Contradiction
An agent may not know whether access is available even when the real
system has a precise state. An event may be missing, a source unreliable
or the policy changed. Not knowing the state and a contradiction between
two statements are different situations. The architecture must represent
them separately.
A probability is a value between zero and one assigned to an outcome
within a model. For mutually exclusive outcomes covering every possibil‐
ity considered, probabilities sum to one. A distribution is the collection of
these values. It describes the agent's uncertainty or a mechanism's regu‐
larity, depending on the model used.
The mean of a random variable is not the outcome that will necessarily
occur. In a finite domain, it is the sum of possible outcomes multiplied by
their probabilities. An unlikely outcome may still occur; a well-calibrated
agent is not one whose individual predictions always come true.
Updating a Belief
Consider a fictional example: before a test, the agent assigns a probability
of 0.2 to a fault being present. A signal occurs in 90% of faulty cases and
10% of cases without a fault. After the signal appears, the updated prob‐
ability is 0.18 divided by 0.26, approximately 0.692.
The calculation compares two ways the signal could have appeared: 0.2 ×
0.9 of cases arise from faults, while 0.8 × 0.1 do not. The new result is a
conditional probability, not a proof. It depends on the correctness of the
initial rates and their applicability to the current situation.
An MRP interpreter can perform this update. Another can challenge the
model supplying the rates. Changing numbers within a model and chan‐
ging the model are different operations; the second may require addition‐
al experience, rather than merely recalculating the first.
15


Loss and Choice
A loss function assigns a cost to a decision in a situation. If incorrectly
granting access causes a loss of 100 and incorrectly denying it a loss of 1,
the rational threshold is not a probability of 0.5. Assuming zero cost for
correct decisions and probability p that access is legitimate, granting ac‐
cess has expected loss 100 × (1 − p), while denying it has loss p.
Granting access is preferable only when p exceeds 100/101, approxim‐
ately 0.9901. This is a calculation about a fictional cost matrix, not an ac‐
cess-policy recommendation. It shows why the same uncertainty can lead
to different decisions under different objectives.
Preferences do not, however, change the distribution of truth. The
threshold may legitimately be changed; the probability must not be falsi‐
fied to justify the desired decision. A transparent system can state: “I con‐
sider the event likely, but the cost of an error requires further checking.”
Evaluating Probabilities
Proper scoring rules are designed so that honest probabilistic prediction
optimizes the expected score. Gneiting and Raftery analyze this class of
methods and the distinction between calibration and the informative
sharpness of predictions [Gneiting2007]. In an MRP experiment, these
methods can prevent evaluation from being reduced to merely choosing
the majority label.
For a binary outcome y equal to zero or one, a common form of Brier loss
is (p − y)². If the true probability is q, expected loss can be rewritten as (p
− q)² + q × (1 − q). The second term does not depend on prediction p. The
first is minimized when p = q. This derivation explains why the score fa‐
vors correct probabilities, not merely correct labels.
The score cannot make data representative by itself. An agent evaluated
only on easy situations can score excellently and perform poorly precisely
in important cases. The test distribution is part of the experiment's spe‐
cification.
Abstention and Information
Abstention is an action with its own cost: delay, human intervention or a
lost opportunity. It must be treated neither as absolute failure nor as a
free solution. A useful technology will report the response rate, error
among supplied answers and the cost of referred cases together.
16


More precise knowledge has value when it can change the decision in a
sufficiently important way. A new observation that changes no relevant
behavior may have scientific value but little operational value in the cur‐
rent episode. In a research program, learning's long-term value must be
accounted for separately from immediate success.
For MRP, probability describes what the agent estimates, loss expresses
what matters for the task, and verification establishes what support ex‐
ists. These are three distinct objects. Keeping them separate enables
metareasoning that saves computation without turning economy into per‐
mission to invent facts.

PART II / Foundations for Distinctions
CHAPTER 05
Representations and
Equivalences
What the Agent May Forget
Every finite representation selects. It preserves some differences and ig‐
nores others. The problem is not eliminating every loss, but identifying
losses incompatible with the task. A summary omitting the cover's color
may be perfectly adequate for analyzing arguments. The same summary
is insufficient for identifying the edition by its appearance.
We fix a family T of tests. A test may be a question with a verifiable an‐
swer or a procedure for interacting with the environment. Initially, we
consider deterministic tests. Two histories are equivalent relative to T if
every test in T produces the same answer for both histories.
h1 is T-equivalent to h2 exactly when Test(h1) = Test(h2) for every Test in
T.
The relation is reflexive, symmetric and transitive because equality of an‐
swers has these properties. It produces a partition of histories. This parti‐
tion specifies which information can be ignored without changing an‐
swers to the declared tests. It does not specify what can be ignored for
every imaginable future question.
The Sufficient Representation
A representation R is sufficient for T if every test can be solved using only
R(h). In a finite domain without computational restrictions, this is equi‐
valent to requiring that R not merge histories with different answers to a
relevant test.
The proof is direct. If two histories receive the same representation, any
function seeing only the representation will produce the same result for
them. It therefore cannot reproduce two different answers. Conversely, if
all answers within each R class are identical, we can assign that answer to
the class. This constructs a function on representations that reproduces
the test.
18


The result concerns information. The constructed function may require a
very large table or be difficult to compute. Information sufficiency is not
the same as efficient access to it. A raw archive may contain everything
needed, while a specialized procedure makes the information usable
within an acceptable time.
The partition induced by T is the coarsest partition sufficient for these
tests. Any other sufficient representation must preserve at least its distinc‐
tions. This formulation does not imply unique syntax: the same classes
can be encoded through numbers, labels, programs or other objects.
A Provable Limit
We return to granting and revoking access. Suppose every history is fully
available before compression, the last operation determines the state, and
the representation preserves only the set of operations. The two orderings
receive the same representation. If they occur with equal probabilities, no
predictor seeing only that representation can exceed an expected accur‐
acy of 50% on this pair.
A deterministic predictor must choose an answer and is wrong on one
case. A randomized predictor saying “yes” with probability p has accur‐
acy 0.5 × p + 0.5 × (1 − p), still 0.5. Randomization does not reconstruct the
lost order.
The claim ceases to apply if the predictor sees another information
source: memory of the episode, a case identifier correlated with the an‐
swer or the raw history. An experiment about information loss must
therefore control every input channel, not just the vector the author calls
the “representation.”
For a finite labeled dataset, the best classification based exclusively on R
chooses the majority label in each R class. The minimum number of er‐
rors is the total number of examples minus the sum of the majority fre‐
quencies across classes. This bound can be calculated exactly, providing a
diagnostic tool before training a more complex predictor.
Refinement as Learning
If a new function D separates the confused cases, we can define Rnew(h) =
(R(h), D(h)). The new representation preserves the old information and
adds the distinction. D may be a computable question: the last operation,
the number of cancellations, the existence of an exception or the author
of a statement.
19


This is what the expression “a dimension is a program” means. The di‐
mension need not be an uninterpreted real number. It may be a discrete
result or a relational structure. The agent learns an operation that
changes what it can observe in its own data.
Refinement may require no new external observations, but it is not com‐
putationally free. Computing D may require rereading a long archive. If
the archive was discarded, D may become impossible to apply retrospect‐
ively. Memory design determines which kinds of later learning remain
possible.
What Compression Does Not Guarantee
A representation sufficient for today's questions may be insufficient for
tomorrow's. We can manage this limit by preserving sources, using mul‐
tiple representations and allowing requests for new observations. We
cannot eliminate it with a universal assertion that “the agent understood
the essence.” Operational essence must be related to a family of uses.
Information theory distinguishes measuring and encoding information
from semantically interpreting messages; this boundary is explicit in
Shannon's work [Shannon1948]. MRP adds a design question: which in‐
formation must be conserved for relevant tests and decisions? A highly
compressed file may remain difficult to use, while a redundant represent‐
ation may enable rapid answers.
The final criterion is therefore not “how small is the memory?” but
“which competence is preserved, at what construction and usage cost, for
which task distribution?” This question will govern both interpreter
learning and evaluation.

CHAPTER 06
Learning States
A Small Protocol
Consider a fictional protocol with three states: inactive, pending and act‐
ive. A request moves the system from inactive to pending. A confirmation
activates only an existing request. A cancellation returns it to inactive. To
avoid ambiguity, a repeated request does not deactivate access that is
already active, and confirming active access leaves it active.
The agent does not see the state name. It sees operations and can ask
whether access is active. Two histories, the empty history and a history
containing a request, produce the same negative answer to the current

Automata and Minimal Memory
A finite automaton has finitely many states and a transition rule for every
admissible input. Its state summarizes what must be retained about the
past for the model's future behavior. An automaton is not a necessary ex‐
planation of the brain, but a mathematical object suited to protocols and
discrete processes.
For regular languages, the Myhill–Nerode principle identifies histories
that no continuation can distinguish with respect to acceptance. In active
automata learning, this perspective leads to constructing states from an‐
21


swers to queries. Dana Angluin's L* algorithm is a fundamental reference,
and generalizations to reactive systems and decision processes show its
relevance beyond simple string recognition [Angluin1987; Tappler2019].
For MRP, the lesson is not that every concept must be reduced to auto‐
mata. It is that some of the agent's internal entities can be constructed
from tests' inability to distinguish histories. A state becomes a hypothesis
about future behavior, rather than a manually assigned semantic label.
Why Closure Matters
If two histories are considered equivalent, applying the same operation
must lead them to states that remain equivalent. Otherwise, the transition
from their shared class is not well defined. For string continuations, this
property is called right congruence.
Equivalence for all relevant continuations has this property: a continu‐
ation distinguishing the successors could be preceded by the common op‐
eration and distinguish the initial histories. But equivalence for an arbit‐
rarily chosen finite list of tests need not have it. In our protocol, the “ac‐
cess now” test merges inactive and pending states, although confirmation
separates them.
A test basis must therefore be evaluated for updating, not merely for the
immediate answer. A representation answering correctly today but incap‐
able of correct updating after the next event is not a sufficient state of the
dynamic system.
A Learning Loop
The agent can maintain a table with representative histories as rows and
test continuations as columns. Identical rows suggest the same state.
When a new continuation produces different answers, the rows are sep‐
arated. When an operation leads to a row type not yet represented, a new
representative must be introduced.
This is a construction mechanism, not merely post-hoc classification. After
every extension, the agent builds a candidate model and confronts it with
the system. A counterexample is a sequence on which the candidate's be‐
havior differs from the target's. The sequence can be reduced to locate the
missing test.
In an ideal setting, an equivalence oracle states whether the candidate
model is correct and supplies a counterexample when it is not. In a real
system, such an oracle is usually replaced by testing. Tappler and col‐
22


leagues explicitly distinguish a perfect-information setting from learning
through sampled traces [Tappler2019]. Failure to find a counterexample
must not be reinterpreted as proof of equivalence.
Resetting and Experimental Access
Comparing two continuations from the same situation may require reset‐
ting the system or reproducing a history. This may be simple in a simulat‐
or. In an organizational workflow, it may be expensive or impossible. The
ability to experiment must not be assumed free.
A learning environment must declare which actions are available, which
observations are obtained, whether resetting is possible and whether exe‐
cution irreversibly changes the world. These conditions determine which
algorithms can be applied and which claims can be validated.
What MRP Gains
A learned automaton can become a reusable interpreter of history. It can
process new events incrementally without continually rereading the en‐
tire past. But this efficiency assumes that the learned state preserves the
necessary differences within the evaluated domain.
The experiment must distinguish inducing a new model from selecting a
supplied one. Only the former tests the construction of distinctions; both
may have engineering value.

Prediction and Intervention
Beyond Visible States
In a probabilistic system, two histories may produce different outcomes
when the same test is repeated without necessarily representing different
states. The difference may arise from randomness. The equivalence cri‐
terion must compare distributions, not a single observation.
We will write Distribution(test, h) for the distribution of test outcomes
after history h. Two histories are predictively equivalent relative to T if
these distributions are equal for every test in T. Exact equality is a theor‐
etical object; experimentally, we estimate distributions from samples and
report uncertainty.
An interactive test includes how actions are chosen. If the first observa‐
tion determines the next action, the test is an experimental policy, not
merely a fixed list. Two systems can be compared only if we use the same
test specification and relevant starting conditions.
Predictive Representations
Predictive state representations, or PSRs, describe state through predic‐
tions about the outcomes of tests executable on the system. Singh, James
and Rudary develop this idea using a system-dynamics matrix whose
rows correspond to histories and columns to tests [Singh2004].
An example makes the intuition clear. Instead of storing a hidden label
such as “latent mode 7,” we can store the probability that a simple check
succeeds, the probability that two successive operations succeed and the
probability that a reset restores functioning. These coordinates are mean‐
ingful because they refer to observations that can be collected.
Not every list of predictions is a sufficient PSR. There must be a rule for
calculating relevant predictions from it and updating it after actions and
observations. Choosing a few intuitive questions does not ensure this
property.
24


Rank Without Mystery
A matrix is a numerical table. Its rank measures how many independent
columns or rows are needed to describe the table's linear information. If
some predictions can be obtained as linear combinations of others, they
need not all be stored as independent dimensions.
This offers possible compression for suitably structured systems. How‐
ever, estimating a low rank from finite data does not prove that the real
system has exactly that rank. Noise may make columns appear independ‐
ent, while insufficient tests can hide differences. A useful predictive rep‐
resentation requires both a structural hypothesis and a validation proced‐
ure.
The coefficients of a linear combination must not automatically be inter‐
preted as probabilities. They may be negative, although the final predic‐
tion must remain a valid probability. Moreover, updating after observing
an event usually involves normalization. Linear compression of predic‐
tions does not mean the entire agent becomes a simple average of models.
Observing Is Not Intervening
Consider two models with binary variables. In the first, a hidden variable
Z determines both X and Y. In the second, Z determines X and X determ‐
ines Y. Suppose Z is zero or one with equal probabilities and every link
copies the value. In observational data from both models, we always see X
= Y.
If we intervene and fix X at zero, the difference emerges. In the first mod‐
el, Y continues to copy Z and is one in half the cases. In the second, Y cop‐
ies X and becomes zero. The same observational distribution can be com‐
patible with different intervention effects.
Causal formalism centers on this separation between conditioning, inter‐
vention and counterfactual questions [Pearl2018]. For MRP, the practical
consequence is that an interpreter trained on correlations does not auto‐
matically gain authority to predict the effects of any action. It needs caus‐
al assumptions or suitable experimental data.
A PSR of a controlled system can predict action tests under its identifica‐
tion and coverage conditions. This does not amount to solving every caus‐
al question about hidden mechanisms or unobserved counterfactual
worlds. Exactly which interventions were available and which prediction
class is supported must be specified.
25


Identifiability and Gaps
A model is identifiable for a question if the available data and assump‐
tions determine the relevant answer. Two models explaining every obser‐
vation may still differ regarding an untested action. In this situation, re‐
fining the internal representation does not itself produce new informa‐
tion about the world.
The agent must choose between experimentation, explicitly assuming a
model or abstaining. Generating an additional explanation is not a fourth
route to acquiring information. This limit prevents MRP from becoming a
rhetoric of omniscience through internal reorganization.
Role in the Architecture
Predictive interpreters can supply verifiable coordinates, detect collisions
and suggest tests that would separate candidate models. A metacontroller
can decide whether the information's benefit justifies the experiment. A
verifier can check whether the final claim exceeds the domain supported
by the data.
MRP can thus connect representation with action without reducing every
form of understanding to next-symbol prediction. The relevant prediction
may concern a protocol, an intervention, a documentary answer or a
rule's consequence. Specifying this target is part of the problem, not a de‐
tail that can remain implicit.

Abstract Interpretations
Sufficient Information for a Property
Abstract interpretation constructs sound approximations of program be‐
havior. Instead of executing every concrete case, it operates in a domain
preserving certain properties and ignoring others [Cousot1977]. For MRP,
this provides a mature example of multiple interpreters with clear math‐
ematical obligations.
Suppose two variables x and y both equal the same number a, and a lies
between zero and ten. An interval representation preserves x between
zero and ten and y between zero and ten. Applied independently to both
intervals, subtraction produces the interval from minus ten to ten. Yet the
concrete result x − y is always zero.
The problem is not that the intervals were calculated incorrectly. They
lost the relation between the variables. A relational interpretation pre‐
serving the equality x = y can immediately prove the result. The choice of
abstract domain determines which properties become accessible to ana‐
lysis.
Sound Approximation
In overapproximation analysis, the abstract result must include every
possible concrete result. It may also include possibilities that do not occur
in reality. These produce false alarms, not necessarily analysis errors. An
approximation excluding a possible concrete behavior may become un‐
sound for the property pursued.
We can view an abstract value as a description of a set of concrete states.
“x is positive” describes many values. An abstract transformation is sound
if, starting from all states described by the input, no possible concrete
state after the operation is lost from the output description.
This obligation is much stronger than “the operation was correct on
tested examples.” An empirically learned interpreter does not become a
sound abstract analyzer merely by producing intervals or formulas. The
relationship between the concrete operation and abstract transformation
must be verified.
27


Precision and Cost
More expressive domains can prove properties simpler domains lose, but
may require more computation. Some relations matter in one program re‐
gion and are useless in another. A reasonable strategy starts simply and
increases precision where the required property demands it.
Cousot's introduction to abstract interpretation explains this relationship
between the properties pursued, approximation and computable analysis
[CousotIntro]. The MRP extension would be a controller learning which
representation change is likely to resolve a particular alarm, without as‐
suming that “more precision” is always free or necessary.
For example, the interval may suffice to verify that x does not exceed ten.
To verify the equality x − y = 0, the relation is decisive. The pragmatic
choice depends on the question; each interpretation's soundness depends
on its rules.
Fixed Points and Stopping
Programs with loops may require repeated information propagation. A
fixed point is an analysis state unchanged by another application of the
propagation rule. Not every process reaches a fixed point quickly, and
some domains allow infinite chains of increasingly precise or increasingly
broad approximations.
Abstract interpretation uses acceleration mechanisms, including widen‐
ing, to force certain analyses to stabilize [CousotIntro]. The price may be
lost precision. For MRP, the general idea is that a stopping policy must
form part of the algorithm. “Keep refining until you understand” does not
define a procedure with finite resources.
A prototype can initially avoid complicated domains and use finite tables
or explicit step limits. The restriction is legitimate if the experiment's do‐
main declares it. It becomes problematic only when conclusions are ta‐
citly extended to systems without those limits.
Two Forms of Evidence
A formal result may say: under these assumptions about the program and
arithmetic, the property holds for every modeled execution. An empirical
result may say: on this test distribution, the interpreter had a particular
error rate. Both are useful, but they are not interchangeable.
28


A hybrid architecture may allow a generator to propose an abstraction
and a verifier to check its local soundness. If verification fails, the hypo‐
thesis must not be promoted to a sound rule. If verification is incomplete,
the result's status must preserve that limit.
Transfer to MRP
Abstract interpretation shows that “multiple modes of interpretation” is
not a new idea in itself. It also shows how this plurality can be disciplined
through formal relations. MRP merits investigation where it adds a meas‐
urable mechanism for selecting, constructing and reusing these modes.
The first plausible application is not an agent inventing its own mathem‐
atics without control. It is an agent identifying which relation an analysis
lacks, proposing a suitable interpreter and proving or testing exactly
which improvement it achieved. This object is more modest, but difficult
and useful enough for a real research program.

Collision as a Witness of Insufficiency
A relevant collision occurs when the same representation corresponds to in‐
compatible answers under the same test and conditions. Identifying it re‐
quires checking precisely those conditions. It is not a collision if one ex‐
ample uses a different rule or the questions differ.
In a deterministic microworld, two histories can supply an exact witness. In
a stochastic environment, two different results are insufficient: the same
distribution can produce both. There, a difference between distributions
must be proved or estimated, with enough repetitions and control of relev‐
ant conditions.
A practical procedure would first seek collisions in the execution log. It
would then try to reproduce the pair in a controlled environment. If repro‐
duction confirms the difference, the agent would generate hypotheses about
lost information. Otherwise, the incident remains open or is reclassified.
Counterexample-Guided Refinement
CEGAR, counterexample-guided abstraction refinement, supplies an import‐
ant discipline: an abstraction is verified and an abstract counterexample is
compared with concrete behavior. If that counterexample is spurious, the
abstraction can be refined to eliminate it [Clarke2000].
MRP would adopt the loop's idea without claiming that every prediction er‐
ror is the same mathematical object. In verification, a false alarm may re‐
quire more precision. In learning, a false prediction may require new data,
a different distribution or a program correction. Identifying the failure type
precedes choosing a refinement.
A proposed refinement must explain why the current representation con‐
fuses the cases. “Add another component” is insufficient explanation. “Pre‐
serve the last operation for each person–resource pair because operation or‐
der changes the answer” is an operational hypothesis.
31


Minimal and General Distinctions
The program “recognize exactly the history that failed” separates any mem‐
orized case. Usually, however, it is not the distinction sought. A program
tracking the last relevant operation can separate an entire family of cases.
The difference between the two appears on new examples, not the initial in‐
cident.
Proposing a distinction must therefore be followed by generating contrast‐
ive cases. Change the person's identity, add irrelevant events, repeat an op‐
eration or interleave other resources. The program should preserve what
matters and ignore variations irrelevant to the rule studied.
These transformations are not arbitrary. If repeating an operation changes
the protocol's meaning, it cannot be treated as an invariance. The family of
accepted transformations forms part of the test specification. Even con‐
structing tests can introduce mistaken assumptions.
Information That Cannot Be Recovered
Suppose the history has been discarded and only an unordered set remains.
If two valid histories produce the same set, no program reading only that set
can reliably reconstruct which history occurred. We can estimate a probab‐
ility from frequencies, but this does not recover the actual event.
MRP must be able to respond: “I identified the necessary distinction, but no
longer have the information needed to compute it.” This state is a useful res‐
ult. It can prompt a change in memory policy or a request for a new event
without fabricating a retrospective solution.
In an implementable system, diagnosis would produce one of three main
outputs: a repair computable from existing information, an experiment that
can supply missing information or an explicit limit. Promoting every incid‐
ent into the first category would turn the architecture into a generator of
unverifiable explanations.
What Can Be Measured
Diagnostic quality has its own test set. In microworlds, we can separately in‐
ject implementation errors, collisions, missing observations and rule
changes. We measure how often the agent identifies the appropriate mech‐
anism and how much the investigation costs.
This test differs from final accuracy. A system can sometimes reach the cor‐
rect answer through the wrong repair, which will fail later. For MRP, what
matters is whether the improvement addresses the actual cause of difficulty,
not merely whether a one-off score rises.

Interpreter Induction
What Is Learned and What Is Supplied
An initial MRP system need not invent machine language, logic or every
basic operation. It can receive a function core and learn new composi‐
tions. Learning's contribution must be described precisely: which primit‐
ives existed, which structures could be expressed and which programs
were discovered.
For event histories, primitives may include filtering by a field, comparing
two values, selecting an extremum, counting, joining by a key and apply‐
ing a transition rule. A type limits admissible inputs: selecting the last re‐
cord takes an orderable sequence, not a probability or an isolated propos‐
ition.
Types reduce search and prevent some errors, but do not establish intent.
A program can be well typed and select the first record instead of the last.
Structural correctness is a necessary condition for certain operations, not
a certificate for the entire solution.
Searching a Finitely Described Language
Induction can begin by enumerating short compositions. If there are
roughly b choices at each position and length d is allowed, the number of
candidate sequences may grow on the order of b to the power d. Types,
symmetries and examples eliminate some combinations without remov‐
ing the combinatorial difficulty.
A guided generator can prioritize programs that seem appropriate to the
incident. For example, the difference between two histories with identical
elements in different orders suggests order-sensitive operations. This
heuristic does not guarantee success; it offers a verifiable way to reduce
computation.
An LLM can participate as a generator of such hypotheses. The potential
advantage is rapidly proposing compositions that would be expensive to
enumerate. The disadvantage is that it may supply additional information
from training, produce fragile programs and dominate the entire system's
contribution. Evaluation must include a variant using the same LLM, tools
and budget but lacking MRP's consolidation mechanism.
33


A Selection Criterion Beyond Fitting Examples
A selection function can combine error, description complexity and exe‐
cution cost. No weights are universally correct. They express the trade-off
chosen for the application and must be published with the results.
Score(candidate) = estimated error + description penalty + estimated cost.
The minimum description length principle, MDL, provides a foundation
for preferring an explanation that compresses both the model and the
data left unexplained [Grunwald2004]. In MRP, this preference could fa‐
vor a reusable rule over a list of exceptions. However, the result depends
on the language in which description length is measured and on data
quality. A short program is not automatically true or ethically preferable.
Separating description cost from execution cost matters. A very short
definition may hide enormous search. Conversely, a longer program may
precompile operations and run quickly. The library must be evaluated in
terms of total cost over relevant tasks.
From a Local Program to a Reusable Concept
Suppose the agent discovers the same pattern across several protocols:
group by identity, select the last event and interpret its type. Consolida‐
tion extracts the common part and parameterizes it with the protocol's
specific fields and rules.
This pattern is a candidate for an operational notion of a concept: a com‐
position organizing many cases that can be executed in new contexts. We
do not claim that every human concept is such a program. We define a
kind of knowledge that can be built and measured.
DreamCoder shows that learning libraries of programmatic abstractions
and guiding search can be combined in a concrete system [Ellis2020]. For
MRP, the distinct question would be whether diagnosing a collision can
guide this induction and whether the new abstraction explicitly improves
the family of distinctions available to the agent.
Program Equivalence and Rewriting
The library may contain different programs computing the same result.
Eliminating redundancy would save memory and simplify selection. But
establishing equivalence for general programs is neither free nor univer‐
sally decidable [Barak].
34


We can work with identities proved in a restricted language. Equality sat‐
uration, including structures such as those used by egg, enables compact
representation of many equivalent expressions and selection by cost
[Willsey2021]. This does not authorize declaring arbitrary LLM-generated
programs equivalent.
If operations have effects, arithmetic overflows or order matters, rewrite
rules must include these conditions. An algebraic identity between ideal
expressions may not hold for concrete execution. In a prototype, a pure
and explicitly restricted core would greatly simplify verification.
Independent Validation and Contamination
The case that generated the hypothesis cannot be the main evidence that
it generalizes. Nor does a test set consulted repeatedly until the candidate
passes remain an independent evaluation. Research on adaptive data
analysis shows why reusing evaluation results can compromise generaliz‐
ation [Dwork2015].
I would separate induction examples, development tests and a final set in‐
accessible to selection. An evaluator could return only the verdict needed
for the current stage while retaining a reserve for final evaluation. All rel‐
evant attempts should also be reported, not just the last candidate.
Successful induction does not mean “a program was generated.” It means
a program constructed from declared primitives repaired an identified in‐
sufficiency, passed appropriate evaluations and justified the cost of con‐
solidation. This is the complete experimental object.

The Rationality of Computation
Computing Is Also an Action
An agent chooses not only between actions in the world but also between
internal operations. It may consult a table, execute a more precise inter‐
preter, request a new observation or construct a hypothesis. Every option
consumes time and may change the final decision.
The value of computation is the expected gain from an internal operation
after subtracting its cost. For a single operation followed by a decision, we
can write, without assuming a perfect solution:
ValueOfComputation = expected decision gain after computation − de‐
cision gain now − computation cost.
This formula is not a new MRP law. Metareasoning and learning policies
for selecting computations already have experimental formulations, in‐
cluding the work of Callaway and colleagues [Callaway2018]. MRP would
expand the repertoire of selectable operations to include constructing and
consolidating interpreters.
A Computation Worth Performing
Assume a hidden state with two equally probable values. A correct an‐
swer is worth one unit and an incorrect answer zero. Without informa‐
tion, the best probability of success is 0.5. A perfect test costs 0.1 units on
the same utility scale.
After the test, success is certain and net value is 0.9. The gain over an‐
swering immediately is 0.4. The test is worthwhile in the given model. If it
cost 0.6, executing it would reduce total utility. Obtaining the same in‐
formation may be rational in one application and irrational in another.
In practice, tests are imperfect and their value is unknown. The agent
must estimate the probability of outcomes and their effects on the de‐
cision. Estimating value is itself a computation and must therefore have a
budget.
36


Why Myopic Selection Can Fail
Consider two hidden, independent and equally probable bits. The task is
to answer whether they differ. Without observations, maximum accuracy
is 0.5. Learning one bit does not improve this accuracy: the other remains
unknown. Learning both raises it to one.
If each observation costs 0.1, a policy evaluating only individual opera‐
tions will reject both: each costs something without immediately improv‐
ing the decision. A policy evaluating the pair sees a net gain of 0.3. This is
a calculated example, not a simulation result.
Interpreter composition can produce precisely this complementarity. One
operation identifies entities and another relations; separately, neither an‐
swers the question. An MRP controller should therefore be tested with dif‐
ferent planning horizons. A local rule must not be assumed to resolve
every dependency.
Avoiding Meta-Regress
If every choice requires another choice about how to choose, the architec‐
ture never stops. The engineering solution is not an infinitely wise inter‐
preter but a bounded controller: a maximum number of evaluations, an
approximate policy and a stopping rule.
Initially, selection may combine precondition checking with an empirical
estimate of success on task families. Later, the policy can be learned. Even
then, it must be compared with a simple rule. Controller complexity is jus‐
tified only if it produces a measurable advantage.
Recent research also applies the rationality of metacomputation to lan‐
guage models [DeSabbata2025]. Merely selecting between a quick answer
and an expensive one is therefore not MRP's distinctive contribution. The
candidate contribution is the connection between selection, representa‐
tion diagnosis and the accumulation of new procedures.
The Reuse Horizon
Building an interpreter may be uneconomical for the current task but
economical for many future tasks. This introduces an amortization hori‐
zon. Estimated value depends on how often the problem will recur, rule
stability and verification cost.
37


A hypothetical example: induction and verification together cost 1,000
milliseconds. The old solution costs 40 milliseconds per task. The new one
costs eight, plus two for selection. Savings are 30 milliseconds per use. The
investment pays off after 34 uses if all other conditions remain identical.
It is insufficient to report that the new interpreter executes five times
faster. Construction cost, reuse rate and cases where rule changes invalid‐
ate it must be included. A library's advantage concerns its lifetime, not
merely its most recent call.
Normative Limits of Optimization
A computation policy can maximize the utility supplied, but does not de‐
cide by itself which objectives are legitimate. The cost of an error to a per‐
son cannot be tacitly introduced as a number chosen by the developer.
Some applications require constraints that cannot be changed merely to
improve an average score.
MRP should distinguish the technical objective, approved limits and pref‐
erences still needing clarification. In a context with important con‐
sequences, abstention and human intervention may be valid actions. A
meta-rational architecture does not become responsible merely by rigor‐
ously optimizing its own criterion.

Memory and Transfer
Three Memories with Different Roles
The proposed architecture would separate episodic memory, representa‐
tion memory and the interpreter library. Episodes preserve what was ob‐
served. Representations preserve the results of transformations. The lib‐
rary preserves procedures that can reconstruct, query or modify those
results.
This separation does not require three physical databases. It requires be‐
ing able to answer three questions: “which information was available?”,
“how was it compressed?” and “which operation produced this conclu‐
sion?” Without these answers, a representation error can easily be con‐
fused with an observation error.
Complete raw memory is often expensive or inappropriate. Yet irrevers‐
ible compression limits reinterpretation. MRP should treat retention
policy as an explicit choice, with classes of recoverable and unrecoverable
information, rather than an infrastructure detail.
Provenance Without Excessive Promises
A result should be linked to its input version, interpreter, parameters and
the activity that produced it. W3C's PROV model supplies a vocabulary for
entities, activities, agents and derivations [W3C2013]. It can inform log in‐
teroperability without requiring every internal component to use the
same representation.
Provenance does not guarantee truth. A false claim may have a perfectly
documented history. What we gain is the ability to inspect dependencies,
attribute transformations and determine which results must be recon‐
sidered after a change.
Two documents repeating the same source do not automatically consti‐
tute two independent pieces of evidence. A provenance library can reveal
their common dependency. Confidence aggregation must account for it,
rather than simply count references.
39


Transfer as a Test of Abstraction
There are at least three levels of reuse. Rerunning the same program on
different names is a modest level. Reusing a composition on a new struc‐
ture with different rule combinations is stronger. Transfer to a domain
with different observations and validity conditions is harder still.
An interpreter selecting the last operation may work for both a permis‐
sion and an updatable preference. It does not follow that it works for a
bank balance, where all relevant operations may contribute cumulatively.
Superficial similarity between logs does not justify transferring the rule.
An application contract should specify this difference: is the property de‐
termined by the last operation or by aggregating operations? This is itself
a possible meta-level of learning. The agent can learn which interpreter
family suits a class of processes.
Consolidation, Forgetting and Migration
Adding interpreters increases capability but also search cost. Consolida‐
tion can merge redundant definitions, introduce parameters and retire in‐
ferior variants. Retirement must not erase the history of results depend‐
ing on them. An old version may remain necessary to reproduce an ana‐
lysis.
A retention criterion can combine recent use, estimated value, unique‐
ness of proven properties and reconstruction cost. This criterion must be
tested for catastrophic forgetting: losing a rare but important capability is
not automatically justified by its low frequency.
Migrating a representation requires an explicit transformation. If the new
interpreter needs event order and the old memory does not preserve it,
migration cannot be complete. The system must mark reconstructed, es‐
timated and unknown segments.
Long-Term Learning
An adaptive library's performance must be tracked over time. A learning
curve can measure average cost per task as the library grows. A retention
curve measures whether old tasks remain solvable. A transfer curve
measures advantages on new families.
40


These curves may have different shapes. The agent may become faster on
familiar tasks but weaker on new ones because the selector favors
routine excessively. Or it may generalize better while consuming unsus‐
tainable memory. MRP must be evaluated as a dynamic system, not
through a single number after training.
On this view, accumulated knowledge would be more than a repository of
propositions. It would be a collection of procedures, results, conditions
and evidence enabling the agent to reconstruct a distinction when it be‐
comes relevant. The usefulness of this organization remains an empirical
hypothesis, but its components are precise enough to implement.

Pragmatic Distances
Closeness Relative to a Question
Two descriptions can use very similar words yet require opposite actions.
“Permitted until Friday” and “prohibited until Friday” are lexically close
but different for an access decision. Conversely, very different formula‐
tions can describe the same rule. A distance between texts is not automat‐
ically a distance between their consequences.
For MRP, the starting question is: how different are the results of relevant
tests for two histories? This establishes both the distance's purpose and its
limits. We are not defining a universal similarity of things, but similarity
relative to a declared family of experiments.
Total Variation
For two discrete distributions P and Q over the same outcomes, total vari‐
ation distance is half the sum of absolute differences between their prob‐
abilities. It is also the greatest difference between probabilities assigned
to the same event.
TV(P, Q) = 0.5 × the sum, over outcomes, of |P(outcome) − Q(outcome)|.
If P and Q are distributions of a binary answer, the distance is the abso‐
lute difference between the probabilities of answering “yes.” Predictions
of 0.2 and 0.7 are at distance 0.5. If they are identical for every outcome,
the distance is zero.
Now fix a test family T. For each test, the two histories induce two distri‐
butions over the outcome. The proposed pragmatic distance is the
greatest difference between these distributions across the family's tests.
dT(h1, h2) = the supremum, for t in T, of TV(Pt(. | h1), Pt(. | h2)).
The supremum is the least upper bound. For a finite test family, it is
simply the maximum. If a test includes actions, the distributions concern
executing that test within the declared model, not observational condi‐
tionings tacitly interpreted as interventions.
42


Why It Is a Pseudometric
The distance is nonnegative and symmetric. It is zero between a history
and itself. The triangle inequality follows from the corresponding in‐
equality for each test, followed by taking the supremum. The mathematic‐
al appendix provides a step-by-step proof.
However, distinct histories can have zero distance: family T may not dis‐
tinguish them. We therefore call the function a pseudometric. Grouping
all histories at zero distance yields a metric on the resulting classes. This
construction connects distance with the equivalences in chapter 5.
Behavioral metrics and bisimulation-based distances have important pre‐
cedents in the study of decision processes [Ferns2004]. The distance for‐
mulated here must not be confused with all those metrics: the choice of
tests, rewards and horizon changes the mathematical object.
Approximation Does Not Automatically Produce
Classes
In practice, we often require not exactly zero distance but a difference be‐
low a threshold. A trap appears: the relation “distance is at most the
threshold” is not generally transitive.
For three binary distributions with probabilities 0.00, 0.06 and 0.12, the
first two are close at a threshold of 0.08, as are the last two. The first and
last are not. If an algorithm merges every close pair through chains, it can
produce a class whose members differ far more than the original
threshold.
Approximate clustering therefore requires an additional criterion: max‐
imum class diameter, a center with a controlled radius or an explicit
bound on aggregation error. The statement “we represent all similar
states through the same concept” is incomplete without this criterion.
When Distance Controls the Decision
Suppose a test's outcome determines a utility between zero and one. The
difference between mean utilities under P and Q is at most TV(P, Q). A
small predictive distance can thus bound error in a decision evaluation,
provided utility is actually calculated from the modeled outcomes.
43


A second simple result is useful. If every action's estimated value is within
e of its true value and we choose the action with the highest estimate, the
loss relative to the genuinely optimal action is at most 2e. One e term
comes from underestimating the optimal action and the other from over‐
estimating the chosen one.
These bounds do not automatically transfer from a single step to every
long plan. For sequential actions, the relevant trajectory distributions or
error propagation must be controlled. Good next-event prediction does
not by itself guarantee a good long-term policy.
Estimating Distance Has a Cost
Exact distance assumes knowledge of the distributions. Finite data yield
estimates and uncertainty. An agent declaring two histories identical be‐
cause it has not yet found differences may confuse absence of evidence
with proof of equivalence.
For the first experiment, I would use microworlds where the evaluator
knows the true distributions while the agent receives only samples. This
allows us to measure how well the agent estimates closeness and when it
declares a representation sufficient prematurely.
Distance serves to turn a metaphor about the “neighborhood of mean‐
ings” into a verifiable claim: these situations have similar consequences
for these tests, with this estimated error. Only afterward is it worth seek‐
ing an efficient geometric representation of these relations.

CHAPTER 14
Cones and Coordinates
What We Call Geometry
A geometric representation assigns coordinates to objects and uses rela‐
tionships between coordinates to express relevant structure. The choice
of distance, dimensions and admissible transformations forms part of the
model. A collection of texts, experiences or programs has no single impli‐
cit geometry.
Gärdenfors's conceptual spaces theory proposes describing concepts
through quality dimensions and geometric regions [Gardenfors2000]. It
motivates questions about the shape of concepts, but does not prove that
all knowledge must be convex in a fixed space. For MRP, the structure to
preserve must be specified: similarity, order, behavior or decision prefer‐
ence.
Convexity and Cones
A set is convex if the segment between any two of its points remains in‐
side it. A disk and a cube are familiar examples. A ring is not convex: the
segment between opposite points can cross the central hole.
A cone, in the sense used here, is closed under multiplying its coordinates
by a nonnegative number. If it is also convex, nonnegative combinations
of its points remain in the cone. A cone need not be imagined only as the
circular object from school geometry; it can be defined by linear inequal‐
ities in many dimensions [Boyd2004].
High dimensionality does not imply nonconvexity. Convex sets exist in
every finite dimension. A cone can also be convex. Opposing “cones” to
“convex shapes” would mix two different classifications.
A Decision-Theoretic Derivation
Assume finitely many possible world states. The agent has a distribution p
over them. Each option i is a fully specified procedure: executing an inter‐
preter, followed by a fixed rule for choosing an action from the result. For
each state, the procedure has net utility u_i, including the modeled costs.
45


The word “fixed” is essential. If the internal procedure changes arbitrarily
with p, its utility is generally no longer represented by a single constant
vector. We can include multiple candidate procedures, but cannot retain
the linear derivation while ignoring that change.
The mean utility of procedure i is the dot product of p and u_i: the sum of
each state's probability multiplied by utility in that state. Procedure i is
preferred to procedure j when this product is at least as large.
Region(i) = {p: every coordinate is nonnegative, their sum is 1, and p · (u_i
− u_j) ≥ 0 for every j}.
Each comparison defines a half-space. Their intersection with the probab‐
ility simplex is a convex polyhedral region. The simplex is the set of all
distributions over the listed states: a segment for two states, a triangle for
three and a higher-dimensional analogue in general.
We now move to unnormalized weights z, all nonnegative. The prefer‐
ence conditions become homogeneous: multiplying every weight by the
same positive number does not change the preferred procedure.
Cone(i) = {z: z ≥ 0 and z · (u_i − u_j) ≥ 0 for every j}.
This set is a convex polyhedral cone. The original region is obtained by in‐
tersecting it with the plane where the weights sum to one. The origin is
mathematically included in the cone but does not represent a normaliz‐
able distribution.
A Calculated Example
We have two possible states and three procedures. The first has utilities
(1; 0), the second (0; 1), the third (0.6; 0.6). These hypothetical values in‐
clude every assumed cost. Let p denote the probability of the first state.
The first procedure has value p, the second 1 − p and the third 0.6. The
first is optimal for p above 0.6; the second for p below 0.4; the third for p
between 0.4 and 0.6. Options tie at the boundaries.
In unnormalized coordinates, the third procedure is preferred when each
weight is at least two-thirds of the other. We obtain a sector in the first
quadrant. The cone describes a family of informational states in which se‐
lecting the same procedure is rational.
This gives the geometric intuition a concrete meaning: some “regions of
understanding” may be regions of decision applicability. It does not follow
that every concept, proposition or memory is a cone.
46


Entailment Cones and Other Meanings
Ganea and colleagues use entailment cones, including in hyperbolic geo‐
metry, to represent hierarchical relations [Ganea2018]. There, the con‐
struction is motivated by preserving a partial order. In our derivation, it is
motivated by comparing expected utilities.
These two types of cones may be useful in an MRP system, but they are
not interchangeable. A subordinate concept in a taxonomy is not the same
as an optimal procedure in a region of beliefs. Using the same drawing
does not establish identical mechanisms.
What Happens to Rings and “Holes”
Consider the region between two circles: points satisfying 1 < x² + y² < 4. It
is nonconvex. An interpreter computing r = x² + y² transforms the ques‐
tion “does the point belong to the region?” into checking the interval 1 < r
< 4.
The transformation loses the angle, however. Different points are com‐
pressed into the same value. We have not freely eliminated geometric
complexity through an invertible coordinate change; we have discarded
information unnecessary to the radial question. For a question about dir‐
ection, the new representation would be insufficient.
Holes and connectivity have a different status from convexity when only
continuous transformations with continuous inverses are allowed. But
MRP interpreters may be discontinuous and compress information. Intu‐
itions about rings or tori must therefore be linked to an explicit class of
admissible transformations.
The Testable Hypothesis
I would not say “knowledge has the shape of cones.” I would say: for this
task family, a representation using particular geometric regions preserves
relevant distinctions and enables more efficient selection or transfer than
alternatives under the same budget.
The test would compare applicability errors, search cost, memory and
transfer. A beautiful visualization is not evidence. If a simple table
achieves the same results at lower cost, the geometric hypothesis has not
yet produced engineering value.

CHAPTER 15
An Atlas of Interpreters
Why Not a Single Space
The same situation may require preserving temporal order, participant
identity, a numerical relationship or a claim's provenance. A single space
can encode all of these in principle, but accessing each property may re‐
main expensive or difficult to verify.
The atlas proposal is more modest: multiple local representations, each
with well-defined operations and a domain of use. “Atlas” is an organiza‐
tional analogy. It does not automatically imply a differentiable manifold,
smooth coordinates or invertible transformations between every repres‐
entation.
A temporal interpreter may answer questions about the latest state. A re‐
lational interpreter may answer questions about dependencies. A docu‐
mentary interpreter may preserve who supports each claim. They can de‐
scribe the same episode without producing the same kind of result.
Translations with Contracts
A translation between two representations must declare what it pre‐
serves. If we construct a current state from a log, we may preserve the an‐
swer to “what is active now?” but not necessarily “how often did it
change?” A useful translation need not be fully reversible; it must be suffi‐
cient for its stated purpose.
In an atlas, links would be executable adapters. Each adapter has precon‐
ditions, an output type and a claim about information preservation. Some
preservation claims may be proved. Others can only be evaluated through
tests. Their status must be retained in metadata and the explanation of
the result.
A round-trip test, translating A into B and back again, is useful for detect‐
ing losses. Success on a few examples does not prove equivalence, how‐
ever. Moreover, an intentionally compressive translation should be evalu‐
ated by preservation of relevant tests, not complete reconstruction.
48


Local Compatibility and Contradiction
Two representations may produce apparently contradictory results
without being logically incompatible. One describes the formal rule, the
other the observed state. A person may have access rights while the con‐
nection is unavailable. The contradiction disappears when targets are
typed correctly.
In other cases, the contradiction is real: two interpreters assert different
values for the same variable, period and assumptions. The system must
not arbitrarily average them. It must check sources, preconditions, ver‐
sions and dependencies.
This work can be represented as a constraint satisfaction problem. If a set
of claims cannot all be true in the declared model, the agent seeks a con‐
flicting subset and marks it. A solver can help for suitable languages
without automatically resolving the natural-language ambiguity that pro‐
duced the formulas.
Operations Define Coordinates
An MRP atlas would store not only values but also the procedures produ‐
cing them. The coordinate “latest valid state” is a program over a history.
“Number of independent sources” is a procedure over a provenance
graph. “Estimated risk” is a model with its own data and assumptions.
This connection allows a new dimension to be checked. We can inspect
the program, modify input data and observe what changes. A learned nu‐
merical dimension without a simple interpretation is not excluded, but
does not automatically receive the same kind of justification.
Here lies one of the program's central ideas: a new dimension of under‐
standing can be a procedure producing a previously inaccessible distinc‐
tion. The resulting geometry depends on the procedure and the questions
for which we use it.
Evaluating the Atlas
For every task, we can measure how many representations were consul‐
ted, how many translations were executed, what information was lost and
what reconciliation cost. The atlas must be compared with a sufficiently
powerful single space and a fixed library of views.
49


A favorable result would show local adaptation avoiding reconstruction
of the entire representation and transferring procedures between tasks.
An unfavorable result would show adapters, selection and checks costing
more than a well-designed common model. Neither conclusion should be
presumed from the chosen metaphor.
The atlas is not an architectural obligation. It is an organizational hypo‐
thesis that can be implemented gradually. An initial system may have
only three views and two adapters; what matters is demonstrating what
each preserves and when changing views resolves a real difficulty.

An Interpreter's Contract
The contract must specify what the interpreter receives and can produce.
For a last-operation interpreter, the input may be a sequence of events with
identity and total order. The precondition may require every event to have a
comparable position. The output is a state value for each key.
The contract would also declare limits: it ignores events absent from the in‐
put; assumes the “last operation prevails” rule; and does not establish that
the log is complete. A correct result about the available log is thus not
presented as evidence about every real event.
For a probabilistic interpreter, the contract includes the distribution's target
and evaluation conditions. For a solver, it includes the theory and model
checked. For an LLM, it may declare the output a textual proposal or candid‐
ate program, rather than a certified result.
The Controller and Executors
The controller decides which operation comes next. Executors may be sym‐
bolic interpreters, programs, statistical models, solvers, database queries or
people asked to clarify a specification. A uniform interface does not imply
uniform trust in the results.

An initial controller can use transparent rules: check preconditions, choose
the cheapest compatible candidate, execute, evaluate and escalate if an in‐
cident occurs. This version provides a baseline for a later learned controller.
Without a baseline, any additional complexity can seem useful because it
has nothing to be compared with.
Executors would separately report time, memory consumption, external op‐
erations and final state. A timeout is an execution outcome, not proof that
the property cannot be established. A parsing error differs from a semantic
counterexample.
The Epistemic Register
I would separate a result's content from its status. “Permission is active” is
content. “Computed exactly from the complete log according to model M” is
one status. “Estimated from incomplete observations” is another. The two
must not be lost as the result passes through other components.
A register could distinguish reported observation, hypothesis, derived res‐
ult, formal verification relative to a model, empirical validation and with‐
drawn result. These categories do not necessarily form a single hierarchy. A
formal proof about a mistaken model and a robust observation about the
real system have different limitations.
Composition should preserve relevant dependencies. A final text based on a
hypothesis does not become fact merely because the last stage is a fluent
generator. A verifier can confirm a document's form without confirming
every claim's truth.
The Minimal Useful Implementation
The first implementation needs no complete ecosystem. It needs a family of
finite tasks, an operation core, a few initial representations, an execution
log, a candidate generator and a separate evaluator. This minimum already
allows collision, refinement and transfer to be tested.
Extensions should be introduced only when they address an identified limit.
If selection becomes expensive, develop library indexing. If evaluation con‐
sumes too much, study test-selection strategies. If transfer does not emerge,
investigate induction or task definitions before adding more components.
A defensible MRP architecture is one whose advantages can be attributed to
its mechanisms. Suggestive names for every component are insufficient;
components must be removable one at a time to measure their contribu‐
tions.

A Complete Walkthrough
The task is to determine current permission from a log. The initial repres‐
entation stores only which event types occurred. The agent encounters
two histories with identical types but different outcomes. The evaluator
confirms that order, rather than noise, explains the difference.
54


The generator proposes an interpreter that groups events by person and
resource, selects the final position and applies the state rule. Structural
checking confirms that the types match. Tests check reversed order, inter‐
leaved keys and repeated operations according to the environment's rule.
An additional verifier may prove, for the finite event language and given
implementation, that the result corresponds to the last relevant operation.
This proof does not confirm that the real log is complete; it confirms the
relationship between input and output within the model.
After acceptance, the program enters the library. On a new task, the se‐
lector must recognize the appropriate rule. If the new protocol is cumulat‐
ive, the contract does not apply. Correct transfer includes refusing to use
an unsuitable abstraction.
Epochs and Controlled Changes
Learning changes data interpretation. If an interpreter is replaced during
execution, the result may mix versions without clear justification. One
solution is to use epochs: each execution sees a stable version of the lib‐
rary and policy.
A modification produces a new epoch. Old results remain associated with
the configuration in which they were obtained. The new interpreter can
be tested on copies of tasks before becoming the default. This facilitates
reproduction and comparison without requiring the entire system to stop
whenever learning occurs.
The change can be transactional: either the interpreter, representation
schema and affected dependencies are updated consistently, or the old
version remains active. Transaction is used here as a consistency discip‐
line, not a promise that every external effect can be undone.
External Effects and Reversibility
An interpreter reading data differs from one modifying a database or
sending a command. Proposed actions must be separated from actual exe‐
cutions. Repeating a check must not inadvertently repeat an irreversible
effect.
For the research prototype, operations can be limited to a simulated en‐
vironment or copies without external effects. When moving to real applic‐
ations, permissions, operation idempotence, approvals and effect records
must be defined. An idempotent operation has the same effect on state
55


when repeated as when executed once. These requirements are not a
philosophical addition; they determine which experiments are reprodu‐
cible.
A sandbox limits the consequences of faulty programs but does not estab‐
lish that their results are correct. Conversely, a proof about a pure func‐
tion does not authorize every use of it in a system with effects. These
problems must be addressed separately.
Selective Recomputation
If a source is corrected or an interpreter withdrawn, not everything needs
recomputing. We can trace the dependency graph and mark affected res‐
ults. This optimization assumes, however, that dependencies are recorded
sufficiently completely.
A result that indirectly reads a global configuration without declaring it
creates a hidden dependency. Reusing it from memory may be wrong
after the configuration changes. An execution's identity therefore includes
not only its visible input but also relevant context parameters and ver‐
sions.
The recomputation policy can begin with an overapproximation: invalid‐
ate every possibly affected result. It can later be refined to avoid unneces‐
sary work. The same logic of precision and cost reappears at infrastruc‐
ture level.
What an Execution Trace Explains
A good trace shows which operations ran, with which inputs and checks.
It can explain the process producing the result. It does not automatically
supply a causal explanation of the worldly phenomenon or a value-based
justification of the chosen objective.
MRP should deliver these levels separately. A scientific result requires
both reproducible computation and adequate models and data. An organ‐
izational decision also requires authority and legitimate criteria. Technic‐
al transparency is valuable precisely when it does not present itself as the
solution to every one of these questions.

MRP-VM and SOP Lang
The Theory Does Not Depend on a Syntax
MRP describes the organization of interpretations and their learning. SOP
Lang may be an option for representing executable compositions, but the
theory must not depend on a particular file extension or naming conven‐
tion. Other languages can implement the same experiment.
This separation enables two evaluations. We can test whether the MRP
mechanism produces transfer and efficiency. Separately, we can test
whether SOP circuit representations facilitate composition, provenance
tracking and verification. A syntax or parser failure does not automatic‐
ally invalidate the general hypothesis.
The Circuit as a Unit of Composition
In one possible implementation, a circuit is a graph of operations. Nodes
execute transformations; links carry values and dependencies. Inputs and
outputs are declared. An operation may call a primitive or another circuit,
and composition produces a more complex procedure.
To simplify analysis, intermediate results can have unique identities with‐
in an execution. Instead of tacitly modifying a value, we produce a new
one and record the dependency. This single-assignment discipline helps
track provenance without excluding controlled persistent state.
An acyclic graph can be executed in dependency order. Recursive or iter‐
ative processes require an additional mechanism: an explicit loop, a fixed
point or the opening of a new stage. Merely drawing a graph resolves
neither termination nor effect management.
A Small Core, an Extensible Library
The core would implement operations needing direct support: data ac‐
cess, type checking, certain computations and communication with ex‐
ecutors. Most higher-level compositions could be declarative circuits. A
new interpreter would thus become an inspectable library object, rather
than an opaque platform modification.
57


The distinction between primitive and circuit must be visible in system
documentation. Otherwise, the agent may be claimed to have learned a
capability actually hidden inside a complex manually supplied operation.
An evaluator must see exactly what was compiled, learned and called.
File organization can define command namespaces, but this convention is
not a cognitive contribution. It becomes useful if it simplifies component
discovery, versioning and reuse. MRP evaluation must remain centered
on capabilities, not organizational aesthetics.
Controlled Language and Intermediate
Representation
A controlled-language interface can make circuits easier to read. For a rig‐
orously defined fragment, we can require textual and intermediate rep‐
resentations to be interconvertible without semantic loss within that frag‐
ment. This is a verifiable design requirement.
We cannot extend the same promise to every natural-language sentence.
Ambiguity, presuppositions and context may permit several interpreta‐
tions. The translator must preserve alternatives or request clarification,
rather than arbitrarily declare that it extracted the “exact meaning.”
A verification loop can compare a circuit's effects with positive and negat‐
ive examples supplied by the user. These examples do not resolve every
ambiguity, but they make the translation open to challenge. Stabilized
meaning becomes the accepted contract for that operation.
The Role of Language Models
An LLM can translate a request into a circuit proposal, generate tests and
suggest a new distinction. It can also serve as a probabilistic executor
when no suitable symbolic procedure exists. The architecture must allow
these roles without confusing proposal with verification.
If the same model generates the program and every test, its errors may be
correlated. Independent evaluators, specification-generated tests or hu‐
man interventions are needed for important properties. Independence
does not necessarily mean another model brand; it means a source of
constraints that does not repeat the same unverified assumption.
Initially, a hybrid system can be very useful even if most program ideas
come from an LLM. But the experimental report must say so. MRP's con‐
tribution may be verification, consolidation and reuse, rather than the ini‐
tial generation of every concept.

A Composition Example Without Artificial Syntax
For a documentary synthesis, a circuit can receive a document and a fam‐
ily of questions. One stage identifies candidate passages. Another separ‐
ates the author's claims from quotations and hypotheses. A third con‐
structs answers with references to passages. A verifier checks whether
the summary preserves the required exceptions.
If the verifier finds a sentence in which a hypothesis became fact, the in‐
cident need not require a new text generator. It may require an interpret‐
er preserving the modality of claims and a composition rule prohibiting
its loss. This new operation can be reused in other documents.
The circuit makes the distinction visible; MRP decides when it must be
constructed and whether consolidation is worthwhile. This is the func‐
tional relationship between the two levels. Neither should be presented as
an already validated solution for all of natural language.

Software and Protocol
Verification
Why This Is a Suitable First Domain
Software offers situations in which inputs can be controlled, executions
reproduced and precise properties formulated. Not every software system
is simple to model, but a bounded protocol family can separate represent‐
ational difficulty from natural-language ambiguity. This is therefore the
first application I would prioritize.
The commercial or engineering problem would be reducing repeated ana‐
lysis costs without losing required properties. An experimental MRP sys‐
tem would begin with simple views of the program or log and activate
more precise representations only where demonstrable insufficiencies
arise.
The system need not immediately compete with every existing verifica‐
tion tool. An initial contribution may be better selection among known
analyses or learning preprocessing steps that make a property accessible
to an existing analyzer.
Use Case: The Reservation Protocol
Consider a reservation service with requests, confirmations and cancella‐
tions. The agent receives an executable specification of the protocol ver‐
sion and labeled logs. The question is whether the resource is reserved at
a given moment and which continuations can change its state.
A coarse interpreter preserves only the existence of a confirmation. It
fails after cancellation. A second preserves the last operation but may fail
if the protocol distinguishes canceling a request from canceling a confirm‐
ation. The necessary distinction depends on the exact rules, not operation
names.
60


The agent can learn a state model with preconditions. Confirmation is val‐
id only after an active request, while certain operations are ignored or
produce an error. Contrastive tests must include invalid operations, differ‐
ent identities and histories longer than those used during induction.
For a finite protocol, the evaluator can explore every reachable model
state. If inputs and states are unbounded, exhaustive proof must be re‐
placed or supplemented by inductive arguments and tests. The size of the
verified domain must be reported, rather than hidden behind “fully
tested.”
Use Case: Choosing the Abstract Domain
In numerical program analysis, the agent begins with intervals. A prop‐
erty cannot be proved because the analysis loses equality between two
variables. Diagnosis identifies the necessary relation and activates a rela‐
tional interpreter only for the relevant portion.
There are two learning levels here. The first learns to choose among do‐
mains already implemented. The second proposes new predicates or
transformations, verified before use. The first is simpler and provides an
important baseline for the second.
If a generator proposes an invariant, the verifier must check initializa‐
tion, preservation through transitions and whether the invariant implies
the required property. Being true on observed traces is insufficient. On a
finite trace set, many generally false claims may appear invariant.
Abstract interpretation and CEGAR supply the technical precedents for
this discipline [Cousot1977; Clarke2000]. MRP's contribution should be
sought in identifying and reusing distinctions, not renaming these meth‐
ods.
Use Case: Migrating an API
A new service version may change a validity condition or precedence
rule. In a controlled experiment, the agent receives examples from both
versions and must distinguish a rule change from an accidental fault.
The old library may remain useful for unchanged parts. The agent should
create a new contract or version, rather than overwrite the past. Earlier
results must remain reproducible relative to their version.
61


Value would come from reducing reanalysis work: reusing compatible in‐
terpreters, reconstructing only affected distinctions and generating tests
that separate the versions. One success criterion is identifying the actual
change without excessively increasing false alarms.
What We Measure
The main measures would be correctly proved properties, missed errors,
false alarms, total time, memory and the cost of constructing new inter‐
preters. Time to the first valid counterexample may matter, but must not
be used alone: a system may find one example quickly and miss other er‐
ror classes.
Comparisons would include a single more precise analysis, a fixed com‐
bination of analyzers and a selector without induction. To isolate reuse,
evaluation tasks must contain new structures, not copies of the same pro‐
grams with renamed variables.
An informative negative result would show selection from a fixed library
producing the entire advantage, with induction adding nothing after ac‐
counting for its cost. This result would favor a simpler technology. MRP
would remain a useful analytical framework, but the adaptive-library hy‐
pothesis would be weakened within the tested domain.
Adoption Conditions
I would consider the application promising where many related tasks ex‐
ist, specifications are sufficiently clear and the verifier is more reliable
than the generator. I would not begin with systems where the target con‐
tinually changes, observability is very poor and evaluating an error costs
more than manual analysis.
Initial integration should be advisory: suggest analyses, produce tests and
explain which relation is missing. Only after independent evaluation
could it automate decisions with effects. Immediate usefulness may con‐
sist in accelerating an engineer's work, without claiming a universal
autonomous verifier.

Documents, Synthesis and
Procedures
The Summary as a Lossy Representation
A summary compresses. The question is not whether it loses information,
but whether it loses information needed for its use. A summary for gener‐
al orientation and one for applying a procedure have different criteria.
The same reduction may be acceptable in the first case and dangerous in
the second.
MRP would represent this requirement through a family of relevant ques‐
tions and transformations. Entities, conditions, exceptions, temporality
and claim status must be preserved insofar as they affect answers. We do
not assume this family can cover every future question.
A documentary result would include the summary, links to source pas‐
sages and a report on checks performed. This would allow the user to dis‐
tinguish “I found no exception” from “I checked every relevant section of
this document according to the defined procedure.”
Use Case: A Procedure with Exceptions
Imagine an internal procedure: requests are approved automatically be‐
low a certain value, except those involving a critical resource; exceptions
require additional approval. This text is an invented example, not a de‐
scription of an actual legal or organizational arrangement.
A summary preserving only the threshold may produce wrong answers.
Its representation confuses two requests with the same value but differ‐
ent resources. The counterexample indicates a conditional distinction, not
necessarily a fluency problem.
A new interpreter can extract the general rule, exception and precedence
relation. Another can check whether a summary sentence omits a neces‐
sary condition. For a controlled fragment, rules can become executable
predicates. For ambiguous texts, the system must retain candidate inter‐
pretations and mark the unresolved question.
63


Tests would vary request value, resource type and the presence of ap‐
proval. Evaluation cases would include differently worded exceptions and
unseen combinations, not merely copied uses of “except.” This separates
lexical recognition from preservation of the logical relation.
Use Case: Claims and Attribution
A report says a team supports hypothesis H but the results do not yet con‐
firm it. A faulty summary may state H directly. The lost information is not
a numerical datum but modality and attribution: who supports what and
with which status.
A documentary circuit could construct records containing the claim's con‐
tent, attributed author, hypothetical or asserted character, source passage
and relation to evidence. This representation does not guarantee correct
extraction, but allows these dimensions to be tested separately.
A verifier would compare the summary's claims with accepted records. If
a claim's status is stronger than the source's, it would flag an unjustified
promotion. This kind of distinction is reusable across documents and may
be a good target for library learning.
Two Links That Must Not Be Confused
The first link is translating text into a representation. The second is com‐
puting over that representation. A solver can impeccably prove that a
conclusion follows from extracted rules while the extraction itself is
wrong. Evaluation must measure both links.
For an initial experiment, we can generate documents from known struc‐
tures and vary their expression. This supplies an exact reference for the
relations studied. Later, authentic documents must be used with inde‐
pendent annotation and room for evaluator disagreement.
Synthetic data help isolate the mechanism, but may remain too close to
the generator's language. Success on them does not automatically estab‐
lish usefulness on real documents. The two stages must be reported separ‐
ately.
Quality Criteria
Accuracy on relevant questions is one criterion, not the only one. Cover‐
age, exception preservation, faithful attribution, source localization and
justified abstention also matter. A very short summary may correctly an‐
swer three known questions yet become useless for the rest of the task.
64


Some evaluation questions must be hidden from the generator. Human
assessment of usefulness must also be included without confusing it with
stylistic preference. A reader may prefer a fluent text that omits precisely
the important warning.
Cost must be compared with a reasonable baseline: an LLM with source
access, search and similar checks. If MRP receives an additional verifier
and memory denied to the alternative, the experiment does not isolate
the architecture.
Usefulness and Limits
The application could be valuable for technical manuals, product docu‐
mentation, internal procedures and research syntheses. Interpreter lib‐
raries would preserve recurring distinctions: condition versus conclusion,
hypothesis versus reported fact, current state versus history, primary
source versus indirect citation.
In domains with legal or medical consequences, this architecture should
be studied as documentary support under specialist oversight, rather
than presented as an autonomous authority. A proof about a summary's
structure is not professional validation of its recommendations.
The intended advantage is reducing identifiable errors and verification
costs across repeated documents. It is not a promise that every natural
meaning can be fully formalized. A useful technology can have explicit
limits and solve only certain types of distinctions very well.

CHAPTER 21
Research and Discovery
Three Different Activities
A research agent can generate hypotheses, select experiments and evalu‐
ate results. These activities are not identical. An interesting hypothesis
may be false; a well-chosen experiment may yield a negative result; a cor‐
rect analysis may lead to no new discovery.
MRP could organize their relationship through interpreters with different
roles. Some construct candidate models. Others calculate predictions. Oth‐
ers identify tests separating models or check whether a conclusion ex‐
ceeds the data. Each role's success must be evaluated against its own tar‐
get.
I would not define novelty through a model's internal surprise. A claim
may be rare in the agent's representation and well known in the literat‐
ure. Conversely, a new idea may be expressed in very ordinary terms.
Evaluating scientific priority requires external sources and a delineated
contribution.
Use Case: Two Mechanisms Compatible with the
Data
Assume a simulated system in which two models explain the same pass‐
ive observations. In the first, X and Y have a common cause. In the
second, X influences Y. The example in chapter 7 shows that observing X
and Y may not separate these mechanisms, while an intervention can.
A predictive interpreter identifies the models' agreement on existing data.
An intervention interpreter calculates which experiments produce differ‐
ent outcomes. The metacontroller compares experiment costs with the
value of separation. After observation, the model library is updated.
In a microworld, the evaluator knows the real mechanism. We can meas‐
ure how many experiments identification requires and their total cost. In
a real laboratory, assumptions about interventions, instruments and er‐
rors become part of the problem. The observation–intervention distinc‐
tion is fundamental, but does not replace experimental design
[Pearl2018].
66


Learning a Discovery Procedure
A more interesting contribution than solving one case would be learning
a reusable procedure. For example, when two models differ only in one
relation, the agent seeks an intervention that blocks the alternative ex‐
planation. The procedure must be formulated under conditions where
this reasoning is valid.
The same pattern cannot be mechanically transferred to every system.
The intervention may change other variables, be impossible or have
delayed effects. A reusable interpreter should request these conditions
rather than assume them.
The research library would therefore store both experiment templates
and their preconditions. Learning would mean not merely “which experi‐
ment worked,” but “in which class of situations does that experiment
identify the relevant difference?”
Compression, Explanation and Discovery
A shorter model may compress observations. This is useful but insuffi‐
cient for causal explanation. An interpolating formula may predict exist‐
ing examples without capturing intervention mechanisms. A reusable
concept must be evaluated on new consequences.
I would require three distinct tests for a hypothesis: fit to known data,
predictions under unseen conditions and, where causal meaning is
claimed, predictions about appropriate interventions. Absence of the
third test does not invalidate a predictive model, but limits its interpreta‐
tion.
MRP can make these limits visible in the epistemic register. A result can
be an excellent predictor while remaining unsupported as a mechanistic
explanation. This separation helps researchers precisely because it pre‐
vents a tacit leap from performance to ontology.
Use Case: Reviewing a Literature
The agent receives a bounded corpus of articles and a research question.
One interpreter extracts relevant claims, another identifies study meth‐
ods and conditions, and another separates independent confirmations
from citations of the same evidence.
67


An incident occurs when two results appear incompatible. The agent may
discover differences in populations, measurements or definitions. The
new distinction does not automatically resolve the dispute, but may refor‐
mulate the question so the conflict becomes investigable.
Evaluation would measure attribution correctness, identification of con‐
ditions and the usefulness of proposed questions for experts. I would not
use the number of self-declared “new ideas” as the main metric. It is too
easy to increase through rewording or unverifiable hypotheses.
The Relationship with the Human Researcher
The researcher establishes important problems, ethical limits and relev‐
ance criteria. The agent can help identify overlooked distinctions and ex‐
plore their consequences. This assumes no rigid division between human
creativity and automated computation; it requires explicit responsibilities
and evidence.
An initial system should produce hypothesis dossiers: model, assump‐
tions, data used, discriminating predictions, proposed experiments and
reasons the result would matter. Its value can be evaluated before any
spectacular discovery through time saved and methodological errors de‐
tected.
The application is ambitious but can be built gradually. The first realistic
target is an agent making research more verifiable and choosing experi‐
ments better within a narrow domain. It is not a machine guaranteeing
novelty or replacing scientific-community validation.

CHAPTER 22
Education and Interactive
Agents
Education as Diagnosing Distinctions
A pupil or student may apply a rule to ordinary examples yet confuse two
situations requiring different treatment. An MRP educational system
would try to identify this collision in responses and propose contrastive
examples. This is a design direction, not an empirical conclusion about
pedagogical effectiveness.
For example, a student may believe order never matters in composition.
The system offers commutative numerical operations and noncommutat‐
ive state operations. The question is not whether the student memorizes
an exception, but whether they recognize which property of the opera‐
tions justifies or prohibits changing their order.
The agent would preserve an explicit model of hypotheses about the stu‐
dent's difficulty. A wrong answer may arise from misunderstanding the
concept, an arithmetic error or ambiguous wording. It must not automat‐
ically become a permanent label of the person's ability.
Use Case: Interpreting a Condition
A learner confuses “if A, then B” with “if B, then A.” The system generates
situations where the original implication is true but its converse false. It
then changes the examples' domain to see whether the distinction trans‐
fers.
The pedagogical interpreter proposes an explanation and a set of ques‐
tions. A separate evaluator measures progress on unseen problems.
Memory should preserve which explanations were tried and under which
conditions they helped, rather than reduce the person to a single score.
MRP's value would lie in selecting pedagogical interventions appropriate
to the collision type. But the system must be compared with well-designed
teaching methods and an adaptive version without induction of new in‐
terpreters. Usefulness cannot be inferred solely from the model's eleg‐
ance.
69


What Must Not Be Optimized
Maximizing correct answers immediately after an explanation may favor
memorization. Maximizing time spent in the application may favor de‐
pendence on assistance. An educational experiment should track reten‐
tion, transfer and autonomy under conditions approved for participants.
Human evaluation requires an appropriate protocol and data protection.
Before that, the mechanism can be tested with simulated agents having
known errors. Those results would validate diagnosis within the model,
not demonstrate real pedagogical effects.
The architecture must allow the teacher or learner to challenge the dia‐
gnosis. A technical explanation of a “missing distinction” does not justify
reducing a complex human difficulty to a single presumed internal rep‐
resentation.
Agents That Observe and Act
An interactive agent can choose to look, measure, ask or execute an oper‐
ation. In these situations, interpreters receive not only textual symbols
but also images, sensor values or tool results. The MRP model does not ex‐
clude these inputs, but must declare which processing procedures are
supplied.
A simple example is a simulated device that may be “on,” “faulty” or
“pending.” The same indicator light can appear in several states. An im‐
age-only interpreter cannot separate the situations. An additional meas‐
urement or test command may produce the necessary distinction.
Here, learning an interpreter and choosing an observation become com‐
plementary. The new program can specify how to combine the indicator
with the test response. But without performing the test, the program ob‐
tains no information about the current state. This preserves the separa‐
tion between computation and data acquisition.
Use Case: A Workflow Assistant
An assistant can manage steps in an administrative or technical process:
request, verification, approval, execution. Initially, it receives explicit
states and simulated operations. The goal is to select the next permitted
action and detect missing information.
70


A collision occurs if two requests have identical visible fields but one re‐
ceived an approval that was later revoked. The agent must preserve relev‐
ant history or consult the authoritative source. Another collision may
arise from the difference between “approved” and “executed.”
A useful system would learn reusable checks for these distinctions
without automatically executing actions outside its mandate. Evaluation
would measure incorrect steps, unnecessary information requests, ab‐
stentions and total cost. Speed is insufficient if the agent skips mandatory
conditions.
Where the Analogy with Understanding Ends
An agent can learn to distinguish a device's states yet fail to understand
the social significance of the operation it performs. It may have a repres‐
entation sufficient for technical tests but insufficient for the consequences
for people.
This does not make the model useless. It shows that the family of tests and
objectives must be extended carefully and that some judgments remain
people's responsibility. Operational understanding always comes with a
boundary: for which tasks, observations and consequences is it sufficient?
Interactive applications are promising when we can observe outcomes,
limit effects and control the cost of experiments. In opaque or irreversible
settings, the same architecture must take a more cautious role, focused on
recommendations and clarification.

The Experimental Program
The Main Claim and Its Scope
A useful empirical hypothesis must name the tasks, the systems compared
and the criterion for success. For the first stage, I would formulate it as
follows: within a family of discrete processes with reusable structure, an
agent that diagnoses collisions, induces executable distinctions and con‐
solidates them achieves a better error–cost tradeoff than alternatives with
comparable access to information and tools.
This claim may be false. The chosen families may not offer enough trans‐
fer; induction may be too expensive; the selector may fail; a single model
may learn the same structure more efficiently. The protocol must be cap‐
able of producing these conclusions too.
We should not begin with the vague objective of “demonstrating under‐
standing.” We should begin with observable properties whose combina‐
tion supports or weakens the proposed interpretation. The name MRP
must not become a substitute for measurement.

Baseline Systems

The ideal baseline is not a realistic competitor and must be labeled ac‐

Ablating the Mechanisms
An ablation removes or replaces a component while keeping the rest as
constant as possible. Without diagnosis, the generator receives only the
error. Without consolidation, each solution remains local. Without
provenance, the agent cannot identify changed dependencies. Without
learned selection, we use a simple rule.
If removing a component does not change the results, it does not yet have
a demonstrated contribution in the experiment. It may be necessary un‐
der other conditions, but that possibility does not justify claiming a cur‐
rent advantage. Ablation should be viewed as a tool for simplification, not
a rhetorical obstacle.
For geometry, the alternatives may be an applicability table, a Euclidean
representation and a representation with cone-inspired regions. We com‐
pare the preservation of relevant relations and the cost, not merely the
error of a visual projection.

Structural Data Splits
Randomly splitting examples can leave the same structure in training and
testing. A program then learns a familiar template, and the result is
presented as generalization. I would split the data by rules, compositions,
dependency lengths and combinations of conditions.
One evaluation could reserve for testing all protocols in which an opera‐
tion cancels another's effect only for a particular key. Another could re‐
serve combinations of ordering and aggregation. Transfer then measures
something stronger than a change of names.
The same principle applies to documents. Fragments from the same docu‐
ment or generated from the same structure must not be treated as en‐
tirely independent observations. The sampling unit may be the document,
protocol or family, rather than each sentence or each nearly identical
trace.
Statistical Uncertainty
Results must be reported across multiple tasks and initializations, includ‐
ing their variation. An average advantage can hide serious failures in a
subfamily. Reporting the distribution of costs and errors, rather than only
the mean, is useful.
If a fixed candidate makes no errors in n independent tests from the same
distribution, its true error rate has not been proved to be zero. A one-
sided 95% confidence bound is 1 − 0.05 to the power 1/n, under the bino‐
mial model. The appendix explains the derivation and why it does not
automatically apply after adaptive selection on the same tests.
Stopping the experiment precisely when a favorable result appears and
omitting earlier attempts can distort the conclusions. The protocol must
specify the budget, stopping conditions and model selection method. Re‐
search on adaptive validation is relevant to this discipline [Dwork2015].

CHAPTER 24
Limits, Objections and
Contributions
“Everything Is an Interpreter”
This is the strongest objection of vacuity. If any function that transforms
an input is called an interpreter, then any system can be redescribed in
MRP vocabulary. Such a general redescription may be philosophically in‐
teresting, but it does not discriminate between architectures.
The response is to narrow the empirical program: explicitly represented
interpreters, scopes of application, observable collisions, induction mech‐
anisms and transfer criteria. We do not claim that a system lacking these
structures cannot understand. We investigate whether these structures
produce particular advantages.
The descriptive part can remain a general perspective. The technical part
must accept comparison with systems that do not use our terminology. A
good result from a monolithic model is relevant information, not a reason
to retrospectively redefine the model as MRP to save the hypothesis.
“It Is Just Operational Semantics”
Operational semantics can describe the system's execution, including
learning and changes of interpreters. MRP does not prove that there is a
cognitive process impossible to express semantically. Such a claim would
require a much stronger argument than the one offered here.
The useful distinction is between describing a system's rules and the
problem of selecting or constructing interpretation rules suited to a task.
MRP focuses on the latter. It can be formalized semantically without los‐
ing its usefulness, just as a learning algorithm can have an exact se‐
mantics.
The formulation “semantics are restricted pragmatics” is acceptable as a
choice of vocabulary within this framework if it means stabilized regimes
of interpretation. It is not a mandatory classification of all theories of
meaning and must not be used to downplay the contributions of formal
semantics.
76


“It Is a Combination of Familiar Ideas”
The objection is largely correct. Behavioral equivalences, abstractions,
active learning, program induction and metareasoning have strong pre‐
cedents. An architecture with multiple modules and a selector is not novel
merely because it contains a plurality; hierarchical mixtures of experts of‐
fer a clear precedent for dividing inputs among specialized components
[Jordan1993].
The difference sought by MRP is not the absence of routing in other sys‐
tems. It is the traceability of a complete transition: an error is diagnosed
as representational insufficiency, a new executable distinction is con‐
structed and checked, and its reuse is then measured. This combination
may yield a contribution, but its precise novelty must be demonstrated
through comparison and experiment.

“A Large Model Can Already Do All This”
In principle, a sufficiently expressive model can implement similar pro‐
cedures internally. MRP does not need monolithic models to be univer‐
sally incapable in order to be useful. The desired advantage may be con‐
trol, local verification, cost or adaptation with few examples.
The relevant comparison is operational: what can each system do with
the same budget and information? A large model may win. A hybrid sys‐
tem may win. The result may depend on task frequency and error costs.
The theory must allow this plurality.
It is also possible that the best technology uses a large model for proposals
and small interpreters for repeated execution. This is not a contradiction.
It shows that the useful unit of comparison is the complete system and
that roles must be attributed correctly.
“Where Is Understanding Beyond Success?”
Test performance does not exhaust human understanding. An agent may
exploit accidental regularities or possess very narrow competence. MRP
tries to reduce this problem through contrastive tests, structural transfer
and procedure verification, but it does not provide a definitive criterion
for consciousness or lived meaning.
The symbol grounding problem remains relevant [Harnad1990]. Linking
a representation to observations and interventions provides operational
grounding, but does not by definition resolve every philosophical ques‐
tion about meaning. The distinction between an engineering contribution
and a complete theory of mind must be preserved.
At the same time, the absence of a complete theory does not prevent test‐
ing concrete capabilities. We can prove that a representation loses a dis‐
tinction and show that a new program recovers it from the available data.
This is precise progress, even if it does not answer every question about
understanding.
Limits of Computability and Data
There is no general procedure that decides all interesting semantic prop‐
erties of arbitrary programs; computability imposes limits, and complex‐
ity practically limits many decidable problems [Barak]. Induction and
verification must therefore work in restricted languages, with approxima‐
tions or the possibility of reaching no conclusion.
78


No more expressive language can create missing empirical information
about the current world. No finite library guarantees sufficiency for all fu‐
ture questions. These limits do not require abandoning the program; they
require explicit domains and criteria for extension.
A system may be right not to learn a new interpreter. If the task is unique,
information is missing or cost exceeds benefit, abstention may be the best
option. Constant adaptation is not an end in itself.
What Is Worth Retaining
The strongest conceptual contribution is the shift in attention from rep‐
resentations viewed as static objects to distinctions constructed through
procedures. This formulation's mathematical contribution is modest but
clear: we can define sufficiency, collisions, distances and some limits of re‐
finement. The elementary results presented here are not claimed as new
theorems in the specialist literature.
The possible engineering contribution is an environment in which these
objects are executable, versioned and evaluated. The empirical contribu‐
tion remains to be established: whether such an environment learns and
transfers more efficiently than well-built alternatives.
In its most defensible form, Meta-Rational Pragmatics does not claim to
have found the ultimate form of knowledge. It proposes a working ques‐
tion: what relevant distinction can the agent not yet make, what proced‐
ure would make it possible, and what evidence shows that it is worth re‐
taining? A real technology can begin right here.

APPENDIX A
Elementary Mathematical
Results
The results below are instructional proofs for the model used in this book.
They are not claimed as original theorems. They show exactly where a
guarantee arises and which assumptions cannot be removed. We use fi‐
nite domains when this avoids difficulties with measurability or the exist‐
ence of an effective representation.
A1. The Limit of a Representation That Confounds
Labels
Consider N examples, each with a correct label, and a representation R.
We partition the examples into classes according to their R value. A pre‐
dictor that sees only R must use the same response distribution for every
example in a class.
Within a class, the best deterministic answer is the most frequent label.
Randomization cannot achieve a higher average accuracy either: the
weighted average of label frequencies does not exceed the maximum fre‐
quency. The maximum number of correct answers in the class is there‐
fore the frequency of its most common label.
Minimum empirical error = 1 − (sum of the majority-label frequencies in
each class) / N.
For two equally probable histories with the same representation and op‐
posite labels, the minimum error is 0.5. The bound applies to a predictor
that receives no other information. If we give it the example's identity or
the original history, we have changed the experiment.
A2. Sufficiency and Response Factorization
Let f be the correct answer to a deterministic question and R a represent‐
ation on a finite domain. A function g such that f(h) = g(R(h)) for every h
exists if and only if all histories with the same R value have the same an‐
swer f.
80


The forward direction is immediate: the same input to g produces the
same result. For the reverse direction, we define g at each R value by its
class's common answer. The condition guarantees that the definition is
unambiguous.
The result asserts the existence of a function, not its efficiency. The table g
may be enormous. For infinite domains, the mathematical existence of a
factorization does not automatically imply a computable procedure or
one accessible to the agent.
A3. Refinement Does Not Worsen Optimal Risk
Without Costs
Suppose the new representation preserves the old one and adds D:
Rnew(h) = (R(h), D(h)). Any old predictor can be simulated on the new rep‐
resentation by ignoring component D. Therefore, the lowest possible risk
on the new representation cannot be greater than the lowest possible risk
on the old one.
The conclusion concerns the optimal predictor within a sufficiently broad
class. A concrete algorithm trained on finite data may perform worse
after a component is added. Cost, estimation difficulty or overfitting risk
may increase. The theorem does not guarantee progress by a real system
after every refinement.
A4. The Number of Strict Refinements in a Finite
Sample
A partition of N objects has at most N classes. If we start with K classes
and each strict refinement increases the number of classes by at least one,
there can be at most N − K strict refinements, provided we do not sub‐
sequently merge classes.
The proof is a counting argument. After m refinements we have at least K
+ m classes; since K + m cannot exceed N, we obtain m ≤ N − K. The
bound says nothing about the cost of finding each refinement. Nor does it
say that the final partition generalizes: separating every example may
amount only to memorization.
81


A5. The Test Pseudometric
For every test t, total variation satisfies TV(P1, P3) ≤ TV(P1, P2) + TV(P2,
P3). Each term on the right is at most the corresponding supremum over
all tests. Taking the supremum on the left as well, we obtain dT(h1, h3) ≤
dT(h1, h2) + dT(h2, h3).
Nonnegativity and symmetry are inherited directly. The distance can be
zero between distinct histories because the tests do not separate them. On
classes of histories at zero distance, the function becomes a metric.
Adding tests cannot decrease this supremum-defined distance, but may
increase the cost of estimating it.
A6. The 2e Bound for Choosing an Action
Assume a finite set of actions. The estimated value of each action differs
from its true value by at most e. Let a* denote a truly optimal action and â
an action with the highest estimate. The symbol â is simply a name for the
choice based on the estimate.
The true value of a* is at most its estimate plus e. By the definition of the
choice, its estimate does not exceed the estimate of â. The estimate of â is
at most the true value of â plus e. Combining these three relations, the
true value of a* is at most the true value of â plus 2e.
The result requires error control for every action compared. A small aver‐
age error, without control of the selected action, does not provide the
same guarantee. For long plans, what the estimated value of each policy
represents must be justified separately.
A7. Zero Errors Does Not Mean Zero Error Rate
Assume a fixed candidate, independent tests and the same true error
probability r on every test. The probability of observing zero errors in n
tests is (1 − r) to the power n. We choose the bound r so that this probabil‐
ity is 0.05. This gives r = 1 − 0.05 to the power 1/n.
For n = 100, the bound is approximately 0.0295, or 2.95%. Observing zero
errors permits this one-sided 95% confidence bound under the stated
model, not the conclusion that error is impossible. For large n, the ap‐
proximation 3/n is useful because the negative natural logarithm of 0.05 is
close to three.
82


The frequentist interpretation concerns the procedure's coverage over re‐
petitions of the experiment, not a 95% probability assigned to the fixed
parameter without a Bayesian model. The bound does not apply un‐
changed when the candidate was selected after trying many variants on
the same tests.
A8. A Simple Control for a Finite Family of
Candidates
We have K candidates fixed before evaluation, where K is a positive in‐
teger, and e and d are numbers strictly between zero and one. For any
candidate with a true error rate of at least e, the probability of passing n
independent tests without error is at most (1 − e) to the power n, which is
at most exp(−n × e).
The probability that at least one of the K bad candidates passes is at most
the sum of these probabilities: K × exp(−n × e). Independence between
candidates is not necessary for this union bound. For the bound to be at
most d, it suffices that n be at least [ln(K) + ln(1/d)] / e.
This is a limited guarantee: the class must be fixed independently of the
evaluation set, the tests must respect the model, and the criterion is
passing without errors. An adaptive search that changes the class without
limit in response to results requires another analysis. The formula ex‐
plains why the number of attempts matters; it does not by itself solve all
of MRP validation.

APPENDIX B
Exercises with Commented
Solutions
B1. Set or Sequence?
Problem: an agent stores only the set of operations “add 1” and “multiply
by 2.” Starting from zero, can it determine the result of any two-operation
sequence in which each appears once?
Solution: no. Addition followed by multiplication produces two; multiplic‐
ation followed by addition produces one. The set is the same, but the res‐
ult differs. A sufficient representation must preserve the order or other
information from which the result can be computed. The number of oper‐
ations does not repair the collision because it is two in both cases.
B2. What Needs Refining?
Problem: the log preserves the correct order, but the interpreter selects
the first event. Is a new representation necessary?
Solution: not for this cause. The necessary information exists. The selec‐
tion operation must be corrected. A system that adds a new representa‐
tion may accidentally repair the result, but would miss the diagnosis and
introduce unnecessary complexity.
B3. Noise or Collision?
Problem: the same representation sometimes produces success and some‐
times failure. Has its insufficiency been demonstrated?
Solution: no. If the process is stochastic, both outcomes may come from
the same distribution. Repetitions or a model showing that two situations
with the same representation induce different distributions are necessary.
A single disagreement between labels does not distinguish variability
from lost information.
B4. The Missing Relation
Problem: x and y each take values between zero and ten, and the agent
must prove that x − y is zero. What additional information is sufficient?
84


Solution: the relation x = y is sufficient. Narrowing the intervals separ‐
ately may help in particular cases, but does not generally capture the de‐
pendency. The example shows why a relational representation can matter
more than greater numerical precision for each variable considered sep‐
arately.
B5. When Is Learning Worthwhile?
Problem: constructing and checking an interpreter costs 1,000 ms. The old
cost is 40 ms per task, and the new cost, including selection, is 10 ms. How
many uses are needed for a strict gain?
Solution: the saving is 30 ms per use. More than 1,000/30 uses are needed,
hence 34. At 34, the old cost is 1,360 ms and the new cost 1,340 ms. The
result assumes that accuracy remains comparable and that no additional
invalidation costs arise.
B6. A Neighborhood That Is Not an Equivalence
Problem: three binary predictions have probabilities 0.00, 0.06 and 0.12.
At threshold 0.08, can we merge all close predictions into a class with dia‐
meter at most 0.08?
Solution: no. The first is close to the second and the second to the third,
but the first and third are 0.12 apart. The transitive closure of the close‐
ness relation can exceed the threshold. The clustering algorithm must
control the diameter or explicitly accept a different guarantee.
B7. A Procedure's Cone
Problem: two procedures have net utilities (0.8; 0.2) and (0.4; 0.6) in two
states. For what probability p of the first state is the first preferable?
Solution: the first has value 0.2 + 0.6p, the second 0.6 − 0.2p. Comparing
them gives 0.8p ≥ 0.4, hence p ≥ 0.5. In unnormalized weights, the condi‐
tion is z1 ≥ z2. This defines a cone in the nonnegative quadrant, with
equal preference on the boundary.
B8. Geometric Compression
Problem: we represent a point by x² + y². Can we answer both whether it
lies in a concentric annulus and whether it lies to the left of the origin?
85


Solution: the first question can be answered by comparing the squared ra‐
dius with the thresholds. The second cannot: points (1; 0) and (−1; 0) have
the same representation. The geometry was simplified by losing direction,
not by preserving all information.
B9. Correct Proof, Unsupported Conclusion
Problem: a solver proves that a summary respects the rules extracted
from a document. Can we declare the summary faithful to the document?
Solution: only if the correctness of extracting the relevant rules is also jus‐
tified. Internal verification does not automatically cover translation from
text. Two distinct bodies of evidence must be preserved: the link between
document and representation, then the link between representation and
summary.
B10. How a Favorite Hypothesis Is Lost
Problem: the MRP system outperforms a simple model but does not out‐
perform a fixed library with a selector once all costs are included. What
conclusion is defensible?
Solution: the result supports the usefulness of specialization or selection
in the experiment, not the advantage of adaptive induction. We must in‐
vestigate whether the fixed library already covers all necessary distinc‐
tions or whether induction is too costly. It is illegitimate to credit the com‐
plete system with an advantage its distinctive component did not pro‐
duce.

APPENDIX C
Operational Glossary
Abstraction. A representation that preserves some properties and ig‐
nores others. Its value depends on the questions for which it is used.
Abstention. An outcome in which the agent does not provide the reques‐
ted answer because information, verification or budget is insufficient.
Adapter. A procedure that transforms one representation into another,
with a contract concerning the information preserved and lost.
Agent. A system that receives observations, maintains a state and chooses
operations or actions in relation to tasks and constraints.
Atlas of interpreters. A proposed organization of multiple local repres‐
entations and the adapters between them; it does not assume a geometric
manifold.
Adaptive library. A versioned collection of interpreters that can be ex‐
tended, consolidated, suspended and evaluated through reuse.
Calibration. Agreement between stated probabilities and the relevant
frequencies of outcomes; it is not identical to accuracy.
Circuit. An executable composition represented by operations and de‐
pendencies between their values.
Relevant collision. The identification under the same representation of
situations requiring different answers or distributions for the same test.
Convex cone. A set closed under linear combinations with nonnegative
coefficients; in this book it can describe preference regions among pro‐
cedures.
Consolidation. The promotion and reorganization of procedures for re‐
use under explicit evaluation and cost criteria.
Contract. A specification of an operation's inputs, outputs, preconditions,
effects and limits.
Counterexample. A case that refutes a precise claim. In CEGAR, whether
the abstract example corresponds to concrete behavior must be checked.
Executable distinction. A relevant difference that a program can com‐
pute from available information and that can be evaluated.
87


Behavioral equivalence. Indistinguishability with respect to a declared
family of continuations or tests.
Episode. A contextualized record of observations and actions, with the
relevant identities and order.
Execution epoch. A logical interval during which a task uses stable ver‐
sions of interpreters and policy.
Evidence. An artifact supporting a bounded status: observation, test, de‐
rivation, proof or independent evaluation.
Factorization. The possibility of obtaining a correct answer through an
intermediate representation and a function applied to it.
Generalization. Performance on cases not used to construct or select the
solution, under explicitly described distributions.
Program induction. The construction of candidate programs from ex‐
amples, specifications and a vocabulary of operations.
Interpreter. A procedure that transforms inputs and state into results,
new state and evidence under declared conditions.
Abstract interpretation. A family of methods for soundly approximating
program behavior in domains that preserve selected properties.
Intervention. An action that changes a component of the system under
study; its effect is generally not identical to observational conditioning.
Invariant. A property preserved through a system's relevant transitions
under explicit assumptions.
Meta-controller. A component that selects computation, observation, in‐
duction or stopping operations, taking costs into account.
MRP. The research program of constructing, selecting and revising inter‐
preters through verifiable consequences and resource constraints.
MRP-VM. A possible execution environment that manages the state, de‐
pendencies, interpreters and acceptance conditions of MRP operations.
Policy. A rule that chooses actions according to available information; it
may include responses to intermediate results.
Provenance. Information about a result's sources and transformations; it
facilitates auditing but does not guarantee truth.
Pseudometric. A distance function that satisfies nonnegativity, symmetry
and the triangle inequality but may be zero between distinct objects.
88


Refinement. The addition of distinctions without losing those previously
preserved, when the term is used in the strict sense of partitions.
Predictive representation. A description of state through predictions
about test outcomes, rather than necessarily identifying a latent state.
SOP Lang. A language option for executable compositions through cir‐
cuits, discussed here as possible support for MRP, not as a premise of the
theory.
Sufficiency. The preservation of information necessary for a declared
family of answers or predictions; it does not mean preserving all informa‐
tion.
Transfer. The reuse of a capability in other situations or task families,
with checks on its conditions of applicability.
Value of computation. The expected decision gain produced by an in‐
ternal operation after relevant costs are subtracted.
Formal verification. Establishing a property for all cases covered by a
model and the proof's assumptions, not for every possible world.
