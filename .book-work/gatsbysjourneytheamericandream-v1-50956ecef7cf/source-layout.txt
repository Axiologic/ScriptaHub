                      INTRODUCTION




        Imagine having everything you’ve ever dreamed of right at your
fingertips—wealth, success, the admiration of countless people—and yet,
feeling like it’s all slipping through your fingers. That’s the world F. Scott
Fitzgerald invites you into with The Great Gatsby, a novel that dazzles on the
surface but hides deep questions beneath its glittering exterior. It’s not just
about lavish parties or fast cars, but about ambition, love, and the dangerous
allure of chasing dreams that might never be real.

        At the heart of the story is Jay Gatsby, a man who appears to have it
all. His mansion is the envy of everyone on Long Island, his parties are the
talk of the town, and he’s managed to build himself up from nothing into a
figure of wealth and success. But here’s the twist: Gatsby’s life is entirely built
around one obsession—winning back his lost love, Daisy Buchanan.
Everything he’s done, all the money, all the charm, has been for her. And this
obsession isn’t just about rekindling an old romance. It’s about reclaiming




                                                                                 7
the past, about convincing himself that if he can win Daisy, he can rewrite his
life and find happiness.

        But here’s the thing—Daisy isn’t the ideal Gatsby has built up in his
mind. In fact, the Daisy he loves might never have existed in the first place.
Gatsby’s pursuit of her is like chasing a mirage; no matter how close he thinks
he’s getting, it remains just out of reach. This is where The Great Gatsby
moves beyond just a story of romance or wealth and dives into something
more universal: the question of what happens when our dreams collide with
reality. How far will we go to chase an illusion? And what happens when that
illusion shatters?

        Fitzgerald uses Gatsby’s life as a critique of the American Dream
itself. We’re told that with hard work, anyone can rise to the top, achieve
success, and find happiness. But in Gatsby’s world, even when you’ve
seemingly made it, there are invisible barriers that wealth alone can’t break
down. Gatsby’s “new money” can never quite get him into the world of “old
money” where Tom Buchanan, Daisy’s husband, resides. No matter how
grand Gatsby parties or how much he tries to fit in, he’ll always be an
outsider looking in. This sense of never truly belonging speaks to the
hollowness of the American Dream as Fitzgerald portrays it—a dream that
promises everything but leaves many feeling empty.

        Through the eyes of Nick Carraway, the novel’s sharp-witted and
sometimes weary narrator, we get a front-row seat to Gatsby’s rise and tragic
fall. Nick is both fascinated by Gatsby’s relentless pursuit of his dream and


                                                                             8
troubled by the costs that come with it. He watches as Gatsby, a man who is
at once grand and fragile, invests everything he has—emotionally, financially,
spiritually—into this one impossible goal. As we follow Gatsby on his path,
we’re left to ask ourselves: is it better to chase a dream, even knowing it might
destroy us, or to accept reality and give up the chase?

         The brilliance of The Great Gatsby lies in its ability to draw us into a
world that looks like it has everything—money, status, beauty—while
showing us how hollow it can be. The parties, the glamour, and the wild
energy of the Roaring Twenties are all part of a sparkling facade that conceals
a much darker truth. Fitzgerald captures the moral decay beneath the glitzy
surface of the 1920s, a time when appearances mattered more than
substance, and people were willing to sacrifice almost anything to keep up the
illusion of success.

         But more than a critique of a particular era, The Great Gatsby
remains timeless because it speaks to a universal human experience—the
drive to make our dreams a reality, even when they might be out of reach.
Gatsby’s tragedy is not just that he fails, but that he believes so deeply in
something that was never truly his to begin with. He’s a man who, despite all
his wealth and charm, remains profoundly lonely and disconnected from the
very world he’s trying to conquer.

         So, why read The Great Gatsby? Because it’s more than just a story
of the Roaring Twenties or a tragic love affair. It’s a novel that invites us to
question the nature of our own ambitions, the things we long for, and the


                                                                               9
lengths we’ll go to achieve them. It asks whether the dreams we chase are ever
really worth the cost and what we lose in the pursuit of things that may not
even exist. And in Gatsby’s rise and fall, we might just catch a glimpse of
ourselves, grappling with our own hopes, illusions, and realities.

the idea that no matter who you are or where you come from, you can climb

the American Dream might not be all it’s cracked up to be.

sometimes, moral shortcuts.

     The Glittering Facade of the Roaring
                              Twenties

        Imagine stepping into one of Jay Gatsby’s legendary parties. The
music is roaring, the champagne is flowing, and the energy is electric.
Everywhere you look, there are glamorous people dancing, laughing, and
losing themselves in the sheer joy of the moment. It’s a world where
everything seems possible, where wealth and extravagance take center stage.
But just as in Gatsby’s life, beneath the surface of these glittering parties,
there’s something hollow, something missing. Welcome to the Roaring
Twenties.

        The 1920s, often called the Jazz Age, was a time of wild prosperity
and rapid social change. World War I had ended, and with it came a sense of
optimism and a hunger for new experiences. People were eager to forget the



                                                                           16
horrors of war, and they turned to jazz music, dancing, and parties as a way
to escape. Prohibition, which banned the sale of alcohol, only made the allure
of these gatherings more enticing. Speakeasies, hidden bars where alcohol
flowed freely, became hotspots of the era. And who better to embody this era
of indulgence and excess than Jay Gatsby?

        Fitzgerald uses Gatsby’s lavish parties as a symbol of the Roaring
Twenties itself. Gatsby’s mansion is a beacon of luxury, where hundreds of
guests—most of whom have never even met Gatsby—show up for nights of
drinking, dancing, and indulging in life’s finest pleasures. But here’s the
thing: these parties are empty at their core. The guests are not there out of
loyalty or friendship; they’re there for the spectacle, for the chance to rub
elbows with the rich and famous. They’re drawn to Gatsby’s wealth, much
like moths to a flame, without caring about the man behind the mansion.

        Fitzgerald is making a bigger point here: the Roaring Twenties may
have looked glamorous, but underneath, it was a time of moral decay. The era
was all about appearances—people wanted to look successful, to look like
they were living the dream. But much like Gatsby’s parties, the substance was
missing. People were chasing temporary highs—whether through money,
alcohol, or social status—without understanding the emptiness it left
behind. Gatsby’s parties, full of strangers enjoying themselves without a care
in the world, are a perfect metaphor for the 1920s as a whole: glittering on
the outside, but ultimately hollow on the inside.

thrill. And when Gatsby’s life falls apart, they’re nowhere to be found.

       The Tragic Downfall of Jay Gatsby

        By the time we reach the final stretch of Jay Gatsby’s story, one thing
is clear: he’s a man driven by unwavering hope and an almost superhuman
ability to believe in the impossible. But here’s the problem with believing too

        When we left off, Gatsby was standing outside Daisy’s house, still
clinging to the hope that she’d choose him, still waiting for her to come
running into his arms. But, as we already know, that’s not how this story
ends. Daisy stays with Tom, locked in her world of privilege, too afraid to
upend her life for a man whose wealth, unlike Tom’s, is rooted in something
unsavory. And Gatsby, still hopeful, can’t quite grasp that he’s already lost.

        The real unraveling begins with the confrontation between Gatsby
and Tom Buchanan—a showdown that takes place in a suffocating,



                                                                                 27
sweltering New York apartment. This is the moment when Gatsby’s fantasy
comes crashing headfirst into reality. Up until this point, Gatsby has believed
that Daisy will leave Tom, that his love and wealth will be enough to pull her
away from her old life. But Tom, brimming with arrogance and old-money
entitlement, isn’t about to let that happen. He exposes Gatsby’s shady past,
calling    out    the    fact   that   his   fortune     comes     from    illegal
activities—bootlegging, to be precise. It’s a crushing blow, not just because it
undermines Gatsby’s credibility, but because it exposes a fundamental truth:
no matter how much money Gatsby has, he will never be accepted into
Tom’s world of old money and social pedigree.

          Tom’s attack does more than just reveal Gatsby’s criminal
background—it forces Daisy to confront a choice she’s been avoiding. For a
brief moment, Daisy seems torn, genuinely confused about whether to stay
with Tom or go with Gatsby. But Tom’s dominance and security win out.
Daisy may love Gatsby, or at least the version of him she’s idealized, but she’s
not willing to sacrifice the safety of her life with Tom. She’s too entrenched
in the comfort of her world to risk it all for Gatsby’s dream.

          For Gatsby, this is the most devastating moment of his life. His entire
identity has been built around this one goal: winning back Daisy. Everything
he’s done—the parties, the wealth, the persona—has been for her. And in
this single moment, that dream evaporates. Yet, even as Daisy retreats back
into Tom’s world, Gatsby still can’t let go. He refuses to believe that it’s over.
He stands outside her house that night, convinced that she’ll come to him.



                                                                               28
The tragic irony is that while Gatsby is waiting, Tom and Daisy are inside,
patching things up, their lives continuing as if Gatsby’s dream never existed.

        What’s heartbreaking about Gatsby’s downfall is not just that he
loses Daisy, but that he never sees it coming. Even after the confrontation,
even after Daisy chooses Tom, Gatsby remains hopeful. He’s deluded himself
into believing that love can conquer all, that money and success will finally
earn him a place at the top. But life, as Fitzgerald so brutally illustrates,
doesn’t work that way. The dream Gatsby has been chasing is built on a
flawed foundation—an illusion that no amount of wealth can make real.

        The real tragedy, though, begins with the accident. As Gatsby and
Daisy leave New York, their world starts to unravel in ways neither of them
could predict. Gatsby lets Daisy drive his car, a bright yellow Rolls-Royce,
and they accidentally hit and kill Myrtle Wilson, Tom’s mistress. Here’s
where things take a dark and irreversible turn. Tom, furious and grieving over
Myrtle’s death, seizes the opportunity to destroy Gatsby once and for all. In a
conversation dripping with manipulation, he plants the idea in George
Wilson’s mind that it was Gatsby driving the car. Of course, it wasn’t—Daisy
was behind the wheel—but that fact doesn’t matter. Tom wants to protect
himself and Daisy, and Gatsby becomes the perfect scapegoat.

        Gatsby, ever the romantic, takes the blame for the accident. He tells
Nick that he’s going to wait outside Daisy’s house, just in case she needs him.
Even now, Gatsby’s belief in Daisy’s love is unwavering. He’s convinced that
she’ll call for him, that she’ll leave Tom and run away with him. It’s a


                                                                             29
moment that’s both touching and tragic. Gatsby has staked everything on
Daisy, and even as his world crumbles around him, he refuses to see the truth.
Daisy, of course, never calls. She’s safely inside, secure in her decision to stay
with Tom, while Gatsby remains outside, waiting for a signal that will never
come.

        The end comes swiftly and violently. George Wilson, convinced that
Gatsby is responsible for Myrtle’s death, tracks him down to his mansion. In
a tragic misunderstanding, Wilson shoots Gatsby while he’s swimming in his
pool—the symbol of his wealth and success—and then takes his own life.
And just like that, Gatsby’s story ends, not with a bang but with a quiet,
lonely death in the middle of his empty mansion.

        The fallout from Gatsby’s death is perhaps the most brutal
commentary on the world he lived in. None of the hundreds of people who
attended his lavish parties come to his funeral. Nick is left to arrange
everything, and only a handful of people—Nick, Gatsby’s father, and a few
servants—show up to mourn the man who, for all his wealth and charm, was
nothing more than a fleeting curiosity to the society he tried so hard to
impress.

        In the end, Gatsby’s downfall is a reflection of the failure of the
American Dream itself. He believed in the promise that money and success
could buy him happiness, love, and a place in the upper echelons of society.
But as Fitzgerald shows us, that dream is flawed. It’s built on appearances, on
a hollow pursuit of wealth and status that can never truly satisfy. Gatsby, for


                                                                               30
all his grand ambition and unshakable hope, was chasing something that
didn’t exist.

         As we look back on Gatsby’s story, it’s hard not to feel a sense of
both admiration and pity for him. He was a man who believed in something
larger than life, who never gave up on his dream, no matter how impossible it
seemed. But in the end, that dream consumed him. It’s a reminder that
sometimes, the things we chase are nothing more than illusions—and when
we cling too tightly to them, they can lead us to ruin.

         And so, as we leave Gatsby behind, floating lifeless in his pool, we’re
left to ask ourselves: was it worth it? Was the pursuit of a dream, however
impossible, better than never dreaming at all? Or was Gatsby simply the
victim of his own illusions, doomed from the start? That, perhaps, is the final
mystery of Jay Gatsby’s life.

                        CONCLUSION




      The Great Gatsby is a powerful reflection on the nature of dreams,
ambition, and the illusions we create for ourselves. Through the rise and fall
of Jay Gatsby, F. Scott Fitzgerald offers us profound insights into the dangers
of chasing fantasies that are rooted in nostalgia or impossible ideals. At the
heart of this novel lies a question that feels timeless: What happens when the
dreams we’ve spent our lives pursuing turn out to be unreachable? And more
importantly, what does that pursuit cost us along the way?

      One of the most striking lessons from The Great Gatsby is the way it
critiques the American Dream. The novel takes place in a time—the
1920s—when the American Dream was widely viewed as an attainable goal.
It was a period of prosperity, where hard work was believed to lead to wealth,
success, and happiness. Gatsby, with his mansion, his lavish parties, and his
carefully constructed persona, seems like the ultimate success story. He rises
from humble beginnings to become one of the wealthiest and most admired
men in New York. But beneath all the wealth and glamor, Gatsby is


                                                                            32
profoundly empty, chasing something that money can’t buy: love,
acceptance, and a return to a lost past.

      The novel shows us that the American Dream, as we often understand
it, can be a hollow pursuit. Gatsby’s wealth comes from questionable sources,
and while he has achieved outward success, he is never truly satisfied or
happy. His dream is not about wealth for wealth’s sake; it’s about using that
wealth to win back Daisy Buchanan, the woman he loved when he was young
and poor. But the tragedy is that Gatsby’s dream of Daisy is built on an
illusion. He’s not just in love with her—he’s in love with the idea of what she
represents: the perfect life, the fulfillment of all his desires. But Daisy, like the
American Dream, is ultimately unattainable. She cannot live up to Gatsby’s
idealized version of her, and when he realizes this, his entire world begins to
crumble.

      This leads us to another critical idea in the novel: the danger of living in
the past. Gatsby’s entire life is defined by his desire to recapture a moment
from his youth—a time when he and Daisy were in love. He believes that if
he can just win her back, they can start fresh as if the years in between never
happened. But Fitzgerald makes it clear that the past is something we can
never truly reclaim. Gatsby’s refusal to accept this is what makes his story so
tragic. He’s chasing something that no longer exists, and in doing so, he’s
blind to the present and the reality of who Daisy is now. In many ways, this
obsession with the past mirrors how we sometimes idealize our own
memories, clinging to versions of our lives that are no longer possible.



                                                                                  33
      The Great Gatsby also explores the divide between appearance and
reality—a theme that resonates beyond the Roaring Twenties and into our
modern lives. On the surface, Gatsby’s life seems perfect. He has the
mansion, the money, the popularity, and the charm. But as we peel back the
layers, we see the cracks in the facade. His wealth is built on illegal activities,
his parties are filled with strangers who don’t care about him, and his love for
Daisy is based on an idealized version of her that doesn’t reflect who she truly
is. This gap between appearance and reality is something we can all relate to
in today’s world, where social media often presents an exaggerated version of
people’s lives, making us feel like everyone else is living a perfect, fulfilled
existence. Fitzgerald’s novel reminds us that what we see on the surface rarely
reflects the truth of someone’s life.

      Gatsby’s tragic flaw—his inability to let go of the past and his
unrelenting belief in the power of wealth and love to solve everything—also
teaches us about the limits of ambition. Ambition, in itself, isn’t the
problem. In fact, Gatsby’s drive and determination are admirable. But when
ambition becomes an obsession, when it blinds us to reality, it can lead to
destruction. Gatsby’s refusal to accept that some things—like the past and
Daisy’s love—are beyond his control is what ultimately leads to his downfall.
It’s a cautionary tale about the dangers of investing too much in one dream
and losing sight of the present and the people around us.

      Moreover, the novel also critiques social class and the rigidity of the
world Gatsby is trying to break into. No matter how wealthy Gatsby



                                                                                34
becomes, he will never truly be accepted by the “old money” elites like Tom
Buchanan. This divide between “new money” and “old money” is a stark
reminder that some barriers can’t be overcome by wealth alone. Gatsby’s
story reveals the limits of upward mobility and the cruel reality that some
aspects of social status are inherited, not earned. His parties might be filled
with people who admire his wealth, but they are quick to disappear when
things fall apart, leaving him isolated and alone.

      In a broader sense, The Great Gatsby invites us to reflect on our own
lives and ambitions. What are the dreams we are chasing? Are they based on
something real, or are they built on illusions? The novel challenges us to
examine the cost of our pursuits and to consider whether the things we think
will bring us happiness are truly worth the sacrifices we’re making. It’s a story
about the human condition—our desire for more, our tendency to idealize
the past, and our struggle to balance ambition with acceptance of reality.

      Ultimately, the lessons from The Great Gatsby are timeless. They
encourage us to reflect on our own dreams and ambitions and to be wary of
the illusions we might be chasing. The novel serves as a reminder that while
it’s important to have goals and desires, it’s equally important to stay
grounded in reality, to value the present, and to recognize when a dream is no
longer worth pursuing. Whether or not you’ve experienced the glitz and
glamor of Gatsby’s world, the themes of this story resonate deeply with
anyone who has ever aspired to something more, only to discover that the
road to fulfillment is far more complicated than it seems.
