Introduction to Artificial Intelligence

Ten-minute edition · Albert Meta One

This edition introduces the main idea of each of the eleven chapters and links to the complete reader.

The Foundations of Artificial Intelligence

The opening chapter defines artificial intelligence as the attempt to build systems that perform tasks associated with human cognition. It traces the field from the Dartmouth workshop through symbolic programs, an AI winter, and renewed interest in learning systems. The author links more recent progress to computing power, data and improved algorithms, while warning that early predictions of human-level AI were overconfident.

Symbolic AI and Logic-Based Systems

Symbolic AI represents knowledge through explicit symbols and rules. Logic, expert systems and ontologies can make reasoning traceable and useful in structured domains. Yet hand-authored rules struggle with ambiguity, changing environments and the scale of everyday knowledge. The chapter treats symbolic methods as a lasting part of AI, even as data-driven approaches take on tasks that resisted fixed rules.

Machine Learning: The Data-Driven Revolution

Machine learning replaces many handcrafted rules with patterns learned from examples. The chapter distinguishes supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning, then considers training data, model evaluation and generalization to unseen cases. It explains underfitting and overfitting as opposite failures. The author sees powerful applications but also stresses bias, interpretability and the need to use models responsibly.

Read the complete chapter

Neural Networks and Deep Learning

Layers of artificial neurons combine weighted inputs and nonlinear activation functions. Training adjusts weights through error measurement and backpropagation. The chapter introduces common loss functions and the rectified linear unit, then discusses deeper architectures and applications. Its mathematical examples show how networks learn patterns, while the surrounding discussion acknowledges their computational demands and the difficulty of understanding what they have learned.

Natural Language Processing and Large Language Models

The language chapter follows the move from rule-based processing to statistical and neural methods. It explains how large language models learn to predict text from vast corpora and can support writing, translation and information access. Their fluent output does not guarantee understanding or truth. The author flags bias, misinformation, privacy and the social effects of systems that increasingly mediate communication.

Computer Vision and Image Recognition

Computer vision turns images and video into information machines can use. The chapter moves from early feature engineering to convolutional networks, object recognition and newer learning methods, including self-supervision. Applications range from medicine to autonomous systems. The author also examines data quality, robustness, privacy and the danger of using visual recognition without suitable oversight.

Reasoning in AI

Reasoning requires drawing conclusions, handling uncertainty and connecting evidence with decisions. The chapter compares logical methods, probabilistic reasoning and neural approaches, including hybrids that combine perception with symbolic steps. It asks how an AI system can explain its conclusions and avoid brittle errors when facing unfamiliar situations. Progress in machine reasoning also raises questions about human reasoning itself.

Read the complete chapter

Planning and Problem Solving in AI

Planning turns goals into sequences of action while accounting for constraints and changing conditions. Search methods, heuristics and reinforcement learning offer different ways to explore possible outcomes. The chapter discusses robotics, games and more open-ended creative problem solving. Its central challenge is to build systems that can adapt a plan rather than merely follow one fixed path.

Read the complete chapter

Knowledge Representation in AI

AI needs ways to organize facts, relationships and uncertain information so it can use them. The chapter surveys symbols, knowledge graphs and representations learned from data. Explicit structures are interpretable but expensive to maintain; learned representations are flexible but often opaque. The author argues that combining these strengths may help systems reason more reliably and make their knowledge more useful to people.

Read the complete chapter

AI Ethics and Societal Impact

Technical capability does not settle whether a use is fair or wise. The chapter considers biased decisions, surveillance, privacy, employment, accountability and the concentration of power. It also raises longer-term risks from more capable systems as questions requiring research and governance. Ethical AI, in the author’s view, calls for diverse participation, clear responsibility and attention to the people affected.

The Future of AI: Challenges and Opportunities

The final chapter looks toward advances in learning, reasoning and collaboration between people and machines. It pairs possibilities in science, health and other fields with challenges of safety, data governance, inequality and public trust. These are projections from the source’s 2024 perspective, not claims about today’s capabilities. The book closes by urging deliberate choices so that AI development reflects human values.
