We Manufacture Our Own Masters

A ten-minute reading

The book begins from tensions inside attractive political ideals. A scheme meant to reduce domination can create a new route to it: equality may concentrate administrative power, markets may let winners buy the rules, and expertise may become a caste. These are proposed “antinomies of governance,” not a claim that Kant’s formal antinomies prove a political result. The text tests liberalism, socialism, localism, authoritarian performance, private city government, AI, and guaranteed income by asking what each system lets a hostile actor do and how those affected could stop that actor. Its appendices separate supported claims from hypothesis and name limits of the evidence.

The recurring contradiction

1. Antinomies are not accidents. The opening chapter asks who defines an ideal outcome and who controls the mechanism used to reach it. Power may change form rather than disappear. The proposed test is deliberately adversarial: a system should be judged not only under a benevolent leader, but under one who exploits its weaknesses. A design that works only when a good person rules is fragile. Full chapter

2. Equality confiscated. Taking away private fortunes does not remove decisions about housing, publishing, promotion, and exit. Those decisions may gather in an administrative hierarchy, creating a nobility without titles. The chapter asks how to detect and contest privilege when access and appointments replace visible wealth as the currency of command. Full chapter

3. Freedom sells its own foundations. Competitive markets can open opportunities to outsiders. Success also gives winners resources with which to close entry, influence rules, and make others dependent. Elections and rights remain valuable, but the chapter asks how economic power can empty them of effect. It treats the concentration of control as a mechanism to examine, not proof that exchange itself is the enemy. Full chapter

4. Censorship without a censor. Legal speech can remain free while attention becomes scarce and controlled. Flooding public discussion with noise, or deciding distribution through platforms, may make important arguments nearly invisible without banning them. Being unheard is not automatically censorship; the political concern is systematic control over what can be heard and judged. Full chapter

5. The mandarins will not save us. Expertise is needed for competent decisions, yet experts who certify and protect their own authority can become an unaccountable caste. Popular participation alone does not make a crowd infallible. The chapter calls for a discipline of explanation, answerability, and the possibility of losing control over evaluation. Full chapter

6. Resentment as raw material. A society can expand education and ambition faster than it expands desirable positions. Some people then seek fairer rules; others may seek domination as compensation. Material deprivation matters, but not every status grievance can be solved by income redistribution. The chapter asks how an order can offer multiple routes to contribution and respect while resisting political entrepreneurs who weaponize resentment. Full chapter

Orders that promise an escape

7. Liberalism does not reproduce itself. Rights require people and institutions able to sustain them across generations. The chapter discusses family formation, education, demographics, environment, and contested evidence about cognitive traits. It rejects a simple hereditary civic destiny and also rejects the assumption that responsible, free adults will form themselves automatically. The problem is institutional reproduction, with no warrant for ranking people by ancestry. Full chapter

8. Chinese order and obedience. China’s economic transformation is a serious fact for critics of liberal democracy to address. It does not prove that every institution of one-party rule caused the outcome or that authoritarian order is generally superior. The chapter compares performance with correction costs, information that fails to reach leaders, and the risks of concentrated power. Full chapter

9. Cosmolocalism under force. Sharing designs globally while making more decisions and goods locally could distribute productive capacity. Local communities, however, can also dominate insiders or face armed outsiders. The chapter asks who protects dissenters, settles cross-community disputes, and supplies defense. The appeal of decentralization survives only if its remaining centers of power are specified and constrained. Full chapter

10. Feudalism with terms and conditions. Governing a city like a company promises measurable service and an exit option for dissatisfied customers. A citizen cannot always move, and a sovereign owner has powers a provider should not have. The chapter distinguishes consumer choice from enforceable civic rights and asks whether the proposed alternative to a captured state writes capture into its founding rules. Full chapter

Automation, provision, and dependence

11. AI and the manufacture of dependence. AI may lower the cost of expertise for small groups while enlarging the power of providers that own infrastructure, data, and access. Widespread use is not the same as widespread control. The chapter asks whether people can retain their data, change tools, own productive assets, and challenge decisions without being materially excluded. Its four future scenarios are possibilities, not quantified predictions. Full chapter

12. Guaranteed income or a leash? A periodic unconditional payment may reduce insecurity; it is not a complete constitution for a free society. Benefits can become a means of control if administrators can withdraw them while citizens lack independent capabilities. The chapter reviews evidence from income experiments carefully and considers services, ownership, social dividends, and shorter work as alternative or complementary arrangements. The decisive question is what a person can refuse without facing ruin. Full chapter

13. Well-fed people are not reconciled people. Work can provide rhythm, contact, recognition, and usefulness as well as pay; it can also bring humiliation and exhaustion. If automation reduces demand for labor, income alone will not answer questions of purpose and standing. Nor does abundance automatically produce idleness or aggression. The chapter asks what institutions and forms of contribution could support adult autonomy rather than comfortable dependence. Full chapter

Building the right to refuse

14. Virtues that produce their opposites. Transparency can aid oversight and expose vulnerable people. Tolerance can protect disagreement and leave room for groups that would abolish it. Safety can paralyze useful action; innovation can impose risks on others. The chapter applies the same scrutiny to its own argument: a movement against domination can become a new oligarchy if it exempts itself from constraints. Full chapter

15. Counterpower, not goodwill. Reducing the state does not by itself restrain private coercion; socializing ownership does not identify who controls administrators; elections alone may not counter concentrated resources. The chapter proposes testable counterpowers: rights to refuse, independent oversight, separable functions, and limits on irreversible actions. It does not claim to have proved a perfect constitution. The goal is to make it difficult to become indispensable or buy every route of challenge. Full chapter

16. How to build the power to refuse. A transition can concentrate more power than the order it promises to replace. The final chapter calls for pilots that can fail honorably, for replacement capacity before dismantling existing systems, and for people able to defend limits when those limits become inconvenient. The epilogue leaves the future with citizens who can stop institutions when they will not correct themselves. Full chapter
