Prologue. Four People Before the Same Door

In an imaginary scene, four people stop before a door bearing the words: “Admission is free.” The first has time, money, and confidence. The second uses a wheelchair, and beyond the door a staircase begins. The third is a child who has been told they can choose anything, provided they choose what the adults want. The fourth could enter, but knows that the person they depend on will demand an account of every minute spent there.

The words on the door do not necessarily lie. Nobody asks them for a ticket. Yet they do not tell the whole truth. The four do not have the same freedom, although they receive the same permission.

Now we change the scene. Inside, someone collapses. The first visitor could call for help. The second could open up access if someone removed the obstacle. The child is frightened. The last looks at the clock: if they are late, they may lose something essential. Suddenly, the question is no longer only who can enter. It is who must stay.

Here freedom and responsibility begin to become entangled. Can we recognize everyone's right to direct their own life without accepting a right to abandon others in every circumstance? Can we ask for help without turning all existence into an impossible debt? Can we hold someone responsible for an act without declaring them the absolute author of all the conditions that brought them there?

Part of the difficulty comes from how we use the same words for different problems. “They are free” may mean that they are not imprisoned, are not threatened, have the means, understand their choice, or do not depend on someone's arbitrary goodwill. “They are responsible” may mean that they caused something, deserve reproach, hold an office, or can remedy a harm they did not cause. We move between these meanings without noticing, then wonder why our arguments fail to meet.

The book begins with a thesis that will be defended but also tested: freedom deserves protection not because we are beings without dependencies, but because we are vulnerable beings capable of living under others' power. Responsibility deserves preservation not because each person created their own destiny, but because our actions, institutions, and omissions change destinies that no one can control alone.

This thesis does not resolve conflicts in advance. Sometimes an obligation really does limit a freedom. Sometimes one person's freedom is defended by limiting another's power. At other times, two people can become freer together. We will not call every well-intentioned constraint “true freedom,” because such a definition would make recognizing abuse impossible.

We will move from the child who breaks a cup to the researcher who opens up a dangerous possibility, from the person without housing to the citizen who obeys an unjust law, from the person unable to formulate an argument to the question of whether an omnipotent creator would have obligations toward his own creation. At every step a less solemn but often more revealing question will return: who gets the options, and who gets the bill?

Scenes without historical attribution are thought experiments, not documentary accounts. Studies are used for what they can show, not as verdicts on human nature. Numbered references point to the final bibliography. Our own arguments remain arguments: readers are invited not to believe them on authority, but to see where they hold and where they can be challenged.

PART I · THE WORD AND ITS HISTORY

1. An Open Door Is Not Enough

Suppose someone is locked in a room but does not try to leave. They are reading an interesting book and would have stayed anyway. Are they free? From within their experience, nothing seems missing. From outside, we know the option of leaving has been removed. The example separates two things we easily confuse: satisfying a present desire and having alternatives.

Now suppose the door is unlocked, but the building's owner can close it at any time without explanation. Today the owner is kind. Tomorrow they may not be. Does our person enjoy permission, or a freedom they can rely on? Finally, let us leave the door permanently open but place the room above a chasm, without stairs. There is no longer a prohibition. Nor a practicable exit.

The distinction popularized by Isaiah Berlin between negative and positive freedom organizes some of these problems. The first concerns the absence of others' interference in a sphere of action; the second concerns self-government, the possibility of being one's own master. They are not synonyms for “bad freedom” and “good freedom,” and their formulations have many variants [1]. From here on, examples will help us see what each formulation leaves out.

Negative freedom is indispensable. Someone who can be arrested for an opinion gains little from the explanation that true freedom lies in the soul. Neither privacy, religious freedom, nor the right to refuse a relationship can be replaced with a personal-development program. Having a sphere in which authority must stop is a moral achievement, not an administrative detail.

But the absence of intervention does not alone answer what we can do. The capabilities approach, associated with Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum, shifts attention toward real opportunities to lead particular kinds of lives. It distinguishes what a person achieves from what they could effectively choose to achieve [2]. Two people may go without eating: one is fasting, the other has no food. The immediate outcome looks similar; the structure of possibilities does not.

It does not follow that every impossibility should be called a lack of political freedom. I cannot breathe underwater or become younger yesterday. If we put every limit of nature in the same category as imprisonment, we lose useful distinctions. The practical question is which obstacles can be changed through human action and who has the power to change them. A staircase without a ramp is not a conspiracy of gravity. It is also the result of design decisions.

The contemporary republican tradition adds freedom as nondomination: not living under arbitrary or uncontrolled power, even when that power does not constantly intervene. A benevolent master does not abolish dependency merely by behaving nicely [3]. In our example, it matters who holds the key and what prevents them from using it against the person in the room.

This perspective explains why a right can matter even when we do not exercise it. We need not divorce for the possibility of leaving a marriage to change the relationship. We need not challenge every decision for the existence of an appeal to alter authority's behavior. A credible option works even when unused.

There is also inner freedom: the ability to examine a desire, delay an impulse, and avoid immediately confusing what attracts us with what we want to become. It does not coincide with political freedom. Someone may enjoy many rights yet be caught in an addiction; another may retain dignity and clarity under oppression. But the possibility of clarity in captivity does not make captivity acceptable.

Freedom therefore cannot be adequately measured by the raw number of options. A hundred almost identical products may matter less than a single possibility of refusing treatment, changing jobs, or telling the truth without reprisals. The difference between two packaging variants cannot compensate for the disappearance of an exit from an abusive relationship.

Let us imagine two cities. In the first, there are few rules, but access to housing, work, and information depends on a handful of people who cannot be challenged. In the second, there are more rules, but people can challenge decisions, change employers, and keep their livelihoods when criticizing authority. We cannot determine which is freer by counting prohibitions alone. We must also examine private powers, the cost of refusal, and the security of rights.

Yet it would be equally wrong to assume that every rule in the second city is justified. Bureaucracy can stifle the very possibilities it claims to protect. An inaccessible, slow, or humiliating appeals procedure is not equivalent to an effective one. Promised protection must be distinguished from usable protection.

Freedom also has a temporal dimension. Today I can choose something that destroys almost all tomorrow's choices. I can promise, take on debt, or assume an obligation. Sometimes commitment itself makes a valuable project possible: a friendship, a child, long-term research. At other times it closes a trap. Respecting freedom means neither forbidding every commitment nor treating every signature as a legitimate surrender of protection.

A useful vocabulary must therefore keep the questions distinct. Am I obstructed? Do I have the means? Do I understand what I am choosing? Can I refuse without disproportionate cost? Do I depend on someone's goodwill? Can I still revise my choice? No single answer replaces all the others.

This way of thinking has an uncomfortable consequence: my freedom may be partly invisible to me because certain doors have always opened for me. Likewise, I may regard as a “personal preference” what someone else has learned to stop asking for. We need not silence their voice to notice these possibilities; we must offer conditions in which that voice can say something else too.

Before declaring someone free, then, it is worth asking: free from what, to do what, in relation to whom, and for how long? The open door is the beginning of the examination, not its result.

2. The History of Those Allowed to Be Free

A community can consider itself free while some of its members live in slavery. The contradiction is obvious to anyone starting from each person's freedom. It becomes less visible in an order that reserves full participation for only some. The first historical question is therefore not “When was freedom invented?” but “Whose freedom was at issue?”

In Politics, Aristotle distinguishes the rule of free people from the relationship between master and slave. He attempts to justify “natural” slavery and distributes authority unequally within the household. Yet the same text records the opposing view: for some, slavery is contrary to nature [4]. We must not turn an influential justification into an era's absolute consensus.

It is troubling that thought capable of moral subtlety can take what serves a social order to be nature. The lesson is not that people in the past were simply less intelligent. It is that intelligence can rationalize the dependencies from which it benefits. The same risk exists whenever the group defining the “rational,” “civilized,” or “responsible” person is also the group distributing rights.

Imagine a small town where only property owners vote. They discuss freedom sincerely, oppose a ruler's arbitrary power, and protect one another's wealth. Outside the assembly, those who serve them have different questions: can they leave, learn, keep what they earn? A history written only from the assembly's speeches would capture a truth, but not the whole truth.

In ancient civic traditions, a community's freedom and a citizen's status do not coincide with the modern idea of equal rights for all. The history of republicanism shows both concern for independence from domination and disputes about the relationship between freedom and political participation [3]. From this inheritance we can draw tools against arbitrary power without importing the exclusions that accompanied it.

A decisive change in the history of freedom occurs when excluded people use an order's declared principles against its boundaries. If freedom is valuable because we are persons, why should it stop at sex, wealth, origin, or inherited status? Sometimes a concept says more than its formulators intended. That is precisely why it can become dangerous to power.

But universalization in words is not universalization through institutions. We can proclaim everyone equal while distributing unequally the time needed for education, physical security, access to property, or credibility before an authority. These differences must be documented in each context, not assumed identical everywhere. Conceptually, however, they show that a universal principle can coexist with selective mechanisms of application.

Frederick Douglass's autobiography provides direct testimony to the link between literacy and liberation. Learning to read appears not merely as accumulating information, but as access to a language in which enslavement can be recognized and challenged. His account also reveals the violence supporting the slaveholding order [5]. It is the testimony of one life, not a statistic about every experience of slavery.

Here freedom meets knowledge ambivalently. Understanding your own powerlessness better does not necessarily make you happier immediately. You may lose the peace of adaptation before gaining the means of change. That makes the argument “they were content before someone explained it to them” morally suspect. Satisfaction can express a good life, but also a horizon kept narrow.

In 1792, Mary Wollstonecraft attacks the education that produces women's dependence and then presents the resulting dependence as evidence of their incapacity. She claims their status as rational beings and their access to an appropriate education [6]. The mechanism she criticizes has a general form: prevent a capacity from developing, then use its absence to justify the prevention.

We can recognize it in an imaginary experiment. An institution does not allow a group to make important decisions. Twenty years later, its leaders observe that the group has little decision-making experience and invoke that lack to retain control. The question is not whether experience matters; of course it can. The question is who prevented its acquisition and what fair transition is now possible.

Freedom can also be formally granted without repairing the conditions produced by unfreedom. Opening a competition does not automatically erase disadvantages accumulated before it opened. Nor does it tell us by itself what reparation would be fair. We need arguments about causation, the continuity of benefits, the limits of liability, and the costs of remedies. Precisely because these questions are difficult, they must not be replaced by the convenient formula “now everyone is allowed.”

Viewed this way, history is not a linear march from darkness to light. Freedom can increase in one domain and decrease in another. A person can become more independent of a local authority and more dependent on a distant economic mechanism. A revolution can remove one domination and create another. These are structural possibilities, not a claim that every historical transformation produces exactly the same result.

Another mistake is to judge every era only by what it could not yet formulate. People act in contexts that make some alternatives hard to imagine. Yet contextualization must not become automatic absolution. Contemporary critics, resistance, and suppressed voices often show that other judgments were possible. Understanding why someone was wrong does not mean declaring they were not wrong.

Who benefited from early conceptions of freedom? Those protected from the arbitrary power of a master, king, or majority. Who could lose? Those whose power over others was limited. But these two changes do not have the same moral value: losing the possibility of owning a person is not equivalent to losing the freedom not to be owned.

The history of freedom is largely the history of expanding and contesting the pronoun “we.” Whenever a society says “we are free,” the checking question remains simple: who must stand outside that sentence for it to seem true?

3. Liberation of the Soul and Disobedience of the Mind

Someone can be rich yet unable to bear criticism. Someone else can have very little yet refuse a humiliation that would bring advantages. These situations do not prove external goods unimportant. But they show why many traditions have also sought freedom in the relationship between the person, desire, fear, and meaning.

Epictetus opens the Handbook by separating things within our power from those outside it. Judgment and the orientation of the will occupy the center of Stoic practice, while reputation, wealth, and many external events remain uncertain [7]. This is not a program for complete emotional control by mere command, but a discipline of examining how we relate to what happens.

The power of this idea appears when we cannot immediately remove a constraint. A persecuted person may find an essential difference between being silenced and inwardly accepting the persecutor's lie. But the same idea can be abused by a superior who says: “Do not change the conditions; change your attitude.” An exercise chosen by the vulnerable is not the same as an obligation to adapt imposed by the powerful.

This distinction will recur throughout the book. A technique of resistance can become a technique of governance. The victim's courage does not absolve the aggressor. Someone's ability to find meaning in a terrible situation does not give another person the right to put them in it.

In Augustine's Christian philosophy, will, evil, and dependence on grace remain in enduring tension. The emphasis falls not only on having options but on the possibility of willing the good; in later writings, human capacity is not treated as self-sufficient. The dispute with the Pelagians shows that the relationship between freedom, guilt, and divine help was contested even within the tradition [8].

For readers who do not share the theological premises, the question remains recognizable: if someone needs help they cannot produce themselves, how far can we attribute their failure entirely to them? For believers, there is the further difficulty of how this help is distributed and how judgment and mercy can coexist. Religion does not automatically eliminate the problem of responsibility; sometimes it sharpens it.

Islamic traditions likewise cannot be reduced to a formula of destiny rendering action useless. Debates over causality, divine action, and human action include very different positions, and explanations of the natural order differ between schools [9]. The comparative lesson is not to confuse a divinity's power, its knowledge, and human participation in producing an act. These are three questions, not one.

In Indian Buddhist ethics, liberation is linked to reducing ignorance, attachment, and suffering. Criticism of a permanent self does not simply cancel the importance of intentions or compassion; different traditions explain action and personal continuity without always assuming an unchanging self [10]. For our subject, the challenge is radical: can responsibility make sense without an absolute metaphysical owner of every mental state?

We can formulate the problem without adopting the entire doctrine. Yesterday's child, today's adult, and tomorrow's older person differ profoundly, but promises, effects, and relationships connect their lives. Practical continuity can matter morally even if we do not assume a core wholly independent of causes. It does not follow that every conception of identity is equally good. It follows that “you are not an isolated substance” does not immediately mean “nothing matters.”

Confucius centers the cultivation of conduct in relationships: how people learn to behave, judge, and participate in a moral order. Ritual and roles are not merely external restrictions; they can cultivate sensitivity and character [11]. This perspective corrects the image of the adult fully constituted before any social bond.

But relationships also need criticism. Being someone's child cannot mean that every parental command is justified. Holding a role in a community cannot mean that a person exists only for that role. An interpretation of harmony that leaves no room for the wronged to complain risks confusing the authority's peace with the whole group's good.

Daoism, in its diversity, questions the rigid fixing of values and forceful intervention that tries to shape the world according to a narrow scheme. Unforced action does not reduce to laziness, and criticism of excessive control is not equivalent to indifference toward consequences [12]. It offers every reformer a useful question: how much of the harm you cause comes from the ambition to eliminate every deviation?

These traditions are not simple variants of the same doctrine. A Stoic is not a European Buddhist; Confucian thought is not a liberalism that has yet to discover its individual; theologies have no single answer concerning freedom. Comparison is fruitful precisely when it preserves differences and uses one tradition's questions to examine another's blind spots.

From their encounter we can draw a shared difficulty without claiming they would all accept the same conclusion: our desires are not automatically freedom's deepest expression. Some may be unexamined, dependent on fear, or formed under pressure. Yet who has the right to declare that they know our “true” desires better than we do?

A cautious answer calls for conditions for reflection, not an omniscient guardian. Offer time, information, support, and the possibility of encountering other lives. Do not turn someone's disagreement into evidence of moral illness. Help them reconsider, but accept that reconsideration may lead to a choice you do not prefer.

Inner freedom without external freedom can become a necessary refuge. External freedom without any capacity for reflection can remain easy to exploit. The two must support each other without either serving as an excuse to abolish the other. Someone who finds peace in captivity has not thereby signed a justification of imprisonment.

4. Modernity and the Individual Who Never Appears Alone

Imagine receiving a perfectly formulated constitution but no way to use it against those who violate it. Then imagine the opposite: a well-organized, protective community where you cannot choose another way of life. In the first case, rights are fragile; in the second, protection approaches captivity. Much of modern political philosophy can be read as an attempt not to remain trapped in either room.

Hobbes begins with the danger of insecurity and the need for a common power capable of preventing destructive conflict. In the architecture of his argument, order is not an ornament added to freedom, but a condition of a life in which promises and projects can have stability [13]. The question he leaves open to criticism is how much power we can concentrate to defend ourselves from others' power.

Security can be truly liberating. Someone constantly afraid of violence lacks the same scope for action as someone protected. But this very need can be exploited: authority can describe every opposition as danger and every check on itself as weakening protection. Assessing a power therefore means examining the mechanisms through which it can be corrected when invoking safety in its own interest.

Locke links government's legitimacy to protecting rights that are not simply the government's gift. Consent and the possibility of resisting a power that betrays its purpose play central roles. Yet interpretations of property, tacit consent, and obligations of assistance are disputed; Locke must not be reduced to the idea that property owners owe nothing to anyone [14].

The difficulty of consent remains conceptually relevant. Remaining in a country is not the same as having approved every one of its rules. Using a road is not an informed signature beneath an unlimited contract. Some obligations must be justified through fairness, rights, and the conditions of cooperation, not by retrospectively inventing an agreement nobody formulated.

Rousseau shifts the question toward the possibility of an association in which people obey a political order without simply becoming subjects of private wills. The general will concerns the common interest and is not wholly identical to the sum of preferences or to every majority decision [15]. The delicate issue is who may claim to know it and what happens to dissenters when authority speaks in their name.

This is one of emancipation's recurring temptations: “I constrain you because, deep down, this is your will too.” Sometimes people need protection from constraints they have normalized. But a regime accepting no evidence of their disagreement and no possibility of challenge does not liberate them merely by using the vocabulary of emancipation.

For Kant, moral autonomy means not following every impulse but orienting oneself by principles reason can uphold as law for rational beings. A person must not be treated merely as a means to another's ends [16]. This emphasis changes freedom's meaning: sometimes refusing what immediately benefits you expresses precisely independence from impulse.

Application nevertheless requires care. If we make sophisticated argumentative ability a condition of human worth, we expose those unable to display it. An ethics of respect must also explain protection for children, profoundly vulnerable people, and those unable to participate in the rational exchange of justifications. We will return to this difficulty without assuming that a philosopher's prestige resolves it.

Mill defends ample space for thought, discussion, and experiments in living. His principle of preventing harm to others limits the legitimate use of coercion over capable adults; the mere conviction that they would live better otherwise is insufficient. Mill also fears social tyranny, not only state tyranny [17].

This approach's merit is that it does not make freedom conditional on majority approval. The difficulty of application is that “harm” has disputed boundaries. Criticism may wound pride without constituting aggression. Private activity may create risks for third parties. A slogan cannot decide every case; we must compare the nature and severity of harm, the evidence, and the proportionality of intervention.

Marx examines another fault line: workers are legally free to sell their labor power but may lack the means to avoid selling it. This double condition appears explicitly in Capital's analysis of the buying and selling of labor power [18]. The contract can be real and the dependency real at the same time.

This criticism does not oblige us to regard every wage relationship as identical to slavery. The legal and practical differences are enormous and morally decisive. It does oblige us not to treat every formal agreement as sufficient evidence of a balanced relationship. Conversely, denouncing dependency does not prove that every system proposed to eliminate it will better protect freedom. Both economic and administrative power need checks.

In Sartre's existentialism, people cannot wholly eliminate the dimension of choice by retreating into roles and explanations. Saying “that is how things are done” or “that is my function” does not settle the question of what I am doing. Yet freedom is lived within a situation, not an obstacle-free vacuum [19]. Read without this precaution, the rhetoric of radical freedom can slide toward unjustly blaming the constrained.

In more recent political philosophy, Rawls asks which principles can fairly organize cooperation, while libertarianism foregrounds individual rights and the limits of coercion. The dispute includes property, transfers, unearned advantages, and the distribution of opportunities. Libertarianism itself is not a single doctrine: significant differences exist between right- and left-wing variants [20, 21].

These philosophies must not become a contest in which one author supplies every answer. Hobbes obliges us to take insecurity seriously; Locke, limited power; Rousseau, self-government; Kant, respect; Mill, diversity; Marx, material dependence; Sartre, evasion of our own participation. But every correction can become excessive when detached from the other questions.

The modern individual does not, in fact, appear alone. Behind them lie the language in which they think, the people who raised them, the infrastructures they use, and the rules recognizing their property. Noticing these conditions does not dissolve the individual into society. It situates them. A person can have rights precisely because they depend on others and need protection within that dependency.

Perhaps political maturity begins when we abandon two symmetrical fantasies: the individual who owes nothing to the world from which they emerged, and the collectivity entitled to everything it can take from the individual. Between them lies the hard work of justification.

PART II · THE REAL HUMAN BEING, NOT THE IDEAL ONE

5. Who Chooses When the Brain Decides?

If tomorrow a device predicted your choice, would you cease to be free? The answer depends on what it predicted, how well, and how it affected the situation. Anticipating a friend's choice through knowledge of their values is not the same as controlling it. And identifying a signal preceding a finger movement is not the same as demonstrating that all important deliberation is useless.

The problem of free will concerns the relationship between action, causality, and control. Compatibilists maintain that certain relevant forms of freedom can exist even if actions have determining causes. Incompatibilists dispute this compatibility; they include both defenders of nondeterministic free will and skeptics about the freedom needed for ultimate moral desert [22]. Doctrinal names do not decide the dispute. We must ask what would count as authorship of an action.

Determinism is not fatalism. In a deterministic universe, what you do can change what happens because your action is one of the causes. Deliberating, seeking advice, or building an institution can form part of the chain producing a different result. Fatalism, in the sense relevant here, would say the outcome occurs regardless of what you do. Confusing them turns a thesis about causes into an unjustified excuse for passivity.

Chance alone does not solve the problem either. If my choice were decided by a random fluctuation, it would not become more mine for that reason. A lottery hidden in the brain is not obviously a superior form of self-government. Defenders of freedom must explain how the person contributes to action, not merely find somewhere prediction fails.

The experiments associated with Benjamin Libet became famous for the discrepancy between preparatory brain activity and the moment participants report intending a simple movement. Their interpretation remains controversial, however: retrospective reports of intention's timing, the task's arbitrary nature, and the neural signal's meaning limit metaphysical conclusions. Later literature explicitly discusses these difficulties [23]. The brain is not a stranger entering the scene before the person; it is part of the conditions of their existence.

A 2019 study by Uri Maoz and colleagues compared arbitrary choices with deliberative choices involving donations, using comparable motor responses. Among the 18 participants analyzed, the classic readiness potential did not appear in the same way before deliberative choices [23]. The result limits a popular generalization. It neither demonstrates a soul independent of the brain nor closes the free-will debate.

Consider an ordinary decision: a researcher discovers an error weakening their paper. They can conceal it or correct the results. The decision involves memory, anticipation of consequences, fear, professional norms, and self-image. Saying all these are realized through brain processes does not make them unreal. The practical question is whether the person could understand the relevant reasons, respond to them, and what pressures distorted their deliberation.

This approach is not metaphysically neutral in every respect, but neither does it claim to resolve the person's ultimate origin. We can construct an operational notion of control: the difference between an involuntary movement, an action under threat, and a careful, informed, uncoerced choice. Even those rejecting absolute moral desert can consider these differences important for protection, trust, and cooperation.

The skeptical objection remains powerful: people did not choose their genes, family, earliest experiences, or even their initial disposition to reflect. If you must be the complete author of your character to deserve punishment's suffering, the requirement seems endlessly to push authorship's origin backward. Every choice that shapes you is made by a “you” already formed to some extent.

This argument can weaken revenge's justification without eliminating the need to respond. Victims must be protected, danger may need stopping, harm may need repairing. But the aim is no longer necessarily to administer suffering because the universe demands metaphysical compensation. We can ask which measures are necessary, proportionate, and likely to reduce harm without turning the perpetrator into an object devoid of dignity.

There is a risk here too, however. If we abandon culpability and retain only the prediction of danger, we might end up restricting people for what a system assumes they will do. A humanizing view of causes does not justify unlimited management of people deemed risky. The right to challenge, evidence, proportionality, and limits on power remain essential.

Praise also needs examination. Recognizing effort, courage, or generosity does not assume that people produced every condition of their success themselves. We can admire an act and acknowledge the luck that made it possible. Admiration becomes dangerous when it turns into the conclusion that success authorizes every subsequent advantage while failure proves worthlessness.

The problem differs in close relationships. When a friend betrays you, the causal explanation may be true and insufficient. You want to know whether they understand what they did, undertake to repair it, and can be trusted again. A world without metaphysical hatred need not be a world without reproach, boundaries, or demands. Asking for an answer can treat the other as a moral participant rather than merely a mechanism.

A reasonable conclusion, therefore, is neither “free will has been proved” nor “science has demonstrated that no one is accountable.” It is more modest and productive: we must separate the will's ultimate origin from the concrete capacities through which people direct and correct their actions. The first remains disputed; the others can be investigated, supported, and sometimes severely impaired.

On the evening when you must choose whether to admit a mistake, the fact that you are not your own absolute cause does not make confession meaningless. It can become one of the causes of the person you will be tomorrow. Practical freedom need not stand outside nature to matter within it.

6. The Psychology of Will: Autonomy, Control, and Fear

“You choose for yourself,” says a teacher, and the child chooses. The teacher sighs, raises an eyebrow, and asks whether they are sure. The child changes the answer. The teacher smiles: “See? You can when you want to.” Formally, the choice was not prohibited. Psychologically, however, the system of approval showed the child which option preserves their relationship with the adult.

Not every social influence cancels autonomy. We learn from others' reactions, and their reasons can become reasons we sincerely accept. The relevant difference is between conduct we endorse and conduct maintained mainly through control, fear, or pressure. Even this difference admits degrees; life does not offer two perfectly separate drawers, one for pure actions and another for actions wholly alien to us.

Self-determination theory, formulated by Richard Ryan and Edward Deci, treats autonomy, competence, and relatedness as important psychological needs. Here autonomy means experiencing action as one's own, not absolute independence from others. Their 2000 article synthesizes the research program and distinguishes forms of motivation and internalization [24]. It is a research-supported theoretical framework, not proof that the same educational recipe works identically for everyone.

In an imaginary workplace, two teams receive the same difficult task. The first understands the purpose, can propose methods, and can identify limits. The second receives contradictory orders and is punished for asking questions. Even with equal pay and a lawful task, their experience of control can differ greatly. Applying the psychological framework suggests what to investigate; it does not permit inventing findings about teams we have not studied.

The experience of control also matters when failure repeats. In 2016, Maier and Seligman revised the classic interpretation of learned helplessness. Neuroscientific animal research suggests that under certain aversive conditions passivity is the default response, while experiencing control trains mechanisms that can counteract it [25]. We must not transfer these protocols directly to every poor, depressed, or disappointed person. They show why the relationship between effort and outcome deserves examination, not provide remote diagnoses.

Suppose every complaint in an office is ignored. After a while, employees stop complaining. Management may interpret silence as satisfaction. An alternative explanation is that people no longer believe the procedure works. To distinguish the hypotheses, we must change something observable: the response, protection from reprisals, the possibility of an outcome. “Why did they not speak up?” is incomplete without “What happened to those who did?”

Here lies an important circular relationship. Freedom to act can develop confidence in one's capacity, and that confidence can make freedom more usable. Conversely, repeated lack of control can reduce initiative, and reduced initiative can justify still greater control. Not every such cycle exists in every institution. But the mechanism is plausible enough to require investigation, not automatic moralizing.

There is also the uncomfortable freedom of uncertainty. Being able to choose sometimes means no longer being able to attribute the whole outcome to an order. People may prefer someone else to decide, not because they are generally incapable, but because they are tired, overwhelmed, or consider the choice insufficiently important. Voluntary delegation can be an exercise of freedom, not necessarily its surrender.

But delegation needs conditions. I must be able to ask the person deciding for me for explanations; I must know what I entrusted to them and be able to revise their mandate. Someone letting a friend choose the restaurant has not thereby surrendered the right to decide where to live. Likewise, an expert's competence does not create unlimited authority over a person's ends.

Will must not be confused with productivity. Someone refusing an imposed objective may be autonomous, not lazy. Someone producing enormously may be driven by fear. From outside, we see conduct and invent the inner story too quickly. Serious assessment asks what reasons are accessible to the person, how they understand them, and under what conditions they can say no.

At the same time, authenticity does not mean the absence of every obligation. I can get up at night to care for a child without wanting to wake at that moment. The action may still express a commitment I recognize as mine. The difference between immediate desire and an endorsed project prevents us from defining freedom exclusively as present pleasure.

The problem becomes harder when the project was formed under pressure. Someone may sincerely say they do not need freedoms they were never allowed to explore. We have no right automatically to declare them incapable of judgment. But we can ask whether they have access to alternatives, whether disagreement would be punished, and whether the preference survives in a less coercive setting. Respecting a voice and examining its conditions do not exclude each other.

A society concerned with autonomy should not demand that people choose constantly. It should help distinguish important choices from noise, offer intelligible information, and preserve the possibility of correction. A form with thirty checkboxes does not, through complexity, demonstrate respect for the person. It may merely transfer the cost of poor organization to them.

Ultimately, the relevant psychological question is not whether we feel free every moment. Feeling can mislead in either direction. It is whether conditions exist for forming intentions, examining them, learning from effects, and changing what we do without every deviation threatening the bonds on which we depend. Sometimes freedom's first sign is not enthusiasm, but being able to make a mistake without losing your place in the world.

7. Poverty, Luck, and the Factory of Choices

Two people delay paying a bill. One forgot. The other bought food. The observable behavior is the same, but assessing it requires different information. When we call the second “irresponsible” without knowing their alternatives, we do more than describe an act. We import the budget and safety margin of our own life into theirs.

Poverty can restrict possibilities directly through lack of resources, and indirectly through how emergencies occupy attention. In 2013, Mani, Mullainathan, Shafir, and Zhao investigated the link between financial difficulties and cognitive-task performance. They used both financial scenarios and comparisons of the same sugarcane farmers before and after harvest, reporting poorer performance under financial pressure [26]. The results do not describe a fixed intelligence of “the poor” or authorize individual diagnosis.

It is important to see the limits too. A seasonal comparison is not identical to an experiment changing only income while holding an entire life constant. Nor does financial pressure necessarily produce the same effect in every situation. In 2016, Carvalho, Meier, and Wang studied low-income households, randomly assigning interview timing before or after payday. They found differences in some monetary choices, but not in cognitive function, risk-taking, or measured decision quality [27]. This is not an exact duplicate of the earlier study; it is evidence that we should not universalize the mechanism.

The cautious conclusion is more useful than a slogan. Resources and context can change decisions; the effect depends on the situation and the measure. We have no scientific license to declare that poverty cancels agency. We have reasons not to treat every choice as though made in a laboratory of comfort.

One advantage of material security is the possibility of making mistakes. Those with savings can refuse a poor offer, wait for a reply, or pay to repair an error. For someone else, the same error can close a sequence of options. In this sense, resources buy more than goods. They buy reversibility.

Imagine two young people attempting a project. The first lives rent-free in the family home; the second pays rent alone and supports a relative. Both work seriously. If the project fails, the first can call it experience; the second may lose their home. It does not follow that the first deserves no credit. It follows that risk cannot be adequately measured merely by the activity's name.

The same caution applies to success. Bertrand and Mullainathan's research, published in 2004, sent fictitious résumés to job advertisements in Boston and Chicago, randomly varying names perceived as associated with racial categories. Names perceived as white received approximately 50% more callbacks [28]. This is a relative difference, not 50 percentage points, and concerns that historical experiment, not every labor market today.

The study reveals a mechanism through which assessments of opportunity can depend on something other than the skills presented. It does not demonstrate that every professional failure results from discrimination. Between denying the mechanism and explaining all outcomes through it lies the space of serious research: what varies, in which context, and with what consequences?

Another important program, Moving to Opportunity, allowed examination of the effects of a randomly assigned offer of assistance to move to lower-poverty neighborhoods. Chetty, Hendren, and Katz's analysis found later benefits for children moving before age 13, while effects for older adolescents differed and were sometimes slightly negative [29]. The offer was randomized; actually moving must not be confused with random assignment. The results highlight the role of exposure duration and disruption of existing relationships.

Environment matters, but not as uniform magic. Removing a child from a place can open opportunities and break bonds. Any policy inspired by such findings must examine age, context, family choice, and necessary support. “Environment influences life” does not mean “we know in advance where everyone should live.”

Luck complicates morality in a way the language of merit often conceals. We do not choose our starting point, or everyone we meet, illnesses avoided, accidents that never happened, or the moments when our work becomes sought after. Philosophy calls situations in which assessments of a person seem influenced by factors beyond their control “moral luck” [30]. This does not prove effort nonexistent. It proves that outcomes do not measure effort in a pure state.

Why, then, do we like to believe everyone receives exactly what they deserve? Without claiming a universal psychological explanation, we can observe the idea's justificatory function. For winners, it turns advantage into a moral certificate. For institutions, it can reduce the obligation to investigate their own mechanisms. For losers, it can turn an unfavorable context into a shameful identity.

There is an opposite danger too: describing people solely through the conditions limiting them can deprive them of all authorship. Poor people then become objects of an expert explanation rather than partners in conversation. We must be able to say simultaneously that a choice was difficult and remains a choice, that an act can be criticized and that the person's entire life cannot be deduced from it.

A fair allocation of responsibility should distinguish being able to contribute from being able to guarantee the outcome. Pupils can work hard without controlling school quality. Employees can be careful without controlling an impossible production pace. Citizens can be informed without controlling the public agenda. Asking for a contribution is one thing; transferring responsibility for the whole system onto the person is another.

To assess real freedom, we must ask not only what people chose but what other choices would have cost. What did refusal cost? Waiting? A mistake? Two lives can contain the same verbs and profoundly different possibilities. The words “they chose” do not exempt us from examining the sentence that follows.

8. Responsibility Is Not One Single Thing

A tree falls on a car. A driver destroys a car through carelessness. A mechanic fails to check a problem they should have noticed. A neighbor sees the accident and can call for help. All these situations involve causes, but not the same kind of responsibility. The tree can be a cause without being guilty; the neighbor can have an obligation without being a cause.

The philosophy of moral responsibility examines the conditions under which an act can be attributed to a person, when blame or praise is appropriate, and the roles of control, knowledge, and human relationships [31]. For our discussion it is useful to separate several questions without claiming a definitive classification accepted by every school.

Causal responsibility concerns contributing to an outcome. Role responsibility concerns what falls to a parent, doctor, administrator, or researcher through their position. Retrospective moral responsibility concerns how we assess what someone did. Prospective responsibility concerns what must be done from now on. Legal liability depends on specific institutional rules and procedures and does not automatically coincide with moral judgment.

These differences change how we discuss suffering. If I did not cause an accident, I may not be culpable for it. But if I can save someone without serious risk, absence of guilt for the accident is not a sufficient answer to whether I should intervene. Conversely, a professional's involvement in repairing harm does not prove they caused it.

Already in Aristotle we find distinctions between voluntary, involuntary, and circumstantially pressured actions. The example of goods thrown overboard during a storm shows some choices' mixed character: nobody would want them in themselves, yet they are chosen for a purpose in a concrete situation [32]. Between full freedom and absolute coercion lie many morally relevant cases.

Imagine an employee signing off on an unsafe procedure because otherwise she will lose her job. The threat does not make her signature nonexistent, but changes its assessment. If the risk to others is enormous and alternatives exist, the obligation to refuse may remain strong. If alternatives are very costly and decision-making power very small, blame must account for that. The manager who created the dilemma cannot disappear from the analysis.

Ignorance requires equally careful examination. Did you not know because information was inaccessible? Because you had no reason to look? Because you deliberately avoided finding out? Because the institution fragmented information so nobody could see the whole? Lack of knowledge can excuse, mitigate, or itself form part of the wrongdoing. It depends on the obligation and possibility of finding out.

Foreseeability is not omniscience either. Researchers cannot anticipate every use two centuries hence. Parents cannot prevent every accident. But both may have an obligation to take sufficiently plausible and important risks seriously. Demanding anticipation of every consequence would make all action impossible; demanding only avoidance of absolutely certain harm would leave too much preventable harm unexamined.

Outcome luck sets a trap. Two drivers are equally careless; a pedestrian appears in front of only one. The actual harm differs and requires a different response. Yet the difference in outcome does not automatically reflect an equal difference in culpability [30]. Distinguishing culpability from the need for reparation lets us recognize the victim without pretending that chance retrospectively changed the perpetrator's entire intention.

For analysis we propose a framework, not an experimentally validated formula: an obligation's extent should be examined through control, knowledge, role, contribution to risk, and capacity to remedy. Power and benefit can create additional reasons to contribute, but must not automatically become evidence of guilt. Neither wealth nor poverty is a sufficient moral verdict.

The framework has an important limit. If we asked only the most capable to help, competence could become a sentence to endless work. If we asked only the guilty, victims would lack support when the guilty were absent or incapable. We need two stages: resolving the emergency and subsequently distributing costs fairly. The first must not be postponed until the second becomes perfect.

Collective responsibility does not mean every group member is equally guilty for everything the group does. It means some effects arise through cooperation, rules, and role distribution, and their remedy requires coordination. An institution can have mechanisms and obligations not simply reducible to one individual's intention [33]. Decision points must nevertheless be identified, rather than invoking “the system” as a fog concealing everyone.

When we say everyone is responsible, we may express solidarity or manufacture impunity. If no one has clear tasks, deadlines, resources, and an obligation to answer, universality becomes emptiness. A nominally responsible person without power is not the solution; they are the possible scapegoat for future failure.

A mature practice of responsibility must allow acknowledgment of mistakes without completely destroying the person. Otherwise, every admission becomes too costly and concealment too tempting. This means neither impunity nor compulsory forgiveness. It means distinguishing victim protection, truth, reparation, sanction, and the possibility of change instead of compressing them into a single act of condemnation.

Freedom without responsibility can become permission to transfer costs. Responsibility without freedom can become an obligation to pay for what you could not decide. A just institution should hold these two truths together and always notice when they separate: authority above, blame below; private benefit, reparation left to others.

PART III · FREEDOM IN UNEQUAL LIVES

9. The Child: The Right to Learn to Be Free

A child breaks a cup and says it was not intentional. The adult replies that they must learn responsibility. So far, the conversation may be reasonable. But what exactly must they learn? To be careful? To repair what they can? To tell the truth? Or to believe every accident proves they are a bad child?

The answer changes education's meaning. Teaching responsibility can develop the ability to see consequences and participate in repairing them. Sweeping blame can turn an act into an identity. We need not choose between no limits and humiliation. We can stop behavior without declaring the person worthless.

Children are neither adults' property nor small adults with every capacity already formed. The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child links parental guidance to evolving capacities, while Article 12 recognizes the right of children capable of forming views to be heard in matters affecting them, with due weight given to age and maturity [34]. This is an international normative reference point, not a description of how every family or country applies it.

Being heard does not mean deciding everything alone. A child may have genuine reasons against changing schools without being able to assess every family constraint. Parents can decide otherwise while still taking those reasons seriously. The difference between authority and arbitrariness appears in explanation, proportionality, and willingness to correct the decision, not only in the final outcome.

Children's freedom must be viewed along two axes. There is the present person, with joys, fears, preferences, and a need for dignity. There is also the future adult, whose possibilities are affected by today's education. Protecting only the future can turn childhood into a construction site with no life of its own. Protecting only immediate preference can close possibilities children do not yet understand.

Imagine a little girl who does not want to learn to read because the exercises are frustrating. Saying “it is her choice” may sound respectful but could abandon an educational responsibility. Coercing her through shame may obtain the behavior while damaging her relationship with learning. A third path tries to change the difficulty, pace, and support while retaining and explaining the objective. This is a reasoned pedagogical proposal, not a guarantee of identical results for every child.

Psychology offers useful warnings against hasty verdicts. In an experiment by Celeste Kidd and colleagues, waiting for a larger reward was influenced by the immediate experience of an adult's reliability. Children encountering an environment presented as more reliable waited longer in the food-reward task [35]. A small sample and laboratory context do not describe all of childhood, but show that waiting can involve a judgment about promises, not just self-control.

In 2018, Watts, Duncan, and Quan reexamined the relationship between delaying gratification and later outcomes. Associations decreased considerably after accounting for differences in family environment and early abilities [36]. It does not follow that perseverance is irrelevant. It follows that a test cannot be turned without caution into a prophecy about character and destiny.

This requires us to reformulate adults' responsibility. Asking children for patience is insufficient; we must also ask how credible our promises are. Asking them to plan is insufficient; a minimum of stability must make planning intelligible. Children learn more than the declared rule. They learn the relationship between powerful people's words and behavior.

Responsibility should grow alongside the power to participate. With adult help, children can gather the safe pieces of a broken object, contribute to repairing a relationship, and suggest how to avoid repetition. They must not carry the family's financial anxiety or mediate parental conflicts. Calling them “mature” for taking on disproportionate burdens may conceal others' withdrawal from their roles.

There is a difference between trust and transferring risk. Trust says: “I give you an appropriate task and remain available.” Transfer says: “I declare you capable so I no longer have to answer.” The difference appears most clearly when something goes wrong. Who stays beside the child then, and who retrospectively invokes the freedom they claim to have offered?

Protection must not be idealized either. Parents can eliminate every small risk and thereby prevent practice in choosing. Adolescents never allowed to decide, negotiate, or face proportionate consequences will have few opportunities to learn these things. Justifiable risk depends on severity, reversibility, support, and competence; it cannot be reduced to a magical age valid for every decision.

Children's rights are not a reward for good behavior. Food, protection, belonging, and respect must not be withdrawn to produce obedience. Some privileges can depend on behavior without turning fundamental security into a bargaining chip. Otherwise children learn that love is a disciplinary institution whose regulations change with the adult's mood.

What happens when a child hurts another child? The perpetrator's vulnerability does not erase the victim's. We must stop the harm and protect the affected person, then analyze what understanding and reparation are possible. Empathy for the one who erred cannot become the victim's obligation to endure repetition. Here the advantage of separating protection, guilt, and learning becomes clear.

Education for freedom does not produce a child who is always right. At best, it produces a future adult who can give reasons, hear others' reasons, and acknowledge an error without feeling they cease to exist. Their freedom begins when “why?” is no longer automatically treated as an offense against authority.

10. The Homeless Person: The Freedom to Have Nowhere to Go

A person sleeps on a bench. Someone says: “At least they are free. No loan repayments, boss, or schedule.” The image seems generous, almost romantic. But it omits the decisive question: can that person choose not to sleep on the bench?

Lacking an obligation is not always an advantage. Having no employment contract can mean independence or lack of access to income. Paying no rent can mean ownership or absence of a home. Having no schedule can mean available time or a day consumed by searching for the minimum conditions for the next day. The same “has no” can describe freedom or deprivation.

Labels must also be separated. A person without housing is not by definition a beggar, and someone asking for money may have some shelter. Neither category alone tells us the person's character, abilities, or history. Describing the situation is more accurate than compressing the person into a noun claiming to explain everything.

Let us construct Andrei's imaginary case. He lost a job, a relationship, and then his home. We do not assume this is everyone's typical story of homelessness. We use it to trace a mechanism: lacking a stable address, somewhere safe for documents, and rest can make opportunities existing in principle harder to use. A door open in the morning does not help equally someone who slept safely and someone who guarded their belongings all night.

In the language of capabilities, a resource is not identical to the effective possibility of turning it into a chosen life [2]. A job advertisement represents a formal possibility; access to hygiene, transport, and stability can distinguish formal possibility from a practicable application. Observing these conditions does not prove employment the appropriate immediate solution for everyone. It shows what must be examined before blame.

But what if Andrei also made bad choices? Refused help, lied, or spent recklessly? We need not falsify his biography to recognize his dignity. Someone can need support and deserve criticism for an act. The right to basic protection should not depend on an error-free moral biography; otherwise almost no one would pass the test.

It does not follow that every request must be met exactly as formulated. Donors may have insufficient information, limited resources, or reasons to prefer an organization they trust. Institutions may have obligations to prevent abuse and protect other beneficiaries. But these choices must be justified by effects and respect for people, not the pleasure of policing them morally.

Assistance can contain a relationship of domination. “I offer you food, but you must adopt my faith.” “I offer shelter, but you may not criticize the conditions.” “I help you, but will publish your image to show how good I am.” Even if the immediate need is met, the price may be submission. Discussing that price does not disparage generosity; it separates generosity from purchasing vulnerability.

On the other hand, every collective service needs rules. Shelters must protect people from violence, and their administrators face real limits. The difference between rules and arbitrariness concerns purpose, proportionality, consistent application, and the possibility of challenge. A safety rule must not be confused with a rule of humiliation; nor does the existence of abusive rules prove all rules abusive.

A homeless person's freedom includes a right benefactors may find difficult to accept: not to be grateful in the expected form. Assistance does not buy emotional obedience. You can set limits on aggressive behavior without making respect conditional on a smile. Recipients do not become compulsory actors in a moral story about the giver.

The question of social responsibility begins where individual stories are insufficient. How are the risks of losing housing distributed? What services exist? What barriers do access rules produce? What happens to people who do not fit the standard administrative profile? Every city needs its own data. Prescribing a universal solution solely from moral intuition is not serious.

We can nevertheless formulate criteria for evaluating solutions. An intervention should be monitored through housing stability, safety, the possibility of deciding, and effects on life, not merely through people's disappearance from visible places. Moving suffering out of the city center is not identical to reducing it. Nor does the number of completed forms alone measure regained autonomy.

A real conflict exists over public space. Residents, workers, children, and homeless people have interests that can overlap or collide. Treating it as a duel between good and bad people is an abusive simplification. But a fair policy cannot resolve conflict by requiring one category to stop existing anywhere. Every exclusion rule must face the question of where an alternative is possible.

What responsibility does Andrei have? As far as his capacity and circumstances allow, not to harm others, to participate in reasonable agreements, and to answer for specific actions. What responsibility cannot we infer? That he alone authored his condition or must demonstrate moral superiority to receive support. What responsibility do others have? To examine their own contributions, possibilities of assistance, and rules that could make escape from precarity more accessible.

Perhaps the most honest test of a city's freedom is not what its richest person can buy, but what its most dependent person can refuse without losing the basic means to carry on. The park bench is not a symbol of freedom from obligations. It may be where society left its obligations unspecified.

11. The Worker and the Contract They Can Sign but Cannot Rewrite

“Nobody forces you to work here.” This can be true in an important sense: employees are not the company's property and can leave. But it can also halt every discussion of conditions. Freedom to exit is essential; the question is how usable it is and whether it can replace every other protection.

Imagine a worker noticing a safety problem. The supervisor orders them to continue because delivery cannot be delayed. The worker knows the risk but controls neither budget, pace, nor procedure. If an accident occurs, the institution may suddenly discover how “responsible” the person with the least power to change the situation should have been.

In this scene, responsibility has been separated from authority. A fairer allocation asks who set objectives, ignored signals, could stop the process, and would have borne the cost of stopping. Employees do not become obligation-free, but their obligations do not absorb management's. Signing that you were informed does not prove you also received the power to act on that information.

The Marxian critique of selling labor power draws attention to the difference between contractual legal freedom and dependence on access to the means of existence [18]. Applied cautiously, it helps avoid two errors: denying the importance of a freely entered contract and assuming its existence concludes the analysis of power.

Not every dependency is domination. Team members depend on one another to accomplish something they could not produce separately. Companies need coordination, and every decision cannot be constantly renegotiated. The issue is whether authority is limited, justifiable, and contestable, or whether one person must live at another's whim. The republican perspective makes this conceptual difference visible [3].

At least three possibilities matter: being able to leave, to be heard, and to have protections independent of the boss's liking. Leaving without an alternative may be too costly. Voice without protection may be dangerous. Protection without enforcement may be decorative. None alone resolves the entire relationship.

Where collective bargaining, representation, or cooperation among workers are proposed as remedies, the argument is that together people can gain bargaining power they lack individually. But representative organizations must also answer to their members. An intermediary becoming impossible to challenge can reproduce part of the problem it was meant to solve. This is a normative condition of representation, not a verdict on every actual organization.

Employers' freedom matters too. Initiative, investment, experimentation, and the possibility of organizing an activity are important forms of action. A poorly designed rule can eliminate opportunities, favor firms able to afford compliance, and prevent new entrants. Protecting workers therefore requires assessing effects, not merely accumulating procedures.

Yet producing value does not give an activity a moral blank check. Economic benefit and risk-taking do not turn employees into extensions of the owner. Nor does the difficulty of running a company justify transferring all uncertainty to those with the smallest reserves. Fairness means not that nobody ever loses, but that losses are not distributed through uncontrollable power.

A subtler case appears when work becomes a total identity. “We are a family,” says the organization, demanding emotional availability and sacrifices. Families may involve obligations not calculated contractually; companies nevertheless involve exchanges, power, and the possibility of dismissal. The metaphor can express authentic care but also conceal a demand for loyalty the institution does not return in the same form.

Hence the importance of the right to a life not absorbed by one's role. Employees can be competent without turning every passion into productivity. They can reject professional ambition without lacking character. Economic freedom is not only the possibility of working more; it is also a space in which people need not justify their time through usefulness to someone else.

Responsibility for work quality remains real. Professionals cannot invoke any pressure to justify any negligence. Clients, patients, colleagues, or the public may depend on them. But demands must relate to resources. If you ask someone to check a hundred things in the time needed to check ten, setting a standard has not created excellence; it has created a hidden choice about what will remain unchecked.

A responsible organization should make such tradeoffs visible. Those deciding the pace should also see the risk. Those demanding a signature should also provide information. Those assigning a task should specify what can be stopped and how. These are not magical solutions but ways to prevent the person at the chain's end from becoming a universal absorber of failure.

Who benefits from the language of contractual freedom? Workers who cannot be forcibly retained and entrepreneurs who can initiate activities. Who can benefit from its incomplete use? Actors presenting every imbalance as equal choice. Who benefits from responsibility? The beneficiaries of work well done. Who can lose? Those assigned accountability without authority, time, or means.

A good contract should not replace freedom with paternalism. It should enable cooperation in which refusal, criticism, and personal limits are not treated as character defects. The final question is not only whether workers can leave. It is whether they can stay without being forced to give themselves up.

12. The Citizen: When Obedience Becomes Irresponsibility

An official applies a rule they consider profoundly unjust. They did not invent it. They are not its only enforcer. If they refuse, someone else may replace them. Are they responsible? One convenient formula says responsibility always rises to the order's author. Another, equally convenient, says every subordinate is entirely free to refuse. Neither sufficiently examines the situation.

Citizenship involves participation in a common order, but participation is not identical to permanent approval. We can have obligations to follow fair rules without considering every law moral. In the contractual tradition, the justification and limits of power themselves permit discussion of resistance when government betrays its purpose [14]. The practical issue remains which opposition is justified and at what cost to others.

Disobedience does not become good merely because it is disobedience. It can defend the wronged, but also serve privilege or self-interest. Serious justification must show the injustice's nature, alternatives tried, proportionality of means, and protection for those who did not choose the conflict. Courage without examining effects can become another form of irresponsibility.

Yet the demand to exhaust every procedure can indefinitely postpone all change. If a procedure is designed not to respond, invoking it ceases to be neutral. An institution asking for patience must show what time does for the affected person, not merely for its own continuity. Deadlines and avenues of appeal are, in this sense, components of freedom.

Citizens' responsibility cannot mean expertise in every field. Nobody can personally check every treaty, statistic, public contract, and technology. A division of knowledge is inevitable in a complex society. The issue is how we choose whom to trust, which signals we check, and how we correct institutions demanding trust without providing reasons.

Responsible citizens can say “I do not know.” They can refrain from sharing a claim, seek an accusation's source, and distinguish evidence from a message's familiarity. But we must not confuse epistemic responsibility with permanent vigilance consuming an entire life. If the information system requires everyone to be a full-time investigator, the problem is institutional too, not merely one of personal discipline.

Freedom of expression matters precisely because people and authorities make mistakes. Mill's defense of discussion includes the value of confronting opinions in examining truth [17]. But this does not guarantee that every marketplace of messages spontaneously leads to truth. Amplification power, access to audiences, and the cost of replying must be examined separately from the right to speak.

Censorship and criticism are not identical. Neither criticism nor loss of approval automatically constitutes deprivation of freedom. Yet an intimidation campaign can make expression practically impossible even without a state prohibition. Each case requires examining means, power, consequences, and avenues of defense, not deciding solely by whether the actor is labeled public or private.

Civic responsibility sometimes includes contributing to common goods. Rules financing or protecting them can limit uses of individual resources. The argument from cooperation is not that resources never belong to individuals, but that a predictable life also depends on institutions nobody can produce alone. At the same time, invoking the common good does not legitimize every expenditure or distribution of burdens [20].

A particular problem concerns citizens benefiting from an unjust order without having created it. Benefit does not automatically prove personal culpability. But it can create a reason to contribute to change, especially when advantage continues, harm is known, and reasonable remedies exist. Distinguishing guilt from prospective responsibility prevents conversation from becoming stuck in defending biographies.

What can we ask of people under threat? Safe observers should not distribute obligations of heroism as though the risk were theirs. The cost of refusal, dependents, and likelihood of effect matter. Yet risk does not automatically absolve every collaboration, especially when people actively help produce serious harm. Here morality ceases to offer comfort: some situations remain tragic even after careful analysis.

A society should also be assessed by how much extraordinary courage it demands for decent behavior. If reporting an ordinary mistake requires risking your entire existence, a few courageous people do not save the system. The price it demands of truth indicts it.

Citizenship is neither ownership of the state nor unlimited service to it. People also have relationships, retreats, private projects, and the right not to turn every hour into mobilization. But that private space is safer when people and institutions defend its possibility. Freedom to withdraw cannot be sustained indefinitely through everyone's withdrawal.

Who benefits when citizens are called responsible? The community, if the term means informed participation and checking power. The leadership, if it means only discipline and silence. The word must be tested by its direction: does it also oblige those commanding to explain, or only those bearing decisions not to cause trouble?

Free citizens are not those who never obey. They are those who can be asked for reasons and, in turn, have a real right to demand reasons from power. When only one party must explain, responsibility has ceased to be reciprocal.

13. Those Who Cannot Decide: Dignity Without a Test

In a room, three people discuss the future of someone who does not speak. One says what would be most practical, another what would be safest, the third what the family wants. The person is present, yet the conversation proceeds as though they were absent. The first question is not whether they can sustain a philosophical argument. It is whether the others have tried hard enough to discover what they can communicate.

The expression “those who cannot think” improperly combines very different situations. Inability to speak does not mean inability to think. Not understanding a complex contract does not mean lacking preferences about housing, food, or relationships. Needing help with one decision does not mean being incapable in every domain. Moreover, some capacities vary over time; careful moral assessment does not turn a difficulty into a sentence on the whole person.

The philosophy of disability criticizes precisely the reduction of disadvantage to individual deficiency. Environment, accessibility, and how a person's testimony is received can matter alongside functional limitations. Neither a purely medical model nor the idea that every difficulty would disappear by changing society alone captures the diversity of experience [37]. A serious approach must recognize both care needs and avoidable barriers.

Article 12 of the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities affirms equal recognition of legal capacity and access to support in exercising it. It requires safeguards against abuse, respect for rights, will, and preferences, and proportionate measures subject to review [38]. Interpretation and application in difficult cases are debated; the principle must not be simplified into an assumption that nobody can ever need a decision made by others in extreme circumstances.

This book does not establish clinical or legal procedures for individual cases. The philosophical issue is what we owe the person before reaching such procedures. A minimum answer requires not confusing disagreement with incapacity, unusual communication with absence of preferences, or carers' convenience with the interests of those receiving care.

Imagine an older woman wanting a daily walk while her family fears accidents. The risk is real. But eliminating walks entirely also costs something: a valued activity and a form of choice. Analysis must compare not “risk” with “nothing” but two sets of effects. Companionship, adapted routes, or other means may preserve more of her desired life.

If the person cannot understand the relevant risk at all, the obligation to protect may strengthen. Yet incapacity in one matter does not authorize total control. Choices they can make must be preserved, and previously expressed preferences investigated where known and relevant. Uncertainty is no reason to act as though their life were a blank page on which the family can write anything.

We thus reach the distinction between a moral agent and a person toward whom we have moral obligations. Agents can, to some extent, understand and regulate conduct in relation to reasons and norms. Those unable to do so can remain beings with interests, vulnerability, and value. Being able to suffer is not the same as being able to argue why your suffering matters.

The position defended here is that fundamental protection must not be earned through cognitive performance. You need not demonstrate usefulness, economic reciprocity, or verbal talent to avoid abandonment. Theories of contract and cooperation need a justification broad enough to include those unable to negotiate mutual advantages; this difficulty is central to debates on disability and justice [37].

The argument “any of us could end up like this” is important but insufficient. It can motivate solidarity through shared vulnerability. Yet even a guarantee that I would never lose a capacity would not make the suffering of someone who does irrelevant. Their value does not depend on my probability of taking their place.

There are also cases where subjective experience is deeply uncertain. Here we must avoid both improvised certainties and automatic prolongation of every intervention merely to soothe others' consciences. Previous wishes, competent assessment, treatment burdens, and protection from outside interests become important. Concrete cases require expertise and procedures, not a verdict extracted from a page of philosophy.

What responsibility can someone unable to understand a norm have? Not the same culpability as someone understanding and deliberately violating it. But others may need to prevent dangerous behavior. Limiting risk does not require turning the protected or restrained person into someone guilty. The distinction between protection and punishment becomes essential here.

Carers must also be seen. Someone providing continuous support needs rest, income, relationships, and help. Recognizing a dependent person's dignity without recognizing their carer's life creates a false solution: it transfers the entire social duty into one home. Ethics of care illuminates precisely these relationships and the distribution of support work [39].

Yet carers' exhaustion must not become devaluation of those receiving care. The problem may be inadequate organization of assistance, not someone's “excessive existence.” Solidarity must broaden the circle supporting the relationship, not ask one party to disappear from it.

Those unable to formulate “I have a right” pose a severe test for the philosophy of freedom. If freedom matters only when eloquently claimed, it remains the privilege of those with a voice. A fairer society must hear subtle preferences, protect interests unable to defend themselves, and recognize that needing others does not mean belonging to them.

PART IV · WHAT DO WE OWE OTHERS?

14. The Suffering Stranger: What Can They Ask of Us?

A child is in danger, and you can help at little cost. You did not endanger them. You do not know them. You signed no contract with their family. Yet the intuition that you should do something seems strong. Now move the child far away and turn assistance into a contribution through an institution. What changed morally, and what changed only psychologically or practically?

Peter Singer's argument in “Famine, Affluence, and Morality” requires preventing harm when we can do so without sacrificing something comparably important. Distance and the fact that others could contribute do not simply eliminate the obligation. Subsequent debate concerns, among other things, how demanding the principle becomes and where duty ends [40]. It is a normative argument, not a result obtained by measuring the brain.

Its force comes from the inconsistency it exposes. If I accept that a minor interest of mine must yield to a nearby endangered life, I must explain why an unseen person's life should weigh radically differently. Distance can change information, effectiveness, and the causal relationship to assistance. But alone it seems no reason to diminish the person's value.

The most serious objection is not that distant suffering is imaginary. It is that needs are enormous in number. If every personal interest must yield to greater suffering, one's own life seems to become a permanent reservoir of resources for others. Holidays, art, time with friends, and almost any project can appear morally suspect luxuries. We will address this in the next chapter; an accusation of selfishness cannot dismiss it.

A defense of individual freedom can start from the distinction between not harming and being obliged to help. If every other's need creates a right over my time, in what sense am I still a separate person with my own ends? Some libertarian versions emphasize limits on coercion and rights over oneself and legitimately acquired property. This does not necessarily imply generosity has no moral value [21].

The argument matters: assistance's value does not automatically justify forcing it in every form. Morally criticizing refusal differs from building an apparatus capable of unlimited coercion. But the distinction between not harming and not helping does not alone tell us what to do in an easy-rescue case. Nor does it answer situations where our advantages depend on rules contributing to others' vulnerability.

A contractualist perspective asks whether we can justify a rule permitting refusal to those affected. Could one reasonably defend a rule allowing me to avoid a minor inconvenience while leaving someone else to suffer severely, although intervention would be safe and effective? This is not merely adding up happiness. It examines the reasons each party can invoke and their relative weight.

A care perspective begins elsewhere: life consists of dependency relationships, and noticing and responding to need is not a sentimental appendix to morality. Feminist ethical traditions have criticized care work's invisibility and the idealization of a wholly independent agent [39]. But care needs rights and fairness to avoid turning caring people into compulsory providers for every shortfall.

There can also be justification through benefit and connection. If I live in an order from which I gain and which contributes to harm, my obligation no longer resembles only philanthropy toward a stranger. Debates on global justice distinguish assistance, contribution to harmful institutions, and obligations of reform [41]. Establishing a concrete contribution requires evidence; declaring every beneficiary guilty of every worldwide harm is unfair.

These arguments can converge without becoming identical. One person may help because suffering matters, another because a rule permitting refusal would be impossible to justify, another through a caring relationship or acknowledgment of their own contribution. Institutions can sometimes operate on practical convergence even when people do not share a metaphysics.

There are reasons not to intervene immediately in the first way that occurs to us. We may harm through incompetence, encourage an abusive relationship, needlessly duplicate existing help, or ignore the person's will. Respect does not demand impulsiveness. It requires taking need seriously alongside effectiveness, consent, and the limits of our expertise. Rejecting a method is not automatically rejecting every obligation.

Suffering does not grant us general authority over someone's life. Helping pay for food does not give me a right to control their relationships. An institution offering support should not collect every intimate detail merely because the beneficiary is dependent. Good assistance must attend not only to what it offers but also to the power it creates.

Does responsibility toward suffering limit our freedom? Sometimes, yes. Accepting an obligation to help makes certain uses of time or resources no longer morally indifferent. Legitimately translating the obligation into a common rule may also involve actual coercion. Denying the cost by definition—saying only selfishness, not freedom, is limited—would be dishonest.

Yet cost alone does not decide justification. Prohibiting assault limits one possibility for aggressors to protect a much more important possibility for victims. Similarly, some support obligations can sustain conditions without which others' freedom remains nominal. The question is not whether any limit arises but whether it is justified, proportionate, and fairly distributed.

Nor should “help makes us all freer” be exaggerated. Sometimes benefits are mutual; at other times someone bears a cost without an equivalent gain. Solidarity need not be defended solely through a promise that everyone immediately benefits. Some things are owed precisely because vulnerable people cannot offer sufficient return.

The answer we defend is therefore affirmative but not unlimited: we have strong reasons to recognize obligations toward serious, preventable suffering, especially when help is accessible, effective, and inexpensive, or when we have a special role or contribution. No general right of the world to consume our entire life follows. What follows is that absence of guilt is not morality's endpoint.

Strangers need not become my friends for their pain to matter. But neither must I cease to be a person to take it seriously. The real difficulty is building a world where this encounter does not depend on the accidental sacrifice of whoever, one day, did not look away.

15. Too Responsible to Be Free?

A woman finishes her workday, cares for a parent, responds to a friend in crisis, and contributes to a cause she believes in. That evening, she reads about other suffering she could reduce. The question is no longer whether she cares. It is whether she is still allowed to stop.

A morality demanding constant attention can end up penalizing moral sensitivity itself. The indifferent retain time for themselves; the attentive always find another obligation. We do not claim every generous person ends up this way. We observe a structural problem: if responsibility grows without limit with willingness to notice it, conscientious people can become bearers of every social backlog.

Philosophy discusses this difficulty under excessive demandingness and the distinction between duty and what goes beyond it. An act can be admirable without its omission being culpable. Where to draw the boundary remains contested, especially before serious preventable suffering [40]. No short formula can eliminate every difficult case.

An apparent solution is “everyone should do their part.” It respects fairness among contributors. But what happens when others do not do their part and someone remains in danger? Can the available person say “I have met my quota” although they could intervene at little cost? The victim is not guilty of cooperation's poor distribution.

We must distinguish emergencies from permanent organization. In an emergency, there may be a strong reason to do more than my fair share. Over the long term, however, this excess must not become normal. Interveners should receive support, costs should be redistributed, and the mechanism producing the shortfall changed. Heroism can resolve a situation; it must not become a staffing plan.

Duty toward those close to us adds another complication. Is it justified to care for my child before a stranger who might benefit more from the same hour? Special relationships are not mere valueless accidents. They create trust, dependency, and promises. Yet preference for those close to us cannot justify every harm to others. The distance between natural care and unlimited privilege requires argument.

We can view our own life not only as a competing interest but as a condition for the moral agent's continuity. Rest, friendship, play, and personal projects are not necessarily tolerable leftovers after the world has been repaired. They are part of what we seek to protect for people generally. A morality demanding their abolition among the willing risks destroying in practice a good it affirms in principle.

The argument has limits. Not every consumption becomes essential because I call it a “personal project.” If huge resources serve a minor advantage while a small contribution would prevent serious harm, invoking one's own life may mask convenience. A reasonable limit must compare the need's severity, cost, effectiveness, existing obligations, and distribution of contributions.

Then comes manipulation through guilt. Someone may say: “If you do not do exactly what I ask, you do not care.” The formula erases alternatives and turns disagreement over method into a character verdict. Responsible people also need freedom to demand evidence, refuse an unsuitable role, and propose another way to help.

At the same time, we cannot turn every reproach into “manipulation.” Sometimes others rightly remind us of an obligation we would prefer to forget. The difference is not whether the message makes us uncomfortable, but whether its reasons are sound, its demand proportionate, and our person respected. Moral discomfort can be information, but is insufficient evidence in either direction.

Unequally distributed care can create a false image of independence. Someone can devote themselves entirely to a profession because another supports their domestic and relational life. Feminist ethics of care has made this work and its organization visible [39]. The conclusion is not that every relationship must be accounted for by the minute, but that some people's freedom must not be described as though it had no supporters.

Imagine a couple where one can accept any business trip while the other organizes every choice around caregiving. Sincere agreements and different preferences may exist. Yet assessing their freedom requires examining whether the agreement can be renegotiated, the work is recognized, and the alternative is practicable. Yesterday's consent must not become a lifelong sentence to a role.

A decent institution should reduce situations in which goodness and freedom enter extreme conflict. Support services, clear procedures, shared tasks, and the possibility of asking for help are proposals to be evaluated through effects and costs. They do not eliminate human dependency, but can prevent its becoming private captivity.

There is no universal number of hours or perfect moral percentage resolving every life. We can nevertheless use a checking question: could people with comparable needs, relationships, and limits sustain over the long term the demand I consider binding on myself? If not, perhaps I have discovered not decency's minimum standard but exceptional sacrifice or an organization that needs changing.

Freedom to stop is not freedom not to care. It may enable a form of care that does not exhaust itself in resentment. Mature responsibility does not mean promising everyone never to disappoint them. It means making sustainable promises, recognizing real obligations, and not concealing behind your own endurance the failure of those who should contribute alongside you.

16. The Researcher: The Right to Discover and the Obligation to Answer

A researcher sees two results on screen. The first supports the hypothesis, the second weakens it. Future funding depends on success. Nobody explicitly asks them to cheat. Yet they know which result will be welcomed enthusiastically and which will complicate the story. Research freedom is at stake not only in spectacular prohibitions, but also in the cost of saying the world did not confirm our hopes.

Researchers have responsibilities toward society, but not because they are morally superior. They have them because they produce claims and tools others may use without being able to verify the entire process themselves. Specialization creates an asymmetry: experts see risks and limits the public does not. From this asymmetry arise reasons for transparency and care, not a general right to direct others' lives.

The first responsibility is epistemic: not presenting mere hope as a result, distinguishing observation from interpretation, and enabling verification appropriate to the field. The European Code of Conduct for Research Integrity, revised by ALLEA in 2023, formulates principles of reliability, honesty, respect, and accountability, assigning tasks to institutions as well as individual researchers [42]. A code is a normative reference point; its existence does not guarantee behavior.

Does this responsibility limit some freedoms? Yes: researchers are not morally free to fabricate data for a cause they consider good. But evidential discipline also enables a deeper intellectual freedom. Without it, debate is occupied by those able to produce the most convenient appearances. Freedom to investigate does not mean immunity from demands to show a claim's origin.

The second responsibility concerns research participants. The 1979 Belmont Report centers respect for persons, beneficence, and justice, discussing consent, risks, and distribution of research burdens [43]. Scientific interest does not turn participants into available material. Nor does promised future benefit justify every cost imposed on those who did not choose it.

The third concerns foreseeable consequences of the tools created. Imagine a method that can improve medical analysis but also enable discriminatory surveillance. It does not automatically follow that the method must be banned or published without precautions. Analysis must address risk severity, accessibility of harmful use, beneficial use's value, possible restrictions, and who bears errors.

Responsibility for dual use cannot be reduced to an individual oath. Researchers can modify publication of details, seek independent assessment, or warn of limits, but funding institutions, implementing companies, and authorizing bodies have their own decisions. If every obligation is pushed onto a paper's author, others' power becomes invisible.

Nor may society demand only comfortable results from researchers. Social responsibility does not mean confirming dominant ideology, funders' agendas, or majority preferences. Sometimes the most important contribution is an unpleasant, well-supported claim. Protecting academic freedom and demanding integrity are not adversaries; both are needed so pressure does not decide truth.

Heroic rhetoric nevertheless has a risk: authors can portray every methodological criticism as persecution and every funding refusal as censorship. Not every project has a right to public resources, just as institutional success does not prove scientific value. Assessment criteria must be justified, open to criticism, and adapted to objectives without confusing popularity with truth.

Basic research presents a special difficulty. We cannot require every project to demonstrate its exact ultimate utility beforehand, because exploration involves the unknown. But the unknown does not exempt rigor: we can demand clear questions, appropriate methods, examination of hypotheses, and reporting what failed. Uncertainty of outcome and seriousness of process must be assessed separately.

Researchers may also bear responsibility for communication. If a small study becomes “proof that people are not free” in a headline, a leap has occurred between finding and narrative. If correlation becomes individual destiny, an essential limit has been lost. Good communication means not eliminating uncertainty to avoid confusing the public, but translating it into intelligible form.

This book itself must be judged by the same criterion. A philosophical example does not become fact because it convinces. A cited study does not validate every normative conclusion built around it. A claim about what we should do that uses empirical findings also needs moral premises: why the effect matters, who has rights, and what cost is acceptable. Science can inform these choices without producing every value alone.

Those paying for research have a legitimate interest in resource use, but do not acquire ownership of truth. Participants have a legitimate interest in protection, but cannot demand altered results to avoid all discomfort. Technology's beneficiaries may have reasons to help remedy its damage, even without causing every error. We have different roles, not one universal, undifferentiated obligation.

Institutions invoking responsibility should create its conditions: time for verification, data retention under relevant rules, protection for honest reporting of problems, and public correction of errors. ALLEA treats the research environment and institutional practices as parts of integrity [42]. Demanding methodological heroism in a system rewarding only victory stories is insufficient.

There is also responsibility to acknowledge the limits of one's expertise. Excellence does not make a physicist an ultimate authority in ethics. Philosophers cannot determine a treatment's efficacy through an argument's elegance. Expertise has a domain and conditions. Prestige transferred without examination can become intellectual domination.

But expertise's limits must not silence experts as citizens. Researchers can contribute to public discussion while specifying the role from which they speak. They can offer data, formulate hypotheses, and defend values. Responsibility also means not blending these activities so personal opinion appears an inevitable consequence of the laboratory.

The final answer is therefore twofold. Researchers owe society care for truth, people, and plausible consequences; society owes them conditions in which truth can be discovered and spoken. Research freedom is not a caste privilege. It is a precious public possibility, corrupted both by ideological coercion and by evasion of accountability.

17. The Philosopher: What Do We Owe Because We Can Think?

Someone spends an evening discussing whether we have obligations toward those who suffer. On leaving, they encounter a person asking for help. Does having thought more about the problem make them more responsible? Perhaps they know more arguments. Perhaps they also know more elegant ways to evade their conclusions.

The capacity to philosophize does not automatically produce virtue. It offers tools for clarification but also rationalization. An argument can discover a real inconsistency or invent a convenient exception for its author. The question is not whether thought has value but what obligations arise from possessing a tool that can change how people justify power and conduct.

The first obligation is not to exploit confusion. If a word has several meanings, say which you use. Do not begin with freedom as absence of violence and end, without warning, with freedom as personal satisfaction. Do not start from causal responsibility to assign blame, or invoke absence of blame to deny every duty to help. Clarity does not resolve every conflict, but prevents some victories obtained by changing the rules during the game.

The second obligation is to address opponents at their strongest. Defenders of limits on assistance are not necessarily indifferent to suffering. Those demanding redistribution are not necessarily hostile to freedom. Religious people are not incapable of doubt, and skeptics are not automatically immoral. Without this discipline, philosophy becomes a more sophisticated way to speak only to those approving us.

The third is not to confuse ingenuity with importance. We can invent spectacular paradoxes that make us forget ordinary but serious problems. A thought experiment's value also depends on what it illuminates. If a scenario simplifies someone until their real life becomes irrelevant, the solution's elegance may be purchased by eliminating the difficulty.

This is not a demand for every idea's immediate utility. Abstract reflection may later change the language in which we recognize problems. No philosopher can guarantee a question's effect beforehand. But they can explain why it deserves inquiry, what it assumes, and what would count as an objection. Freedom to explore must not be confused with a right to demand admiration for every obscurity.

Who can philosophize? Not only degree holders. Parents wondering whether their protection stifles a child, workers discussing an order's legitimacy, and poor people challenging how they are judged formulate philosophical questions. Academic expertise can bring history and precision, but does not monopolize the ability to notice injustice.

There is nevertheless an advantage in time available for reflection. Those able to read, compare, and write without immediate survival pressure possess a possibility others lack to the same extent. Their entire life does not thereby become a debt to repay. There is a reason for modesty and sometimes accessibility: not turning received resources into walls of prestige.

Philosophers can respond socially through teaching, writing, participating in deliberation, or constructing tools of criticism. Not everyone must choose the same path. The obligation to contribute must not eliminate diverse talents and projects. But professional freedom does not justify indifference toward uses of one's arguments when effects are plausible and near.

Responsibility for an argument differs from responsibility for every reader who distorts it. Authors cannot control every interpretation. They can avoid foreseeably dangerous ambiguities, correct uses reversing their meaning, and refuse to present fragile claims as definitive truth. Demanding more could impose impossible self-censorship; demanding less would leave communication unaccountable.

Philosophy can also become an alibi for delay. “The problem is complex” can be true and simultaneously used to avoid obvious help. We need no complete theory of justice before opening a heavy door for someone unable to open it. Some local obligations remain sound even when ultimate foundations are disputed.

Conversely, moral certainty can become an alibi against thought. “We already know what is good” can close investigation of a policy's consequences. Right intention does not guarantee method. Responsible philosophers must hold together urgency of action and willingness to correct, without using either to cancel the other.

For Sartre, retreat into a role can conceal choice and personal participation [19]. Intellectuals' roles may say: “I only formulate ideas.” Sometimes this boundary is legitimate; sometimes it conceals a contribution to legitimizing harm. We must examine what was said, in which context, with what intention and possibilities of anticipation, not distribute guilt by mere association.

Those unable to sustain complex reasoning do not owe silence to those who can. The ability to win a dispute is no title of ownership over another's life. A decent justificatory system must explain, as far as possible, to those affected why it demands something. When explanation becomes inaccessible, the need for representation and oversight grows rather than shrinks.

This book's position is that cognitive advantage creates primarily an obligation of honesty, not unlimited moral authority. Seeing more options may make some omissions harder to excuse. But nobody must become a universal savior because they can think. Philosophers too remain finite people with lives they are not obliged to consume entirely in an abstraction's name.

Perhaps philosophy's best test is not whether it leaves us doubt-free, but whether it makes our own justifications harder to fool us with. Leaving the conversation, the person encountering suffering needs more than a theory. They must recognize when theory stopped clarifying and began protecting them from answering.

18. God Before His Own Creation

Imagine a being able to create a world inhabited by creatures capable of suffering. It knows many consequences beforehand and can modify the world's rules. What responsibility does it have? Does creating the creatures give it unlimited rights over them, or instead produce a special obligation toward the vulnerability it made possible?

The question proves neither God's existence nor nonexistence. It examines the consequences of hypotheses. We must distinguish a finite god, very powerful but limited, from classical theism's God, described through attributes such as omnipotence, omniscience, and perfect goodness. Religious traditions do not all understand these attributes identically, and some conceptions of divinity do not fit at all the model of a personal agent deciding events.

For a finite god, assessment may partly resemble assessing a very powerful ruler. What did it know? What could it prevent? What risks did it create? What would intervention have cost? The greater the power and knowledge, the less available some human excuses become. But unlimited responsibility does not follow if the being has real limits acknowledged by the hypothesis.

For an omnipotent, perfectly good being, the difficulty intensifies. The problem of evil asks how evil and suffering coexist with such attributes. The literature distinguishes the logical problem, concerning compatibility, from evidential arguments asking how plausible such a God's existence remains in light of observed evil [44]. Rejecting a strict contradiction does not mean explaining every concrete suffering.

A free-will defense holds that the existence of free agents may permit moral evil. In the formulation associated with Alvin Plantinga, a defense seeks to show possible compatibility, not necessarily describe God's actual reasons. Theodicies invoking character formation and responses based on human knowledge's limits attempt other routes, each with its difficulties [44]. Here our analysis begins, rather than ends.

The first question is whose freedom is protected when evil is permitted. If aggressors exercise their will, victims may lose safety, bodily integrity, and a future. A freedom-based justification must explain not only the value of aggressors' choice but the cost imposed on victims' freedom. Otherwise “freedom” is applied selectively.

Moreover, stopping aggression does not seem to eliminate all the aggressor's moral possibilities. In human life, we can prevent an intention's execution without erasing it from the mind. Someone can desire harm and be unable to accomplish it. By analogy, the will's value alone does not immediately demonstrate the necessity of every successful evil act. Theistic defenders may invoke intervention's global effects, but these require argument, not assumption.

The second question concerns suffering not directly resulting from a human agent's choice: illness, catastrophes, organisms' vulnerability. A world of stable laws may matter for action, learning, and responsibility. Yet stability's importance does not automatically imply that precisely this level and distribution of suffering are necessary. Could other coherent rules preserve the relevant goods at lower cost? The question remains.

We cannot construct an easy proof merely by imagining a pleasanter world. We may not see every relationship between conditions, consequences, and life's possibility. But invoking complexity alone likewise does not prove the existing world optimal or justified. It shows a limit of knowledge requiring symmetrical application: we know neither all objections nor all justifications.

The third question concerns the idea that suffering builds character. Some difficulties offer opportunities for courage, solidarity, and maturation. But an opportunity is not a complete justification. Who may impose serious harm on someone to give a third person the chance to be generous? Victims cannot be reduced to the universe's teaching material.

Religious defenders may reply that life does not end with visible existence and that a broader perspective changes assessment. This is a possibility internal to some beliefs, not a fact established by this philosophical argument. Even accepting future compensation, it remains debatable whether subsequent reparation justifies every harm permitted now. In human relationships, promised compensation grants no general right to harm.

Perhaps the analogy with human relationships is mistaken? A theologian may argue that the creator–creature relationship is incomparable to that between two finite persons. The objection deserves serious attention. But if divine goodness becomes wholly detached from every intelligible meaning of care and justice, another difficulty arises: what do we actually assert when saying God is good? A difference of scale must not be confused without argument with reversing moral meaning.

Power is not legitimacy. A being's ability to punish does not prove the punishment just. Its ability to create does not prove the creature mere property. In a thought experiment, an inventor producing beings with subjective experience would have reasons for responsibility precisely because they created those beings' dependence. The analogy does not settle theology, but shows why “I made you” alone is an insufficient moral argument.

There is also the problem of divine freedom. Can a perfectly good being choose evil? If not, is it less free? The answer depends on the notion of freedom. If freedom means every logically imaginable alternative, evil's impossibility seems a limit. If it means uncoerced action according to a perfectly rational and good nature, that limit may not be interpreted as deprivation. Once again, the dispute shows why we cannot use the word without defining it [22].

Knowledge of the future adds another tension. Knowing what someone will do is not identical to causing their action. Yet supposedly infallible knowledge raises questions about an alternative future's possibility. Neither “it knows, therefore it compels” nor “it only knows, therefore no problem exists” is enough. Compatibility between knowledge, time, and freedom requires a more precise model than these formulas offer.

What practical conclusion can we draw without deciding between belief and nonbelief? If someone can reduce suffering, a possible divine justification of the world does not automatically justify inaction. Their intervention may belong to the good the tradition demands. And if God does not exist, absence of a cosmic guarantor makes suffering no less real and assistance no less important.

Likewise, someone's invocation of divine will does not automatically grant the right to control others. The assertion remains one made by a finite person in a world of interpretation and disagreement. Religious freedom must include freedom from religious coercion. Faith can motivate care; it must not substitute for publicly justifying power.

We have resolved neither God's existence nor the problem of evil here. We have identified a criterion surviving a change of scale: possessing power over vulnerable beings does not eliminate the need for justification. It may make it more urgent. If this criterion seems too severe for gods, we should at least be cautious when granting humans exceptions we could not defend even before a suffering child.

PART V · REALITY, POWER, AND PRICE

19. Is Freedom Real or Merely a Useful Story?

If we sought freedom in a brain, we would find tissues and processes. In a constitution, we would find signs. On the street, we would see people and behavior. Would that mean freedom does not exist? Only if we assumed everything real must exist like a stone.

A promise has no weight in itself, yet can change behavior. A border is not merely a natural line, yet can stop someone. Money, offices, and rules are classic examples of social realities whose existence depends, in disputed ways, on practices, institutions, and recognition [33]. Being socially constructed does not mean being effectless or subject to individual imagination's whim.

Freedom must be examined on several levels. There are capacities and possibilities: I can walk, understand, refuse, choose. There are institutional relationships: I have a right, protection, an avenue of appeal. There is subjective experience: I feel the author of my action. There is also normative judgment: we consider these possibilities deserving protection. No level can be wholly replaced by another.

Feeling free does not guarantee freedom's existence. I might feel unrestricted because I do not know the barriers or dare not imagine alternatives. Conversely, I may feel the burden of an endorsed obligation without being dominated. Experience matters, but must be compared with options, resources, and power relations.

Declared rights likewise do not guarantee effective possibility. An institution may say anyone can appeal, but the procedure may cost more than the person can afford. Showing the paper is insufficient; we must observe use. At the same time, a right's practical uselessness in a context does not prove its formulation worthless. It can provide a basis for challenging that context.

Can freedom be measured? Some components can: access time and cost, prohibitions' existence, ability to change providers, frequency of reprisals within a documented setting. We can describe decisions where the person is heard and situations where they are not. But aggregating these into one score requires weights and judgments. No natural unit tells us how many commercial options compensate for losing freedom of conscience.

This measurement limit does not make analysis useless. Temperature and pain are measured differently, yet both can matter. For freedom, a multidimensional picture may be more honest than rankings concealing tradeoffs. Above all, distribution must be examined: a high average can coexist with a minority's practical captivity.

Responsibility also has social reality. Saying “this task falls to you” can change expectations, behavior, sanctions, and trust. But social effects do not prove moral justification. An unjust rule can be very real. A society's assignment of blame to a group does not make that assignment true or legitimate.

Here we encounter the metaethical question: do we discover moral truths independent of our opinions, or construct principles through reason, agreement, and practice? We will not settle this dispute by declaring it meaningless. For this book, observing that both families of positions can distinguish a rule from its justification is enough. Lack of unanimity does not make all arguments equivalent.

For instance, “the powerful may do anything” may be a stable rule in a context, but faces the objection of the person exposed to arbitrariness. “Nobody need justify restricting another” eliminates the very framework within which those affected could challenge it. A defense of freedom can start from shared vulnerability and a demand for reasons irreducible to force, without claiming to have discovered a moral particle.

But what if freedom is merely a useful story for coordination? Then we must ask useful to whom and in what sense. A story making people accept obligations can serve cooperation or exploitation. Utility is an insufficient verdict until we specify beneficiaries, costs, and possibilities of challenge. A system can work well for administrators and badly for those administered.

Some realities would also persist without the word “freedom.” Animals can be captive, people prevented from leaving, individuals with or without alternatives. But vocabulary changes what we can identify, claim, and justify together. Calling a situation “domination” instead of “protection” can open a different moral inquiry; the label does not guarantee the inquiry is right, but can make the question possible.

It would therefore be too simple to say freedom was invented and for that reason can be canceled without loss. Humans also invented procedures for recognizing injustice, repairing harm, and limiting power. Their value must be argued through what they protect and how they can be justified, not denied because of their human origin.

It would be equally simple to treat freedom as an eternal object with one obvious definition merely discovered by history. We have seen that definitions select different aspects and can serve different interests. The concept must be corrected through contact with experiences earlier formulations excluded. Universality does not mean the first formulation is untouchable.

The position adopted here is practical realism in a descriptive sense, not the name of a new philosophical school: possibilities, constraints, and dependencies are real; concepts organizing them are constructed and revisable; justifying norms remains an argumentative task. We cannot abolish captivity by renaming it, but can begin abolishing it by finding words that make it contestable.

The good question, then, is not only whether freedom exists. It is what reality we recognize, what evidence we demand, and what we lose by denying it. Sometimes those calling freedom a fiction do not deny that people are locked up. They deny only that they should explain the locked door.

20. Who Gains and Who Loses from These Concepts?

Imagine a company, a family, and a state using the same sentence: “You are free, therefore responsible.” In each case it can affirm personal dignity: you are not an object; your actions matter. But it can also conceal withdrawal of obligations: we called your situation a choice, so everything following concerns you alone.

We must distinguish the benefits of justified principles, the benefits of selectively defining them, and the benefits of merely invoking words while practice contradicts them. Concept, application, and propaganda are not the same.

Freedom as protection most obviously benefits those exposed to arbitrary power. They gain the right to refuse, challenge, and pursue their own projects. The loser holds unjustified power. But that loss is not a symmetrical injustice. Losing the possibility of coercing another person is not the same as losing protection against coercion.

There are mutual gains too. A predictable rule can enable cooperation between people otherwise afraid of each other. Promises, professional responsibilities, and remedies can make trust possible. In such cases, limiting actions does more than redistribute a fixed good; it can create conditions for more people to pursue their ends. This possibility is explicable through cooperation's mechanisms, not proof that every rule produces common gain.

Freedom of property provides space for projects and independence. But property also distributes power to exclude. Analysis noticing only owners' freedom may omit restrictions on those without access. Conversely, analysis seeing only exclusion may omit property's security and capacity for planning. The moral dispute concerns legitimate title, limits, alternatives, and institutions, not a single label [21].

Retrospective responsibility can benefit victims by enabling demands for explanations and reparation. But it can also benefit an institution choosing a convenient perpetrator to avoid examining its own decisions. An accident portrayed solely as the last employee's carelessness can leave untouched the system of incentives and pressures contributing to it.

Prospective responsibility can benefit those in need even without an identifiable culprit. But it can disproportionately burden caring people, relatives, or nearby professionals. Availability does not prove someone must become permanently responsible. Proximity may create an initial obligation to intervene; it does not alone justify permanently allocating the cost.

Freedom of expression benefits dissenters, researchers, artists, and critical citizens. It can also benefit powerful actors with far greater amplification resources. The solution is not suppressing freedom in the name of equalization, but examining access, transparency, and possibilities of reply. Equal rights to speak and equal power to be heard are different problems.

In families, these concepts can protect children and dependent partners. But “responsibility toward the family” can prevent exit from abuse. Someone else can use “personal freedom” to abandon without explanation commitments on which vulnerable people depend. Neither connection nor exit should be absolutized. Safety, promises, alternatives, and affected people must be considered.

For homeless people, formal freedom can matter: it protects against becoming an institution's property. But invoking it can also serve refusal to create real possibilities. “They can go anywhere” does not help when every place is practically inaccessible. Conversely, social responsibility can justify support or invasive surveillance of beneficiaries. Remedies must be examined through the power they produce.

For disabled people, autonomy can mean recognition of their own voice, while protection can provide access and safety. But a definition reserving freedom for cognitive performance can exclude them, and care defined without rights can infantilize them. Real gain lies in combining support and respect, not compulsory choice between abandonment and control.

For researchers, freedom enables exploration and responsibility enables credibility. But funders may call compliance with their agenda “responsibility,” while researchers call refusal of all verification “freedom.” Both abuses turn a public good into a private shield. The dispute must return to evidence, roles, and possibilities of correction.

For power, one great advantage of moral vocabulary is that it can move control inside the person. An order says “do this.” One use of responsibility says “a good person would do this without being asked.” The latter can sustain authentic cooperation but also submission without visible orders. Failing to see a prohibition is insufficient; we must observe conditions under which refusal remains possible.

Those setting definitions thus wield power over distributing admiration and shame. If success is defined exclusively as merit, luck's beneficiaries may receive more authority than the facts justify. If failure is defined exclusively as a structural effect, we may deny personal contribution and obligations of reparation. Serious analysis does not replace one convenient determinism with another.

Another beneficiary is invisible: those who need not name their dependencies. They appear self-sufficient because supporting work and infrastructure remain in the background. Asking who benefits must also track what goes uncounted: carers' time, implementers' risk, the cost borne by those waiting, and the voices of those not invited to define the problem.

We can empirically test language's effects without claiming an experiment resolves morality. We could randomly assign participants to assess the same case presented as “individual choice,” “contextual constraints,” or without a label. We would measure blame attribution, support for assistance, and justifications, subsequently varying the act's severity and the person's control. This is a research proposal, not a result reported here.

Such a test might show vocabulary influences judgment. It would not automatically establish which judgment is correct. That requires examining relevant information and moral arguments. We must also avoid concluding that interest explains every conviction: someone can sincerely defend a principle reducing their advantages, and an argument can be valid even if its author benefits from it.

“Who benefits?” is therefore a checking tool, not a universal method of unmasking. Using it to reject every idea through its author's supposed interest abandons argument. Never using it risks confusing convenient definitions with neutral truths.

The most dangerous combination concentrates freedom among the powerful and responsibility among the vulnerable. The most promising expands the possibility of refusal while bringing obligations closer to knowledge, control, and benefit. This orientation produces no perfect formula either. But it shows where to look when a society speaks beautifully of principles and distributes consequences badly.

21. Freedom Administered by Machines

Suppose a system organizes your life so well you hardly need to choose. It suggests jobs, information, relationships, routes, and purchases. It understands you enough to avoid many errors. Are you freer because you save time, or less free because someone else selects your possible world?

The scenario is deliberately hypothetical. It assumes neither an omniscient system nor correct predictions. It reveals a problem: technology can expand capacity for action while concentrating power over options. Efficiency and autonomy can converge, but are not identical.

An assistant translating, explaining a form, or organizing information can reduce a barrier. Someone previously wholly dependent on an intermediary can gain control. But if the assistant conceals alternatives, pursues unknown goals, or makes refusal costly, support can become dependency. The question is not only how capable the system is, but on whose behalf it uses that capacity.

Philosophies of relational autonomy show that independence must not be confused with isolation: support and relationships can enable self-government, while other relationships undermine it [45]. Applied to technology, this distinction prevents assuming either that all automated help enslaves us or that every reduction in effort liberates us.

Imagine a platform offering the same correct recommendation in two ways. In the first, it shows relevant reasons, uncertainty, and alternatives, and users can refuse. In the second, it conceals having excluded options that generate no platform revenue. Even if the final recommendation proves good in one case, the power structure differs. A favorable outcome does not cancel the need for control over the process.

Another trap is responsibility through the last click. A person must confirm a complex recommendation without time or information to verify it. After an error, the organization says the decision was human. But a hand on a button does not prove meaningful deliberation. Control must be assessed through real possibilities of understanding, refusal, and intervention, not merely formal confirmation.

This problem is not exclusively technological. An administrative form can transfer risk in the same way. In our scenario, however, technology can reproduce it at scale and obscure it through complexity. Responsibility must therefore be traced to those choosing objectives, data, usage rules, and oversight conditions.

Delegation to a system does not eliminate creators' and operators' obligations. If an organization uses a tool for an activity affecting lives, choosing that tool is itself a decision. It does not follow that every developer answers identically for every use. It follows that “the system decided” does not conclude causal and moral analysis.

We can also imagine a highly persuasive system helping justify our choices. It can identify real objections or produce, on request, the explanation absolving us. Cognitive freedom is more than access to answers. It is the possibility of encountering resistance from facts and arguments, even when they contradict our preferred self-image.

Privacy here has value beyond concealing secrets. A space to explore ideas, err, and reconsider without permanent assessment can protect judgment's formation. In a hypothetical world where every hesitation immediately produces a score, people may select publishable thoughts before examining them. This is a potential risk requiring research, not a universal diagnosis of technology users.

Total transparency is no simple solution either. Making every personal detail visible can increase institutional power over people. More relevant is a justified asymmetry: those exercising authority should explain decisions, while those affected should not have to expose their entire lives to challenge them. Holding power accountable and surveilling the vulnerable are not the same project.

If one day artificial systems had relevant forms of experience, interests, or moral agency, additional questions would arise about their own rights and responsibilities. This book assumes neither that existing systems meet that condition nor that verbal competence alone proves it. Producing an excuse is not alone evidence that someone exists who morally understands excuses.

For the present argument, the criterion is simpler: technology should also be judged by how much justifiable control it offers affected people, not only by performance. Can I learn it intervened? Correct relevant data? Challenge an outcome? Choose a reasonable alternative? Who answers when I lack the means to discover what happened?

Automation can free time for research, care, and creation. It can also turn people into nominal supervisors of processes they cannot direct. The difference is not inevitably inscribed in “intelligence.” Architecture, institutions, and power distribution produce it. A machine easing our lives is a gain; a machine invoked to make responsibility unlocatable is a cover.

22. A Society That Does Not Demand Heroism Every Morning

In an imaginary society, decent people must constantly be extraordinary. Teachers compensate for resource shortages with sleepless nights. Workers risk everything to report danger. A daughter abandons her life to care for a parent. Researchers sacrifice their jobs to publish uncomfortable findings. Society praises them and carries on unchanged.

The praise may be deserved. But stopping there makes heroism a mechanism through which institutions avoid change. A decent order should not make kindness useless; it should reduce situations where ordinary moral behavior demands exceptional sacrifices.

From this book's analysis we can formulate a guiding principle: basic freedom should be widely distributed, and additional responsibility brought closer to control, knowledge, role, and remedial capacity. This is no equation and produces no automatic policy. It is a rule of examination preventing two shortcuts: “everyone is equally responsible” and “only whoever pressed the last button answers.”

The first domain of application is protecting refusal. People should not lose every means of existence to say no to abuse. This does not mean every refusal must be cost-free. It means distinguishing justified consequences from reprisals making freedom nominal. In family, work, research, and administration, exit and challenge must be real possibilities.

The second is distributing support. If care needs are treated solely as private affairs, freedom may depend on family luck and one member's sacrifice. Organizational alternatives must be assessed through access, continuity, quality, cost, and respect for preferences. Moving a service from family to institution is insufficient if the person becomes less heard there.

The third is education for judgment. A school of freedom should provide appropriate choices, ask for reasons, and allow revision. It should distinguish error from fraud, misunderstanding from ill will, and resource shortage from lack of interest. These normative proposals require pedagogical testing; we claim no single method can achieve them.

A concrete test could compare two ways of handling mistakes in a school project: one focused exclusively on sanctions, another on explanation, reparation, and a new plan. Assessment should track not only immediate obedience but honest reporting, understanding consequences, recurrence of error, and protection of those affected. Without such criteria, “autonomy” and “responsibility” risk remaining pedagogical labels.

The fourth is organizing work and expertise. Tasks must come with proportionate resources and authority. Those responsible for safety need an effective way to stop unsafe processes. Those responsible for a result's truth must be able to report unfavorable findings. Those responsible for care must be able to request help before exhaustion.

The fifth is a culture of correction. Not every error deserves the same reaction, nor does every acknowledgment conclude reparative obligations. Mature institutions can say simultaneously: “Thank you for reporting” and “We must remedy this.” Separating investigation from public humiliation can promote truth-finding, but must preserve accountability for deliberate concealment and victim protection. This is a design objective, not an excuse for opacity.

The sixth is making costs visible. Proposing a new freedom requires asking who bears the risk. Proposing a new obligation requires asking who receives time and means for it. Invoking efficiency requires examining whether we reduced total cost or merely shifted it to people with less power to protest.

This examination must include future generations. They cannot consent to our decisions or negotiate with us. We do not know their exact preferences, but have reasons to preserve general conditions for lives in which choices remain possible. Arguments about global justice and responsibility across borders or generations show why nonparticipation in a present contract does not automatically eliminate affected interests' importance [41].

Yet the future can be invoked abusively. A grand promise for hypothetical people does not justify every sacrifice imposed on existing ones. We must compare present harm's certainty, benefit's plausibility, the decision's reversibility, and alternatives. Respecting the future does not mean turning the present into material without rights.

How do we know reform increases freedom? Not merely by asking its administrators. Those affected must be heard, including those who do not use it, abandoned it, or cannot formulate a complaint in the expected language. Unintended effects and cases where protection produces new dependency must be sought. A reform admitting no failure criteria demands faith, not evaluation.

A checking framework could track refusal's cost, access to alternatives, time lost obtaining a right, distribution of care tasks, responses to appeals, and possibilities of repairing mistakes. These indicators are no complete moral measure and must not be blindly added into one score. They make visible questions solemn declarations can conceal.

There is also space a just society must leave unprogrammed. People are not only producers of benefits, carers, citizens, or bearers of projects. They also sit, listen to music, exchange an idea without results, change their minds, or spend time in ways no indicator can justify. If every moment must be optimized morally or economically, freedom has become a performance task.

Protecting this space does not deny obligations. It recognizes that cooperation's purpose is not absorbing the person into cooperation. We want institutions precisely so we need not negotiate every condition of existence alone each morning, or save one another exclusively through exhaustion.

This book offers no administrative paradise. Every system produces conflicts between interests, costs, and values. Some choices will remain tragic; some obligations heavy. What we can demand is that conflicts be visible, contestable, and distributed without turning vulnerability into guilt or power into a shield.

A free society is not one where nobody owes anything. It is one where obligations can be justified to those asked to fulfill them, freedoms do not depend on others' arbitrary goodwill, and those unable to defend themselves remain within the circle of consideration. Its deepest responsibility is to make room for lives that need not constantly prove they deserve to be lived.

Epilogue. The Freedom Not to Look Away

Let us return to the opening door. Someone has fallen. Four people are nearby. They have unequal power, information, and costs of delay. Yet none must be reduced to a label: the capable person, the dependent person, the child, the frightened one.

Perhaps the first calls for help. Perhaps the second sees a solution the others missed. The child can do something appropriate to their ability without receiving the whole outcome on their shoulders. The frightened person can stay a few minutes and may, in turn, need someone to defend them from an unjust consequence of delay.

In a bad version of the story, rescue succeeds and the vulnerable person alone pays the cost. In a better version, immediate help is followed by care for those who enabled it. In a still better version, institutions learn: access is repaired, procedures are clear, and decent intervention no longer depends on hidden sacrifice.

None of this requires believing people are the absolute causes of their own lives. Nor assuming every obligation simple or every freedom preservable intact. It requires seeing the difference between possessing power and deserving to exercise it, between needing help and becoming the helper's property, between making mistakes and being reduced to them.

If God exists, the question of goodness does not diminish. If not, suffering thereby receives no excuse. If ultimate free will remains disputed, our capacity to modify circumstances does not disappear. If freedom is articulated through human concepts, the doors those concepts can open are no less real.

The most convenient freedom lets us avoid seeing costs transferred to others. The most convenient responsibility is the one we can assign to someone else. A book about both must make us less comfortable with both conveniences.

We are not obliged to save the world alone. But perhaps we are obliged not to use total salvation's impossibility to refuse every reasonable share. And perhaps we may demand that this share not be set exclusively by those retaining power and outsourcing their duties.

Freedom is not only the possibility of leaving. It is also the possibility of staying without submission. Responsibility is not only the obligation to pay for the past. It is also the capacity to make the future less captive to its consequences.

The door is still open. The question is no longer whether its sign tells the truth. It is who can pass through, who can refuse, and who ensures that one person's freedom is not built from another's disappearance.

Reading Note. How the Claims Can Be Checked

In a book between science and philosophy, questions do not all demand the same evidence. A claim that a context changes behavior requires observation and appropriate methods. A claim that behavior is unjust also requires normative argument. A claim that someone is free may need both: knowing their alternatives and explaining why those alternatives matter.

An experiment can isolate a difference between conditions without reproducing an entire life. For every finding, ask who participated, how participants were assigned, what was measured, and what the result was compared with. A difference between groups does not automatically become a rule for every member. Nor does failure to detect a difference prove the effect impossible in every context.

Correlation and explanation are not interchangeable. If two characteristics appear together, one may contribute to the other, both may depend on other circumstances, or the relationship may be more complex. Even well-designed studies require separating measurements from proposed interpretation. Waiting for a reward in a task is not identical to one's entire character; an electrical signal is not identical to all deliberation.

What exactly was randomly assigned also matters. An offer of support is not identical to actually using it. Interview timing is not identical to a life of poverty. Changing a variable in a narrow setting can inform a social problem without resolving it. Extending the conclusion requires separate justification.

Studies with differing results do not mechanically cancel one another. Sometimes they examine different populations, measures, or interventions. Sometimes one corrects an overgeneralized conclusion. The useful question is not “which camp won?” but “under what conditions does the claim hold?” Findings limiting an attractive explanation therefore belong to the story, not to notes to be concealed.

Thought experiments serve another function. The open door, the child breaking a cup, or the god designing a world are not samples. They deliberately modify conditions to test a principle's consistency. They can reveal contradiction or assumption, but do not tell us how common a situation is in reality.

Finally, findings about an act's causes do not alone produce a responsibility verdict. That requires discussing control, knowledge, obligations, and remedial possibilities. Explaining does not automatically excuse. Rejecting an excuse does not mean having explained. This book can be challenged in both registers: through better evidence and better arguments.

The bibliography below identifies source types: primary philosophical texts, specialist syntheses, empirical studies, and normative documents. References to the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy indicate synthesis articles, not full readings of every historical work they discuss. Rights documents formulate normative standards; they are not themselves evidence of universal compliance.

Glossary. Distinctions That Prevent Short Circuits

Autonomy. The capacity and conditions for directing your life in a way you can recognize as your own. It may require support, information, and nonabusive relationships; it is not synonymous with isolation.

Capability. A real opportunity to achieve a particular way of being or acting. It differs from actual achievement: being able to choose a life does not mean being obliged to choose it.

Compatibilism. The family of positions holding that a relevant form of freedom can exist even if actions are causally determined. It does not assume every caused behavior is free.

Coercion. Restriction of options through force, threat, or other relevant pressures. Not every inconvenience is coercion in the same sense; the pressure's type and intensity must be specified.

Determinism. The thesis that relevant states and laws fix subsequent development. It is not fatalism: in a deterministic system, efforts and decisions can be among an outcome's causes.

Domination. Dependence on arbitrary or insufficiently controlled power. It can exist even when the power holder is not currently intervening.

Right. A justified claim or protection within a moral or legal framework. Needing something and having a right to it are related but not identical assertions.

Moral equality. Refusal to assign some people lesser fundamental value. It does not imply identical capacities, needs, or concrete responsibilities.

Incompatibilism. The family of positions holding that determinism cannot coexist with the freedom relevant to the debate. It includes both defenses of free will and forms of skepticism about it.

Negative freedom. Absence of others' interference in a relevant sphere of action. Defining interference and delimiting the sphere are themselves disputed.

Positive freedom. Self-government, control over one's life. It must not automatically be confused with well-being or an authority's right to decide someone's “true” desires.

Political libertarianism. A family of theories assigning central importance to individual freedom and strong property rights. It is not metaphysical libertarianism concerning free will.

Moral luck. The problem of uncontrolled factors influencing moral assessment of a person or act. Recognizing it does not automatically eliminate every distinction between behaviors.

Paternalism. Intervention in someone's choice justified by their purported good. Its forms and intensities vary; protective intention does not conclude the analysis of legitimacy.

Causal responsibility. Contribution to producing an outcome. It can exist without guilt and does not alone establish what reparation is owed.

Retrospective responsibility. Assessment of an act already performed: explanations, reproaches, sanctions, or reparation. These responses are not equivalent and can have different justifications.

Prospective responsibility. An obligation to prevent harm or perform a future task. It can arise from a role, special capacity, or relationship without prior guilt.

Structural responsibility. Responsibility linked to processes and institutions to which multiple actors contribute. It does not mean everyone is equally guilty or nobody needs specific identification.

Decision-making support. Assistance in understanding, expressing, and realizing preferences. It differs from automatically replacing someone's will with their assistant's.

Theodicy. An attempt to justify evil's existence in a world created or governed by a good God. A defense of logical compatibility pursues a more limited objective.
