Prologue. Four People Before the
Same Door
In an imaginary scene, four people stop before a door bearing the
words: “Admission is free.” The first has time, money, and confidence.
The second uses a wheelchair, and beyond the door a staircase begins.
The third is a child who has been told they can choose anything,
provided they choose what the adults want. The fourth could enter,
but knows that the person they depend on will demand an account of
every minute spent there.
The words on the door do not necessarily lie. Nobody asks them for a
ticket. Yet they do not tell the whole truth. The four do not have the
same freedom, although they receive the same permission.
Now we change the scene. Inside, someone collapses. The first visitor
could call for help. The second could open up access if someone re‐
moved the obstacle. The child is frightened. The last looks at the clock:
if they are late, they may lose something essential. Suddenly, the ques‐
tion is no longer only who can enter. It is who must stay.
Here freedom and responsibility begin to become entangled. Can we
recognize everyone's right to direct their own life without accepting a
right to abandon others in every circumstance? Can we ask for help
without turning all existence into an impossible debt? Can we hold
someone responsible for an act without declaring them the absolute
author of all the conditions that brought them there?
Part of the difficulty comes from how we use the same words for dif‐
ferent problems. “They are free” may mean that they are not im‐
prisoned, are not threatened, have the means, understand their
choice, or do not depend on someone's arbitrary goodwill. “They are
responsible” may mean that they caused something, deserve re‐
proach, hold an office, or can remedy a harm they did not cause. We
move between these meanings without noticing, then wonder why
our arguments fail to meet.
4


The book begins with a thesis that will be defended but also tested:
freedom deserves protection not because we are beings without de‐
pendencies, but because we are vulnerable beings capable of living
under others' power. Responsibility deserves preservation not because
each person created their own destiny, but because our actions, insti‐
tutions, and omissions change destinies that no one can control alone.
This thesis does not resolve conflicts in advance. Sometimes an obliga‐
tion really does limit a freedom. Sometimes one person's freedom is
defended by limiting another's power. At other times, two people can
become freer together. We will not call every well-intentioned con‐
straint “true freedom,” because such a definition would make recog‐
nizing abuse impossible.
We will move from the child who breaks a cup to the researcher who
opens up a dangerous possibility, from the person without housing to
the citizen who obeys an unjust law, from the person unable to formu‐
late an argument to the question of whether an omnipotent creator
would have obligations toward his own creation. At every step a less
solemn but often more revealing question will return: who gets the
options, and who gets the bill?
Scenes without historical attribution are thought experiments, not
documentary accounts. Studies are used for what they can show, not
as verdicts on human nature. Numbered references point to the final
bibliography. Our own arguments remain arguments: readers are in‐
vited not to believe them on authority, but to see where they hold and
where they can be challenged.

PART I · THE WORD AND ITS HISTORY
1. An Open Door Is Not Enough
Suppose someone is locked in a room but does not try to leave. They
are reading an interesting book and would have stayed anyway. Are
they free? From within their experience, nothing seems missing. From
outside, we know the option of leaving has been removed. The ex‐
ample separates two things we easily confuse: satisfying a present de‐
sire and having alternatives.
Now suppose the door is unlocked, but the building's owner can close
it at any time without explanation. Today the owner is kind. Tomor‐
row they may not be. Does our person enjoy permission, or a freedom
they can rely on? Finally, let us leave the door permanently open but
place the room above a chasm, without stairs. There is no longer a
prohibition. Nor a practicable exit.
The distinction popularized by Isaiah Berlin between negative and
positive freedom organizes some of these problems. The first concerns
the absence of others' interference in a sphere of action; the second
concerns self-government, the possibility of being one's own master.
They are not synonyms for “bad freedom” and “good freedom,” and
their formulations have many variants [1]. From here on, examples
will help us see what each formulation leaves out.
Negative freedom is indispensable. Someone who can be arrested for
an opinion gains little from the explanation that true freedom lies in
the soul. Neither privacy, religious freedom, nor the right to refuse a
relationship can be replaced with a personal-development program.
Having a sphere in which authority must stop is a moral achievement,
not an administrative detail.
But the absence of intervention does not alone answer what we can
do. The capabilities approach, associated with Amartya Sen and
Martha Nussbaum, shifts attention toward real opportunities to lead
particular kinds of lives. It distinguishes what a person achieves from
what they could effectively choose to achieve [2]. Two people may go
without eating: one is fasting, the other has no food. The immediate
outcome looks similar; the structure of possibilities does not.
6


It does not follow that every impossibility should be called a lack of
political freedom. I cannot breathe underwater or become younger
yesterday. If we put every limit of nature in the same category as im‐
prisonment, we lose useful distinctions. The practical question is
which obstacles can be changed through human action and who has
the power to change them. A staircase without a ramp is not a con‐
spiracy of gravity. It is also the result of design decisions.
The contemporary republican tradition adds freedom as nondomina‐
tion: not living under arbitrary or uncontrolled power, even when
that power does not constantly intervene. A benevolent master does
not abolish dependency merely by behaving nicely [3]. In our ex‐
ample, it matters who holds the key and what prevents them from us‐
ing it against the person in the room.
This perspective explains why a right can matter even when we do
not exercise it. We need not divorce for the possibility of leaving a
marriage to change the relationship. We need not challenge every de‐
cision for the existence of an appeal to alter authority's behavior. A
credible option works even when unused.
There is also inner freedom: the ability to examine a desire, delay an
impulse, and avoid immediately confusing what attracts us with what
we want to become. It does not coincide with political freedom.
Someone may enjoy many rights yet be caught in an addiction; anoth‐
er may retain dignity and clarity under oppression. But the possibility
of clarity in captivity does not make captivity acceptable.
Freedom therefore cannot be adequately measured by the raw num‐
ber of options. A hundred almost identical products may matter less
than a single possibility of refusing treatment, changing jobs, or
telling the truth without reprisals. The difference between two pack‐
aging variants cannot compensate for the disappearance of an exit
from an abusive relationship.
Let us imagine two cities. In the first, there are few rules, but access to
housing, work, and information depends on a handful of people who
cannot be challenged. In the second, there are more rules, but people
can challenge decisions, change employers, and keep their livelihoods
7


when criticizing authority. We cannot determine which is freer by
counting prohibitions alone. We must also examine private powers,
the cost of refusal, and the security of rights.
Yet it would be equally wrong to assume that every rule in the second
city is justified. Bureaucracy can stifle the very possibilities it claims to
protect. An inaccessible, slow, or humiliating appeals procedure is not
equivalent to an effective one. Promised protection must be distin‐
guished from usable protection.
Freedom also has a temporal dimension. Today I can choose some‐
thing that destroys almost all tomorrow's choices. I can promise, take
on debt, or assume an obligation. Sometimes commitment itself makes
a valuable project possible: a friendship, a child, long-term research.
At other times it closes a trap. Respecting freedom means neither for‐
bidding every commitment nor treating every signature as a legitim‐
ate surrender of protection.
A useful vocabulary must therefore keep the questions distinct. Am I
obstructed? Do I have the means? Do I understand what I am choos‐
ing? Can I refuse without disproportionate cost? Do I depend on
someone's goodwill? Can I still revise my choice? No single answer re‐
places all the others.
This way of thinking has an uncomfortable consequence: my freedom
may be partly invisible to me because certain doors have always
opened for me. Likewise, I may regard as a “personal preference”
what someone else has learned to stop asking for. We need not silence
their voice to notice these possibilities; we must offer conditions in
which that voice can say something else too.
Before declaring someone free, then, it is worth asking: free from
what, to do what, in relation to whom, and for how long? The open
door is the beginning of the examination, not its result.

2. The History of Those Allowed to
Be Free
A community can consider itself free while some of its members live
in slavery. The contradiction is obvious to anyone starting from each
person's freedom. It becomes less visible in an order that reserves full
participation for only some. The first historical question is therefore
not “When was freedom invented?” but “Whose freedom was at is‐
sue?”
In Politics, Aristotle distinguishes the rule of free people from the rela‐
tionship between master and slave. He attempts to justify “natural”
slavery and distributes authority unequally within the household. Yet
the same text records the opposing view: for some, slavery is contrary
to nature [4]. We must not turn an influential justification into an era's
absolute consensus.
It is troubling that thought capable of moral subtlety can take what
serves a social order to be nature. The lesson is not that people in the
past were simply less intelligent. It is that intelligence can rationalize
the dependencies from which it benefits. The same risk exists
whenever the group defining the “rational,” “civilized,” or “respons‐
ible” person is also the group distributing rights.
Imagine a small town where only property owners vote. They discuss
freedom sincerely, oppose a ruler's arbitrary power, and protect one
another's wealth. Outside the assembly, those who serve them have
different questions: can they leave, learn, keep what they earn? A his‐
tory written only from the assembly's speeches would capture a truth,
but not the whole truth.
In ancient civic traditions, a community's freedom and a citizen's
status do not coincide with the modern idea of equal rights for all. The
history of republicanism shows both concern for independence from
domination and disputes about the relationship between freedom and
political participation [3]. From this inheritance we can draw tools
against arbitrary power without importing the exclusions that accom‐
panied it.
9


A decisive change in the history of freedom occurs when excluded
people use an order's declared principles against its boundaries. If
freedom is valuable because we are persons, why should it stop at sex,
wealth, origin, or inherited status? Sometimes a concept says more
than its formulators intended. That is precisely why it can become
dangerous to power.
But universalization in words is not universalization through institu‐
tions. We can proclaim everyone equal while distributing unequally
the time needed for education, physical security, access to property, or
credibility before an authority. These differences must be documented
in each context, not assumed identical everywhere. Conceptually,
however, they show that a universal principle can coexist with select‐
ive mechanisms of application.
Frederick Douglass's autobiography provides direct testimony to the
link between literacy and liberation. Learning to read appears not
merely as accumulating information, but as access to a language in
which enslavement can be recognized and challenged. His account
also reveals the violence supporting the slaveholding order [5]. It is
the testimony of one life, not a statistic about every experience of
slavery.
Here freedom meets knowledge ambivalently. Understanding your
own powerlessness better does not necessarily make you happier im‐
mediately. You may lose the peace of adaptation before gaining the
means of change. That makes the argument “they were content before
someone explained it to them” morally suspect. Satisfaction can ex‐
press a good life, but also a horizon kept narrow.
In 1792, Mary Wollstonecraft attacks the education that produces wo‐
men's dependence and then presents the resulting dependence as
evidence of their incapacity. She claims their status as rational beings
and their access to an appropriate education [6]. The mechanism she
criticizes has a general form: prevent a capacity from developing,
then use its absence to justify the prevention.
We can recognize it in an imaginary experiment. An institution does
not allow a group to make important decisions. Twenty years later, its
leaders observe that the group has little decision-making experience
10


and invoke that lack to retain control. The question is not whether ex‐
perience matters; of course it can. The question is who prevented its
acquisition and what fair transition is now possible.
Freedom can also be formally granted without repairing the condi‐
tions produced by unfreedom. Opening a competition does not auto‐
matically erase disadvantages accumulated before it opened. Nor does
it tell us by itself what reparation would be fair. We need arguments
about causation, the continuity of benefits, the limits of liability, and
the costs of remedies. Precisely because these questions are difficult,
they must not be replaced by the convenient formula “now everyone
is allowed.”
Viewed this way, history is not a linear march from darkness to light.
Freedom can increase in one domain and decrease in another. A per‐
son can become more independent of a local authority and more de‐
pendent on a distant economic mechanism. A revolution can remove
one domination and create another. These are structural possibilities,
not a claim that every historical transformation produces exactly the
same result.
Another mistake is to judge every era only by what it could not yet
formulate. People act in contexts that make some alternatives hard to
imagine. Yet contextualization must not become automatic absolution.
Contemporary critics, resistance, and suppressed voices often show
that other judgments were possible. Understanding why someone was
wrong does not mean declaring they were not wrong.
Who benefited from early conceptions of freedom? Those protected
from the arbitrary power of a master, king, or majority. Who could
lose? Those whose power over others was limited. But these two
changes do not have the same moral value: losing the possibility of
owning a person is not equivalent to losing the freedom not to be
owned.
The history of freedom is largely the history of expanding and contest‐
ing the pronoun “we.” Whenever a society says “we are free,” the
checking question remains simple: who must stand outside that sen‐
tence for it to seem true?

3. Liberation of the Soul and
Disobedience of the Mind
Someone can be rich yet unable to bear criticism. Someone else can
have very little yet refuse a humiliation that would bring advantages.
These situations do not prove external goods unimportant. But they
show why many traditions have also sought freedom in the relation‐
ship between the person, desire, fear, and meaning.
Epictetus opens the Handbook by separating things within our power
from those outside it. Judgment and the orientation of the will occupy
the center of Stoic practice, while reputation, wealth, and many ex‐
ternal events remain uncertain [7]. This is not a program for complete
emotional control by mere command, but a discipline of examining
how we relate to what happens.
The power of this idea appears when we cannot immediately remove
a constraint. A persecuted person may find an essential difference
between being silenced and inwardly accepting the persecutor's lie.
But the same idea can be abused by a superior who says: “Do not
change the conditions; change your attitude.” An exercise chosen by
the vulnerable is not the same as an obligation to adapt imposed by
the powerful.
This distinction will recur throughout the book. A technique of resist‐
ance can become a technique of governance. The victim's courage
does not absolve the aggressor. Someone's ability to find meaning in a
terrible situation does not give another person the right to put them in
it.
In Augustine's Christian philosophy, will, evil, and dependence on
grace remain in enduring tension. The emphasis falls not only on hav‐
ing options but on the possibility of willing the good; in later writings,
human capacity is not treated as self-sufficient. The dispute with the
Pelagians shows that the relationship between freedom, guilt, and di‐
vine help was contested even within the tradition [8].
12


For readers who do not share the theological premises, the question
remains recognizable: if someone needs help they cannot produce
themselves, how far can we attribute their failure entirely to them?
For believers, there is the further difficulty of how this help is distrib‐
uted and how judgment and mercy can coexist. Religion does not
automatically eliminate the problem of responsibility; sometimes it
sharpens it.
Islamic traditions likewise cannot be reduced to a formula of destiny
rendering action useless. Debates over causality, divine action, and
human action include very different positions, and explanations of
the natural order differ between schools [9]. The comparative lesson
is not to confuse a divinity's power, its knowledge, and human parti‐
cipation in producing an act. These are three questions, not one.
In Indian Buddhist ethics, liberation is linked to reducing ignorance,
attachment, and suffering. Criticism of a permanent self does not
simply cancel the importance of intentions or compassion; different
traditions explain action and personal continuity without always as‐
suming an unchanging self [10]. For our subject, the challenge is radic‐
al: can responsibility make sense without an absolute metaphysical
owner of every mental state?
We can formulate the problem without adopting the entire doctrine.
Yesterday's child, today's adult, and tomorrow's older person differ
profoundly, but promises, effects, and relationships connect their
lives. Practical continuity can matter morally even if we do not as‐
sume a core wholly independent of causes. It does not follow that
every conception of identity is equally good. It follows that “you are
not an isolated substance” does not immediately mean “nothing mat‐
ters.”
Confucius centers the cultivation of conduct in relationships: how
people learn to behave, judge, and participate in a moral order. Ritual
and roles are not merely external restrictions; they can cultivate sens‐
itivity and character [11]. This perspective corrects the image of the
adult fully constituted before any social bond.
But relationships also need criticism. Being someone's child cannot
mean that every parental command is justified. Holding a role in a
community cannot mean that a person exists only for that role. An in‐
13


terpretation of harmony that leaves no room for the wronged to com‐
plain risks confusing the authority's peace with the whole group's
good.
Daoism, in its diversity, questions the rigid fixing of values and force‐
ful intervention that tries to shape the world according to a narrow
scheme. Unforced action does not reduce to laziness, and criticism of
excessive control is not equivalent to indifference toward con‐
sequences [12]. It offers every reformer a useful question: how much
of the harm you cause comes from the ambition to eliminate every de‐
viation?
These traditions are not simple variants of the same doctrine. A Stoic
is not a European Buddhist; Confucian thought is not a liberalism that
has yet to discover its individual; theologies have no single answer
concerning freedom. Comparison is fruitful precisely when it pre‐
serves differences and uses one tradition's questions to examine an‐
other's blind spots.
From their encounter we can draw a shared difficulty without claim‐
ing they would all accept the same conclusion: our desires are not
automatically freedom's deepest expression. Some may be unex‐
amined, dependent on fear, or formed under pressure. Yet who has
the right to declare that they know our “true” desires better than we
do?
A cautious answer calls for conditions for reflection, not an omni‐
scient guardian. Offer time, information, support, and the possibility
of encountering other lives. Do not turn someone's disagreement into
evidence of moral illness. Help them reconsider, but accept that recon‐
sideration may lead to a choice you do not prefer.
Inner freedom without external freedom can become a necessary
refuge. External freedom without any capacity for reflection can re‐
main easy to exploit. The two must support each other without either
serving as an excuse to abolish the other. Someone who finds peace in
captivity has not thereby signed a justification of imprisonment.

4. Modernity and the Individual
Who Never Appears Alone
Imagine receiving a perfectly formulated constitution but no way to
use it against those who violate it. Then imagine the opposite: a well-
organized, protective community where you cannot choose another
way of life. In the first case, rights are fragile; in the second, protection
approaches captivity. Much of modern political philosophy can be
read as an attempt not to remain trapped in either room.
Hobbes begins with the danger of insecurity and the need for a com‐
mon power capable of preventing destructive conflict. In the architec‐
ture of his argument, order is not an ornament added to freedom, but
a condition of a life in which promises and projects can have stability
[13]. The question he leaves open to criticism is how much power we
can concentrate to defend ourselves from others' power.
Security can be truly liberating. Someone constantly afraid of violence
lacks the same scope for action as someone protected. But this very
need can be exploited: authority can describe every opposition as
danger and every check on itself as weakening protection. Assessing a
power therefore means examining the mechanisms through which it
can be corrected when invoking safety in its own interest.
Locke links government's legitimacy to protecting rights that are not
simply the government's gift. Consent and the possibility of resisting a
power that betrays its purpose play central roles. Yet interpretations
of property, tacit consent, and obligations of assistance are disputed;
Locke must not be reduced to the idea that property owners owe noth‐
ing to anyone [14].
The difficulty of consent remains conceptually relevant. Remaining in
a country is not the same as having approved every one of its rules.
Using a road is not an informed signature beneath an unlimited con‐
tract. Some obligations must be justified through fairness, rights, and
the conditions of cooperation, not by retrospectively inventing an
agreement nobody formulated.
15


Rousseau shifts the question toward the possibility of an association
in which people obey a political order without simply becoming sub‐
jects of private wills. The general will concerns the common interest
and is not wholly identical to the sum of preferences or to every ma‐
jority decision [15]. The delicate issue is who may claim to know it and
what happens to dissenters when authority speaks in their name.
This is one of emancipation's recurring temptations: “I constrain you
because, deep down, this is your will too.” Sometimes people need
protection from constraints they have normalized. But a regime ac‐
cepting no evidence of their disagreement and no possibility of chal‐
lenge does not liberate them merely by using the vocabulary of eman‐
cipation.
For Kant, moral autonomy means not following every impulse but ori‐
enting oneself by principles reason can uphold as law for rational be‐
ings. A person must not be treated merely as a means to another's
ends [16]. This emphasis changes freedom's meaning: sometimes re‐
fusing what immediately benefits you expresses precisely independ‐
ence from impulse.
Application nevertheless requires care. If we make sophisticated argu‐
mentative ability a condition of human worth, we expose those un‐
able to display it. An ethics of respect must also explain protection for
children, profoundly vulnerable people, and those unable to particip‐
ate in the rational exchange of justifications. We will return to this dif‐
ficulty without assuming that a philosopher's prestige resolves it.
Mill defends ample space for thought, discussion, and experiments in
living. His principle of preventing harm to others limits the legitimate
use of coercion over capable adults; the mere conviction that they
would live better otherwise is insufficient. Mill also fears social
tyranny, not only state tyranny [17].
This approach's merit is that it does not make freedom conditional on
majority approval. The difficulty of application is that “harm” has dis‐
puted boundaries. Criticism may wound pride without constituting ag‐
gression. Private activity may create risks for third parties. A slogan
cannot decide every case; we must compare the nature and severity of
harm, the evidence, and the proportionality of intervention.
16


Marx examines another fault line: workers are legally free to sell their
labor power but may lack the means to avoid selling it. This double
condition appears explicitly in Capital's analysis of the buying and
selling of labor power [18]. The contract can be real and the depend‐
ency real at the same time.
This criticism does not oblige us to regard every wage relationship as
identical to slavery. The legal and practical differences are enormous
and morally decisive. It does oblige us not to treat every formal agree‐
ment as sufficient evidence of a balanced relationship. Conversely, de‐
nouncing dependency does not prove that every system proposed to
eliminate it will better protect freedom. Both economic and adminis‐
trative power need checks.
In Sartre's existentialism, people cannot wholly eliminate the dimen‐
sion of choice by retreating into roles and explanations. Saying “that is
how things are done” or “that is my function” does not settle the ques‐
tion of what I am doing. Yet freedom is lived within a situation, not an
obstacle-free vacuum [19]. Read without this precaution, the rhetoric
of radical freedom can slide toward unjustly blaming the constrained.
In more recent political philosophy, Rawls asks which principles can
fairly organize cooperation, while libertarianism foregrounds indi‐
vidual rights and the limits of coercion. The dispute includes property,
transfers, unearned advantages, and the distribution of opportunities.
Libertarianism itself is not a single doctrine: significant differences
exist between right- and left-wing variants [20, 21].
These philosophies must not become a contest in which one author
supplies every answer. Hobbes obliges us to take insecurity seriously;
Locke, limited power; Rousseau, self-government; Kant, respect; Mill,
diversity; Marx, material dependence; Sartre, evasion of our own par‐
ticipation. But every correction can become excessive when detached
from the other questions.
The modern individual does not, in fact, appear alone. Behind them
lie the language in which they think, the people who raised them, the
infrastructures they use, and the rules recognizing their property. No‐
ticing these conditions does not dissolve the individual into society. It
situates them. A person can have rights precisely because they depend
on others and need protection within that dependency.
17


Perhaps political maturity begins when we abandon two symmetrical
fantasies: the individual who owes nothing to the world from which
they emerged, and the collectivity entitled to everything it can take
from the individual. Between them lies the hard work of justification.

PART II · THE REAL HUMAN BEING, NOT THE IDEAL ONE
5. Who Chooses When the Brain
Decides?
If tomorrow a device predicted your choice, would you cease to be
free? The answer depends on what it predicted, how well, and how it
affected the situation. Anticipating a friend's choice through know‐
ledge of their values is not the same as controlling it. And identifying a
signal preceding a finger movement is not the same as demonstrating
that all important deliberation is useless.
The problem of free will concerns the relationship between action,
causality, and control. Compatibilists maintain that certain relevant
forms of freedom can exist even if actions have determining causes.
Incompatibilists dispute this compatibility; they include both defend‐
ers of nondeterministic free will and skeptics about the freedom
needed for ultimate moral desert [22]. Doctrinal names do not decide
the dispute. We must ask what would count as authorship of an ac‐
tion.
Determinism is not fatalism. In a deterministic universe, what you do
can change what happens because your action is one of the causes.
Deliberating, seeking advice, or building an institution can form part
of the chain producing a different result. Fatalism, in the sense relev‐
ant here, would say the outcome occurs regardless of what you do.
Confusing them turns a thesis about causes into an unjustified excuse
for passivity.
Chance alone does not solve the problem either. If my choice were de‐
cided by a random fluctuation, it would not become more mine for
that reason. A lottery hidden in the brain is not obviously a superior
form of self-government. Defenders of freedom must explain how the
person contributes to action, not merely find somewhere prediction
fails.
The experiments associated with Benjamin Libet became famous for
the discrepancy between preparatory brain activity and the moment
participants report intending a simple movement. Their interpreta‐
19


tion remains controversial, however: retrospective reports of inten‐
tion's timing, the task's arbitrary nature, and the neural signal's mean‐
ing limit metaphysical conclusions. Later literature explicitly dis‐
cusses these difficulties [23]. The brain is not a stranger entering the
scene before the person; it is part of the conditions of their existence.
A 2019 study by Uri Maoz and colleagues compared arbitrary choices
with deliberative choices involving donations, using comparable mo‐
tor responses. Among the 18 participants analyzed, the classic readi‐
ness potential did not appear in the same way before deliberative
choices [23]. The result limits a popular generalization. It neither
demonstrates a soul independent of the brain nor closes the free-will
debate.
Consider an ordinary decision: a researcher discovers an error weak‐
ening their paper. They can conceal it or correct the results. The de‐
cision involves memory, anticipation of consequences, fear, profes‐
sional norms, and self-image. Saying all these are realized through
brain processes does not make them unreal. The practical question is
whether the person could understand the relevant reasons, respond
to them, and what pressures distorted their deliberation.
This approach is not metaphysically neutral in every respect, but
neither does it claim to resolve the person's ultimate origin. We can
construct an operational notion of control: the difference between an
involuntary movement, an action under threat, and a careful, in‐
formed, uncoerced choice. Even those rejecting absolute moral desert
can consider these differences important for protection, trust, and co‐
operation.
The skeptical objection remains powerful: people did not choose their
genes, family, earliest experiences, or even their initial disposition to
reflect. If you must be the complete author of your character to de‐
serve punishment's suffering, the requirement seems endlessly to
push authorship's origin backward. Every choice that shapes you is
made by a “you” already formed to some extent.
This argument can weaken revenge's justification without eliminating
the need to respond. Victims must be protected, danger may need
stopping, harm may need repairing. But the aim is no longer necessar‐
ily to administer suffering because the universe demands metaphysic‐
20


al compensation. We can ask which measures are necessary, propor‐
tionate, and likely to reduce harm without turning the perpetrator
into an object devoid of dignity.
There is a risk here too, however. If we abandon culpability and retain
only the prediction of danger, we might end up restricting people for
what a system assumes they will do. A humanizing view of causes
does not justify unlimited management of people deemed risky. The
right to challenge, evidence, proportionality, and limits on power re‐
main essential.
Praise also needs examination. Recognizing effort, courage, or gener‐
osity does not assume that people produced every condition of their
success themselves. We can admire an act and acknowledge the luck
that made it possible. Admiration becomes dangerous when it turns
into the conclusion that success authorizes every subsequent advant‐
age while failure proves worthlessness.
The problem differs in close relationships. When a friend betrays you,
the causal explanation may be true and insufficient. You want to know
whether they understand what they did, undertake to repair it, and
can be trusted again. A world without metaphysical hatred need not
be a world without reproach, boundaries, or demands. Asking for an
answer can treat the other as a moral participant rather than merely
a mechanism.
A reasonable conclusion, therefore, is neither “free will has been
proved” nor “science has demonstrated that no one is accountable.” It
is more modest and productive: we must separate the will's ultimate
origin from the concrete capacities through which people direct and
correct their actions. The first remains disputed; the others can be in‐
vestigated, supported, and sometimes severely impaired.
On the evening when you must choose whether to admit a mistake,
the fact that you are not your own absolute cause does not make con‐
fession meaningless. It can become one of the causes of the person
you will be tomorrow. Practical freedom need not stand outside
nature to matter within it.

6. The Psychology of Will:
Autonomy, Control, and Fear
“You choose for yourself,” says a teacher, and the child chooses. The
teacher sighs, raises an eyebrow, and asks whether they are sure. The
child changes the answer. The teacher smiles: “See? You can when you
want to.” Formally, the choice was not prohibited. Psychologically,
however, the system of approval showed the child which option pre‐
serves their relationship with the adult.
Not every social influence cancels autonomy. We learn from others'
reactions, and their reasons can become reasons we sincerely accept.
The relevant difference is between conduct we endorse and conduct
maintained mainly through control, fear, or pressure. Even this differ‐
ence admits degrees; life does not offer two perfectly separate draw‐
ers, one for pure actions and another for actions wholly alien to us.
Self-determination theory, formulated by Richard Ryan and Edward
Deci, treats autonomy, competence, and relatedness as important psy‐
chological needs. Here autonomy means experiencing action as one's
own, not absolute independence from others. Their 2000 article syn‐
thesizes the research program and distinguishes forms of motivation
and internalization [24]. It is a research-supported theoretical frame‐
work, not proof that the same educational recipe works identically for
everyone.
In an imaginary workplace, two teams receive the same difficult task.
The first understands the purpose, can propose methods, and can
identify limits. The second receives contradictory orders and is pun‐
ished for asking questions. Even with equal pay and a lawful task,
their experience of control can differ greatly. Applying the psycholo‐
gical framework suggests what to investigate; it does not permit in‐
venting findings about teams we have not studied.
The experience of control also matters when failure repeats. In 2016,
Maier and Seligman revised the classic interpretation of learned help‐
lessness. Neuroscientific animal research suggests that under certain
aversive conditions passivity is the default response, while experien‐
22


cing control trains mechanisms that can counteract it [25]. We must
not transfer these protocols directly to every poor, depressed, or disap‐
pointed person. They show why the relationship between effort and
outcome deserves examination, not provide remote diagnoses.
Suppose every complaint in an office is ignored. After a while, em‐
ployees stop complaining. Management may interpret silence as satis‐
faction. An alternative explanation is that people no longer believe the
procedure works. To distinguish the hypotheses, we must change
something observable: the response, protection from reprisals, the
possibility of an outcome. “Why did they not speak up?” is incomplete
without “What happened to those who did?”
Here lies an important circular relationship. Freedom to act can de‐
velop confidence in one's capacity, and that confidence can make free‐
dom more usable. Conversely, repeated lack of control can reduce ini‐
tiative, and reduced initiative can justify still greater control. Not
every such cycle exists in every institution. But the mechanism is
plausible enough to require investigation, not automatic moralizing.
There is also the uncomfortable freedom of uncertainty. Being able to
choose sometimes means no longer being able to attribute the whole
outcome to an order. People may prefer someone else to decide, not
because they are generally incapable, but because they are tired, over‐
whelmed, or consider the choice insufficiently important. Voluntary
delegation can be an exercise of freedom, not necessarily its sur‐
render.
But delegation needs conditions. I must be able to ask the person de‐
ciding for me for explanations; I must know what I entrusted to them
and be able to revise their mandate. Someone letting a friend choose
the restaurant has not thereby surrendered the right to decide where
to live. Likewise, an expert's competence does not create unlimited au‐
thority over a person's ends.
Will must not be confused with productivity. Someone refusing an im‐
posed objective may be autonomous, not lazy. Someone producing
enormously may be driven by fear. From outside, we see conduct and
invent the inner story too quickly. Serious assessment asks what reas‐
ons are accessible to the person, how they understand them, and un‐
der what conditions they can say no.
23


At the same time, authenticity does not mean the absence of every ob‐
ligation. I can get up at night to care for a child without wanting to
wake at that moment. The action may still express a commitment I re‐
cognize as mine. The difference between immediate desire and an en‐
dorsed project prevents us from defining freedom exclusively as
present pleasure.
The problem becomes harder when the project was formed under
pressure. Someone may sincerely say they do not need freedoms they
were never allowed to explore. We have no right automatically to de‐
clare them incapable of judgment. But we can ask whether they have
access to alternatives, whether disagreement would be punished, and
whether the preference survives in a less coercive setting. Respecting
a voice and examining its conditions do not exclude each other.
A society concerned with autonomy should not demand that people
choose constantly. It should help distinguish important choices from
noise, offer intelligible information, and preserve the possibility of
correction. A form with thirty checkboxes does not, through complex‐
ity, demonstrate respect for the person. It may merely transfer the
cost of poor organization to them.
Ultimately, the relevant psychological question is not whether we feel
free every moment. Feeling can mislead in either direction. It is
whether conditions exist for forming intentions, examining them,
learning from effects, and changing what we do without every devi‐
ation threatening the bonds on which we depend. Sometimes free‐
dom's first sign is not enthusiasm, but being able to make a mistake
without losing your place in the world.

7. Poverty, Luck, and the Factory
of Choices
Two people delay paying a bill. One forgot. The other bought food. The
observable behavior is the same, but assessing it requires different in‐
formation. When we call the second “irresponsible” without knowing
their alternatives, we do more than describe an act. We import the
budget and safety margin of our own life into theirs.
Poverty can restrict possibilities directly through lack of resources,
and indirectly through how emergencies occupy attention. In 2013,
Mani, Mullainathan, Shafir, and Zhao investigated the link between
financial difficulties and cognitive-task performance. They used both
financial scenarios and comparisons of the same sugarcane farmers
before and after harvest, reporting poorer performance under finan‐
cial pressure [26]. The results do not describe a fixed intelligence of
“the poor” or authorize individual diagnosis.
It is important to see the limits too. A seasonal comparison is not
identical to an experiment changing only income while holding an en‐
tire life constant. Nor does financial pressure necessarily produce the
same effect in every situation. In 2016, Carvalho, Meier, and Wang
studied low-income households, randomly assigning interview timing
before or after payday. They found differences in some monetary
choices, but not in cognitive function, risk-taking, or measured de‐
cision quality [27]. This is not an exact duplicate of the earlier study; it
is evidence that we should not universalize the mechanism.
The cautious conclusion is more useful than a slogan. Resources and
context can change decisions; the effect depends on the situation and
the measure. We have no scientific license to declare that poverty can‐
cels agency. We have reasons not to treat every choice as though made
in a laboratory of comfort.
25


One advantage of material security is the possibility of making mis‐
takes. Those with savings can refuse a poor offer, wait for a reply, or
pay to repair an error. For someone else, the same error can close a se‐
quence of options. In this sense, resources buy more than goods. They
buy reversibility.
Imagine two young people attempting a project. The first lives rent-
free in the family home; the second pays rent alone and supports a re‐
lative. Both work seriously. If the project fails, the first can call it ex‐
perience; the second may lose their home. It does not follow that the
first deserves no credit. It follows that risk cannot be adequately
measured merely by the activity's name.
The same caution applies to success. Bertrand and Mullainathan's re‐
search, published in 2004, sent fictitious résumés to job advertise‐
ments in Boston and Chicago, randomly varying names perceived as
associated with racial categories. Names perceived as white received
approximately 50% more callbacks [28]. This is a relative difference,
not 50 percentage points, and concerns that historical experiment, not
every labor market today.
The study reveals a mechanism through which assessments of oppor‐
tunity can depend on something other than the skills presented. It
does not demonstrate that every professional failure results from dis‐
crimination. Between denying the mechanism and explaining all out‐
comes through it lies the space of serious research: what varies, in
which context, and with what consequences?
Another important program, Moving to Opportunity, allowed examin‐
ation of the effects of a randomly assigned offer of assistance to move
to lower-poverty neighborhoods. Chetty, Hendren, and Katz's analysis
found later benefits for children moving before age 13, while effects
for older adolescents differed and were sometimes slightly negative
[29]. The offer was randomized; actually moving must not be confused
with random assignment. The results highlight the role of exposure
duration and disruption of existing relationships.
26


Environment matters, but not as uniform magic. Removing a child
from a place can open opportunities and break bonds. Any policy in‐
spired by such findings must examine age, context, family choice, and
necessary support. “Environment influences life” does not mean “we
know in advance where everyone should live.”
Luck complicates morality in a way the language of merit often con‐
ceals. We do not choose our starting point, or everyone we meet, ill‐
nesses avoided, accidents that never happened, or the moments when
our work becomes sought after. Philosophy calls situations in which
assessments of a person seem influenced by factors beyond their con‐
trol “moral luck” [30]. This does not prove effort nonexistent. It proves
that outcomes do not measure effort in a pure state.
Why, then, do we like to believe everyone receives exactly what they
deserve? Without claiming a universal psychological explanation, we
can observe the idea's justificatory function. For winners, it turns ad‐
vantage into a moral certificate. For institutions, it can reduce the ob‐
ligation to investigate their own mechanisms. For losers, it can turn an
unfavorable context into a shameful identity.
There is an opposite danger too: describing people solely through the
conditions limiting them can deprive them of all authorship. Poor
people then become objects of an expert explanation rather than part‐
ners in conversation. We must be able to say simultaneously that a
choice was difficult and remains a choice, that an act can be criticized
and that the person's entire life cannot be deduced from it.
A fair allocation of responsibility should distinguish being able to con‐
tribute from being able to guarantee the outcome. Pupils can work
hard without controlling school quality. Employees can be careful
without controlling an impossible production pace. Citizens can be in‐
formed without controlling the public agenda. Asking for a contribu‐
tion is one thing; transferring responsibility for the whole system onto
the person is another.
To assess real freedom, we must ask not only what people chose but
what other choices would have cost. What did refusal cost? Waiting? A
mistake? Two lives can contain the same verbs and profoundly differ‐
ent possibilities. The words “they chose” do not exempt us from ex‐
amining the sentence that follows.

8. Responsibility Is Not One Single
Thing
A tree falls on a car. A driver destroys a car through carelessness. A
mechanic fails to check a problem they should have noticed. A neigh‐
bor sees the accident and can call for help. All these situations involve
causes, but not the same kind of responsibility. The tree can be a cause
without being guilty; the neighbor can have an obligation without be‐
ing a cause.
The philosophy of moral responsibility examines the conditions under
which an act can be attributed to a person, when blame or praise is
appropriate, and the roles of control, knowledge, and human relation‐
ships [31]. For our discussion it is useful to separate several questions
without claiming a definitive classification accepted by every school.
Causal responsibility concerns contributing to an outcome. Role re‐
sponsibility concerns what falls to a parent, doctor, administrator, or
researcher through their position. Retrospective moral responsibility
concerns how we assess what someone did. Prospective responsibility
concerns what must be done from now on. Legal liability depends on
specific institutional rules and procedures and does not automatically
coincide with moral judgment.
These differences change how we discuss suffering. If I did not cause
an accident, I may not be culpable for it. But if I can save someone
without serious risk, absence of guilt for the accident is not a suffi‐
cient answer to whether I should intervene. Conversely, a profession‐
al's involvement in repairing harm does not prove they caused it.
Already in Aristotle we find distinctions between voluntary, involun‐
tary, and circumstantially pressured actions. The example of goods
thrown overboard during a storm shows some choices' mixed charac‐
ter: nobody would want them in themselves, yet they are chosen for a
purpose in a concrete situation [32]. Between full freedom and abso‐
lute coercion lie many morally relevant cases.
28


Imagine an employee signing off on an unsafe procedure because oth‐
erwise she will lose her job. The threat does not make her signature
nonexistent, but changes its assessment. If the risk to others is enorm‐
ous and alternatives exist, the obligation to refuse may remain strong.
If alternatives are very costly and decision-making power very small,
blame must account for that. The manager who created the dilemma
cannot disappear from the analysis.
Ignorance requires equally careful examination. Did you not know be‐
cause information was inaccessible? Because you had no reason to
look? Because you deliberately avoided finding out? Because the insti‐
tution fragmented information so nobody could see the whole? Lack
of knowledge can excuse, mitigate, or itself form part of the wrongdo‐
ing. It depends on the obligation and possibility of finding out.
Foreseeability is not omniscience either. Researchers cannot anticip‐
ate every use two centuries hence. Parents cannot prevent every acci‐
dent. But both may have an obligation to take sufficiently plausible
and important risks seriously. Demanding anticipation of every con‐
sequence would make all action impossible; demanding only avoid‐
ance of absolutely certain harm would leave too much preventable
harm unexamined.
Outcome luck sets a trap. Two drivers are equally careless; a pedestri‐
an appears in front of only one. The actual harm differs and requires
a different response. Yet the difference in outcome does not automat‐
ically reflect an equal difference in culpability [30]. Distinguishing
culpability from the need for reparation lets us recognize the victim
without pretending that chance retrospectively changed the perpet‐
rator's entire intention.
For analysis we propose a framework, not an experimentally valid‐
ated formula: an obligation's extent should be examined through con‐
trol, knowledge, role, contribution to risk, and capacity to remedy.
Power and benefit can create additional reasons to contribute, but
must not automatically become evidence of guilt. Neither wealth nor
poverty is a sufficient moral verdict.
The framework has an important limit. If we asked only the most cap‐
able to help, competence could become a sentence to endless work. If
we asked only the guilty, victims would lack support when the guilty
29


were absent or incapable. We need two stages: resolving the emer‐
gency and subsequently distributing costs fairly. The first must not be
postponed until the second becomes perfect.
Collective responsibility does not mean every group member is
equally guilty for everything the group does. It means some effects
arise through cooperation, rules, and role distribution, and their rem‐
edy requires coordination. An institution can have mechanisms and
obligations not simply reducible to one individual's intention [33]. De‐
cision points must nevertheless be identified, rather than invoking
“the system” as a fog concealing everyone.
When we say everyone is responsible, we may express solidarity or
manufacture impunity. If no one has clear tasks, deadlines, resources,
and an obligation to answer, universality becomes emptiness. A nom‐
inally responsible person without power is not the solution; they are
the possible scapegoat for future failure.
A mature practice of responsibility must allow acknowledgment of
mistakes without completely destroying the person. Otherwise, every
admission becomes too costly and concealment too tempting. This
means neither impunity nor compulsory forgiveness. It means distin‐
guishing victim protection, truth, reparation, sanction, and the possib‐
ility of change instead of compressing them into a single act of con‐
demnation.
Freedom without responsibility can become permission to transfer
costs. Responsibility without freedom can become an obligation to pay
for what you could not decide. A just institution should hold these two
truths together and always notice when they separate: authority
above, blame below; private benefit, reparation left to others.

PART III · FREEDOM IN UNEQUAL LIVES
9. The Child: The Right to Learn to
Be Free
A child breaks a cup and says it was not intentional. The adult replies
that they must learn responsibility. So far, the conversation may be
reasonable. But what exactly must they learn? To be careful? To repair
what they can? To tell the truth? Or to believe every accident proves
they are a bad child?
The answer changes education's meaning. Teaching responsibility can
develop the ability to see consequences and participate in repairing
them. Sweeping blame can turn an act into an identity. We need not
choose between no limits and humiliation. We can stop behavior
without declaring the person worthless.
Children are neither adults' property nor small adults with every ca‐
pacity already formed. The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child
links parental guidance to evolving capacities, while Article 12 recog‐
nizes the right of children capable of forming views to be heard in
matters affecting them, with due weight given to age and maturity
[34]. This is an international normative reference point, not a descrip‐
tion of how every family or country applies it.
Being heard does not mean deciding everything alone. A child may
have genuine reasons against changing schools without being able to
assess every family constraint. Parents can decide otherwise while
still taking those reasons seriously. The difference between authority
and arbitrariness appears in explanation, proportionality, and willing‐
ness to correct the decision, not only in the final outcome.
Children's freedom must be viewed along two axes. There is the
present person, with joys, fears, preferences, and a need for dignity.
There is also the future adult, whose possibilities are affected by
today's education. Protecting only the future can turn childhood into a
construction site with no life of its own. Protecting only immediate
preference can close possibilities children do not yet understand.
31


Imagine a little girl who does not want to learn to read because the ex‐
ercises are frustrating. Saying “it is her choice” may sound respectful
but could abandon an educational responsibility. Coercing her
through shame may obtain the behavior while damaging her relation‐
ship with learning. A third path tries to change the difficulty, pace, and
support while retaining and explaining the objective. This is a
reasoned pedagogical proposal, not a guarantee of identical results for
every child.
Psychology offers useful warnings against hasty verdicts. In an experi‐
ment by Celeste Kidd and colleagues, waiting for a larger reward was
influenced by the immediate experience of an adult's reliability. Chil‐
dren encountering an environment presented as more reliable waited
longer in the food-reward task [35]. A small sample and laboratory
context do not describe all of childhood, but show that waiting can in‐
volve a judgment about promises, not just self-control.
In 2018, Watts, Duncan, and Quan reexamined the relationship
between delaying gratification and later outcomes. Associations de‐
creased considerably after accounting for differences in family envir‐
onment and early abilities [36]. It does not follow that perseverance is
irrelevant. It follows that a test cannot be turned without caution into
a prophecy about character and destiny.
This requires us to reformulate adults' responsibility. Asking children
for patience is insufficient; we must also ask how credible our prom‐
ises are. Asking them to plan is insufficient; a minimum of stability
must make planning intelligible. Children learn more than the de‐
clared rule. They learn the relationship between powerful people's
words and behavior.
Responsibility should grow alongside the power to participate. With
adult help, children can gather the safe pieces of a broken object, con‐
tribute to repairing a relationship, and suggest how to avoid repeti‐
tion. They must not carry the family's financial anxiety or mediate
parental conflicts. Calling them “mature” for taking on disproportion‐
ate burdens may conceal others' withdrawal from their roles.
There is a difference between trust and transferring risk. Trust says: “I
give you an appropriate task and remain available.” Transfer says: “I
declare you capable so I no longer have to answer.” The difference ap‐
32


pears most clearly when something goes wrong. Who stays beside the
child then, and who retrospectively invokes the freedom they claim to
have offered?
Protection must not be idealized either. Parents can eliminate every
small risk and thereby prevent practice in choosing. Adolescents nev‐
er allowed to decide, negotiate, or face proportionate consequences
will have few opportunities to learn these things. Justifiable risk de‐
pends on severity, reversibility, support, and competence; it cannot be
reduced to a magical age valid for every decision.
Children's rights are not a reward for good behavior. Food, protection,
belonging, and respect must not be withdrawn to produce obedience.
Some privileges can depend on behavior without turning fundament‐
al security into a bargaining chip. Otherwise children learn that love
is a disciplinary institution whose regulations change with the adult's
mood.
What happens when a child hurts another child? The perpetrator's
vulnerability does not erase the victim's. We must stop the harm and
protect the affected person, then analyze what understanding and re‐
paration are possible. Empathy for the one who erred cannot become
the victim's obligation to endure repetition. Here the advantage of
separating protection, guilt, and learning becomes clear.
Education for freedom does not produce a child who is always right.
At best, it produces a future adult who can give reasons, hear others'
reasons, and acknowledge an error without feeling they cease to exist.
Their freedom begins when “why?” is no longer automatically treated
as an offense against authority.

10. The Homeless Person: The
Freedom to Have Nowhere to Go
A person sleeps on a bench. Someone says: “At least they are free. No
loan repayments, boss, or schedule.” The image seems generous, al‐
most romantic. But it omits the decisive question: can that person
choose not to sleep on the bench?
Lacking an obligation is not always an advantage. Having no employ‐
ment contract can mean independence or lack of access to income.
Paying no rent can mean ownership or absence of a home. Having no
schedule can mean available time or a day consumed by searching for
the minimum conditions for the next day. The same “has no” can de‐
scribe freedom or deprivation.
Labels must also be separated. A person without housing is not by
definition a beggar, and someone asking for money may have some
shelter. Neither category alone tells us the person's character, abilities,
or history. Describing the situation is more accurate than compressing
the person into a noun claiming to explain everything.
Let us construct Andrei's imaginary case. He lost a job, a relationship,
and then his home. We do not assume this is everyone's typical story
of homelessness. We use it to trace a mechanism: lacking a stable ad‐
dress, somewhere safe for documents, and rest can make opportunit‐
ies existing in principle harder to use. A door open in the morning
does not help equally someone who slept safely and someone who
guarded their belongings all night.
In the language of capabilities, a resource is not identical to the effect‐
ive possibility of turning it into a chosen life [2]. A job advertisement
represents a formal possibility; access to hygiene, transport, and sta‐
bility can distinguish formal possibility from a practicable application.
Observing these conditions does not prove employment the appropri‐
ate immediate solution for everyone. It shows what must be examined
before blame.
34


But what if Andrei also made bad choices? Refused help, lied, or spent
recklessly? We need not falsify his biography to recognize his dignity.
Someone can need support and deserve criticism for an act. The right
to basic protection should not depend on an error-free moral bio‐
graphy; otherwise almost no one would pass the test.
It does not follow that every request must be met exactly as formu‐
lated. Donors may have insufficient information, limited resources, or
reasons to prefer an organization they trust. Institutions may have ob‐
ligations to prevent abuse and protect other beneficiaries. But these
choices must be justified by effects and respect for people, not the
pleasure of policing them morally.
Assistance can contain a relationship of domination. “I offer you food,
but you must adopt my faith.” “I offer shelter, but you may not criti‐
cize the conditions.” “I help you, but will publish your image to show
how good I am.” Even if the immediate need is met, the price may be
submission. Discussing that price does not disparage generosity; it
separates generosity from purchasing vulnerability.
On the other hand, every collective service needs rules. Shelters must
protect people from violence, and their administrators face real limits.
The difference between rules and arbitrariness concerns purpose,
proportionality, consistent application, and the possibility of chal‐
lenge. A safety rule must not be confused with a rule of humiliation;
nor does the existence of abusive rules prove all rules abusive.
A homeless person's freedom includes a right benefactors may find
difficult to accept: not to be grateful in the expected form. Assistance
does not buy emotional obedience. You can set limits on aggressive be‐
havior without making respect conditional on a smile. Recipients do
not become compulsory actors in a moral story about the giver.
The question of social responsibility begins where individual stories
are insufficient. How are the risks of losing housing distributed? What
services exist? What barriers do access rules produce? What happens
to people who do not fit the standard administrative profile? Every
city needs its own data. Prescribing a universal solution solely from
moral intuition is not serious.
35


We can nevertheless formulate criteria for evaluating solutions. An in‐
tervention should be monitored through housing stability, safety, the
possibility of deciding, and effects on life, not merely through people's
disappearance from visible places. Moving suffering out of the city
center is not identical to reducing it. Nor does the number of com‐
pleted forms alone measure regained autonomy.
A real conflict exists over public space. Residents, workers, children,
and homeless people have interests that can overlap or collide. Treat‐
ing it as a duel between good and bad people is an abusive simplifica‐
tion. But a fair policy cannot resolve conflict by requiring one cat‐
egory to stop existing anywhere. Every exclusion rule must face the
question of where an alternative is possible.
What responsibility does Andrei have? As far as his capacity and cir‐
cumstances allow, not to harm others, to participate in reasonable
agreements, and to answer for specific actions. What responsibility
cannot we infer? That he alone authored his condition or must
demonstrate moral superiority to receive support. What responsibility
do others have? To examine their own contributions, possibilities of
assistance, and rules that could make escape from precarity more ac‐
cessible.
Perhaps the most honest test of a city's freedom is not what its richest
person can buy, but what its most dependent person can refuse
without losing the basic means to carry on. The park bench is not a
symbol of freedom from obligations. It may be where society left its
obligations unspecified.

11. The Worker and the Contract
They Can Sign but Cannot Rewrite
“Nobody forces you to work here.” This can be true in an important
sense: employees are not the company's property and can leave. But it
can also halt every discussion of conditions. Freedom to exit is essen‐
tial; the question is how usable it is and whether it can replace every
other protection.
Imagine a worker noticing a safety problem. The supervisor orders
them to continue because delivery cannot be delayed. The worker
knows the risk but controls neither budget, pace, nor procedure. If an
accident occurs, the institution may suddenly discover how “respons‐
ible” the person with the least power to change the situation should
have been.
In this scene, responsibility has been separated from authority. A
fairer allocation asks who set objectives, ignored signals, could stop
the process, and would have borne the cost of stopping. Employees do
not become obligation-free, but their obligations do not absorb man‐
agement's. Signing that you were informed does not prove you also re‐
ceived the power to act on that information.
The Marxian critique of selling labor power draws attention to the dif‐
ference between contractual legal freedom and dependence on access
to the means of existence [18]. Applied cautiously, it helps avoid two
errors: denying the importance of a freely entered contract and as‐
suming its existence concludes the analysis of power.
Not every dependency is domination. Team members depend on one
another to accomplish something they could not produce separately.
Companies need coordination, and every decision cannot be con‐
stantly renegotiated. The issue is whether authority is limited, justifi‐
able, and contestable, or whether one person must live at another's
whim. The republican perspective makes this conceptual difference
visible [3].
37


At least three possibilities matter: being able to leave, to be heard, and
to have protections independent of the boss's liking. Leaving without
an alternative may be too costly. Voice without protection may be dan‐
gerous. Protection without enforcement may be decorative. None
alone resolves the entire relationship.
Where collective bargaining, representation, or cooperation among
workers are proposed as remedies, the argument is that together
people can gain bargaining power they lack individually. But repres‐
entative organizations must also answer to their members. An inter‐
mediary becoming impossible to challenge can reproduce part of the
problem it was meant to solve. This is a normative condition of rep‐
resentation, not a verdict on every actual organization.
Employers' freedom matters too. Initiative, investment, experimenta‐
tion, and the possibility of organizing an activity are important forms
of action. A poorly designed rule can eliminate opportunities, favor
firms able to afford compliance, and prevent new entrants. Protecting
workers therefore requires assessing effects, not merely accumulating
procedures.
Yet producing value does not give an activity a moral blank check.
Economic benefit and risk-taking do not turn employees into exten‐
sions of the owner. Nor does the difficulty of running a company justi‐
fy transferring all uncertainty to those with the smallest reserves.
Fairness means not that nobody ever loses, but that losses are not dis‐
tributed through uncontrollable power.
A subtler case appears when work becomes a total identity. “We are a
family,” says the organization, demanding emotional availability and
sacrifices. Families may involve obligations not calculated contractu‐
ally; companies nevertheless involve exchanges, power, and the pos‐
sibility of dismissal. The metaphor can express authentic care but also
conceal a demand for loyalty the institution does not return in the
same form.
Hence the importance of the right to a life not absorbed by one's role.
Employees can be competent without turning every passion into pro‐
ductivity. They can reject professional ambition without lacking char‐
38


acter. Economic freedom is not only the possibility of working more; it
is also a space in which people need not justify their time through use‐
fulness to someone else.
Responsibility for work quality remains real. Professionals cannot in‐
voke any pressure to justify any negligence. Clients, patients, col‐
leagues, or the public may depend on them. But demands must relate
to resources. If you ask someone to check a hundred things in the time
needed to check ten, setting a standard has not created excellence; it
has created a hidden choice about what will remain unchecked.
A responsible organization should make such tradeoffs visible. Those
deciding the pace should also see the risk. Those demanding a signa‐
ture should also provide information. Those assigning a task should
specify what can be stopped and how. These are not magical solutions
but ways to prevent the person at the chain's end from becoming a
universal absorber of failure.
Who benefits from the language of contractual freedom? Workers
who cannot be forcibly retained and entrepreneurs who can initiate
activities. Who can benefit from its incomplete use? Actors presenting
every imbalance as equal choice. Who benefits from responsibility?
The beneficiaries of work well done. Who can lose? Those assigned ac‐
countability without authority, time, or means.
A good contract should not replace freedom with paternalism. It
should enable cooperation in which refusal, criticism, and personal
limits are not treated as character defects. The final question is not
only whether workers can leave. It is whether they can stay without
being forced to give themselves up.

12. The Citizen: When Obedience
Becomes Irresponsibility
An official applies a rule they consider profoundly unjust. They did
not invent it. They are not its only enforcer. If they refuse, someone
else may replace them. Are they responsible? One convenient formula
says responsibility always rises to the order's author. Another, equally
convenient, says every subordinate is entirely free to refuse. Neither
sufficiently examines the situation.
Citizenship involves participation in a common order, but participa‐
tion is not identical to permanent approval. We can have obligations
to follow fair rules without considering every law moral. In the con‐
tractual tradition, the justification and limits of power themselves per‐
mit discussion of resistance when government betrays its purpose
[14]. The practical issue remains which opposition is justified and at
what cost to others.
Disobedience does not become good merely because it is disobedi‐
ence. It can defend the wronged, but also serve privilege or self-in‐
terest. Serious justification must show the injustice's nature, alternat‐
ives tried, proportionality of means, and protection for those who did
not choose the conflict. Courage without examining effects can be‐
come another form of irresponsibility.
Yet the demand to exhaust every procedure can indefinitely postpone
all change. If a procedure is designed not to respond, invoking it
ceases to be neutral. An institution asking for patience must show
what time does for the affected person, not merely for its own con‐
tinuity. Deadlines and avenues of appeal are, in this sense, compon‐
ents of freedom.
Citizens' responsibility cannot mean expertise in every field. Nobody
can personally check every treaty, statistic, public contract, and tech‐
nology. A division of knowledge is inevitable in a complex society. The
issue is how we choose whom to trust, which signals we check, and
how we correct institutions demanding trust without providing reas‐
ons.
40


Responsible citizens can say “I do not know.” They can refrain from
sharing a claim, seek an accusation's source, and distinguish evidence
from a message's familiarity. But we must not confuse epistemic re‐
sponsibility with permanent vigilance consuming an entire life. If the
information system requires everyone to be a full-time investigator,
the problem is institutional too, not merely one of personal discipline.
Freedom of expression matters precisely because people and authorit‐
ies make mistakes. Mill's defense of discussion includes the value of
confronting opinions in examining truth [17]. But this does not guar‐
antee that every marketplace of messages spontaneously leads to
truth. Amplification power, access to audiences, and the cost of reply‐
ing must be examined separately from the right to speak.
Censorship and criticism are not identical. Neither criticism nor loss
of approval automatically constitutes deprivation of freedom. Yet an
intimidation campaign can make expression practically impossible
even without a state prohibition. Each case requires examining
means, power, consequences, and avenues of defense, not deciding
solely by whether the actor is labeled public or private.
Civic responsibility sometimes includes contributing to common
goods. Rules financing or protecting them can limit uses of individual
resources. The argument from cooperation is not that resources never
belong to individuals, but that a predictable life also depends on insti‐
tutions nobody can produce alone. At the same time, invoking the
common good does not legitimize every expenditure or distribution of
burdens [20].
A particular problem concerns citizens benefiting from an unjust or‐
der without having created it. Benefit does not automatically prove
personal culpability. But it can create a reason to contribute to change,
especially when advantage continues, harm is known, and reasonable
remedies exist. Distinguishing guilt from prospective responsibility
prevents conversation from becoming stuck in defending biographies.
What can we ask of people under threat? Safe observers should not
distribute obligations of heroism as though the risk were theirs. The
cost of refusal, dependents, and likelihood of effect matter. Yet risk
41


does not automatically absolve every collaboration, especially when
people actively help produce serious harm. Here morality ceases to of‐
fer comfort: some situations remain tragic even after careful analysis.
A society should also be assessed by how much extraordinary courage
it demands for decent behavior. If reporting an ordinary mistake re‐
quires risking your entire existence, a few courageous people do not
save the system. The price it demands of truth indicts it.
Citizenship is neither ownership of the state nor unlimited service to
it. People also have relationships, retreats, private projects, and the
right not to turn every hour into mobilization. But that private space
is safer when people and institutions defend its possibility. Freedom to
withdraw cannot be sustained indefinitely through everyone's with‐
drawal.
Who benefits when citizens are called responsible? The community, if
the term means informed participation and checking power. The lead‐
ership, if it means only discipline and silence. The word must be
tested by its direction: does it also oblige those commanding to ex‐
plain, or only those bearing decisions not to cause trouble?
Free citizens are not those who never obey. They are those who can be
asked for reasons and, in turn, have a real right to demand reasons
from power. When only one party must explain, responsibility has
ceased to be reciprocal.

13. Those Who Cannot Decide:
Dignity Without a Test
In a room, three people discuss the future of someone who does not
speak. One says what would be most practical, another what would be
safest, the third what the family wants. The person is present, yet the
conversation proceeds as though they were absent. The first question
is not whether they can sustain a philosophical argument. It is wheth‐
er the others have tried hard enough to discover what they can com‐
municate.
The expression “those who cannot think” improperly combines very
different situations. Inability to speak does not mean inability to
think. Not understanding a complex contract does not mean lacking
preferences about housing, food, or relationships. Needing help with
one decision does not mean being incapable in every domain.
Moreover, some capacities vary over time; careful moral assessment
does not turn a difficulty into a sentence on the whole person.
The philosophy of disability criticizes precisely the reduction of disad‐
vantage to individual deficiency. Environment, accessibility, and how
a person's testimony is received can matter alongside functional limit‐
ations. Neither a purely medical model nor the idea that every diffi‐
culty would disappear by changing society alone captures the di‐
versity of experience [37]. A serious approach must recognize both
care needs and avoidable barriers.
Article 12 of the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabil‐
ities affirms equal recognition of legal capacity and access to support
in exercising it. It requires safeguards against abuse, respect for
rights, will, and preferences, and proportionate measures subject to
review [38]. Interpretation and application in difficult cases are de‐
bated; the principle must not be simplified into an assumption that
nobody can ever need a decision made by others in extreme circum‐
stances.
43


This book does not establish clinical or legal procedures for individual
cases. The philosophical issue is what we owe the person before
reaching such procedures. A minimum answer requires not confusing
disagreement with incapacity, unusual communication with absence
of preferences, or carers' convenience with the interests of those re‐
ceiving care.
Imagine an older woman wanting a daily walk while her family fears
accidents. The risk is real. But eliminating walks entirely also costs
something: a valued activity and a form of choice. Analysis must com‐
pare not “risk” with “nothing” but two sets of effects. Companionship,
adapted routes, or other means may preserve more of her desired life.
If the person cannot understand the relevant risk at all, the obligation
to protect may strengthen. Yet incapacity in one matter does not au‐
thorize total control. Choices they can make must be preserved, and
previously expressed preferences investigated where known and rel‐
evant. Uncertainty is no reason to act as though their life were a blank
page on which the family can write anything.
We thus reach the distinction between a moral agent and a person to‐
ward whom we have moral obligations. Agents can, to some extent,
understand and regulate conduct in relation to reasons and norms.
Those unable to do so can remain beings with interests, vulnerability,
and value. Being able to suffer is not the same as being able to argue
why your suffering matters.
The position defended here is that fundamental protection must not
be earned through cognitive performance. You need not demonstrate
usefulness, economic reciprocity, or verbal talent to avoid abandon‐
ment. Theories of contract and cooperation need a justification broad
enough to include those unable to negotiate mutual advantages; this
difficulty is central to debates on disability and justice [37].
The argument “any of us could end up like this” is important but in‐
sufficient. It can motivate solidarity through shared vulnerability. Yet
even a guarantee that I would never lose a capacity would not make
the suffering of someone who does irrelevant. Their value does not
depend on my probability of taking their place.
44


There are also cases where subjective experience is deeply uncertain.
Here we must avoid both improvised certainties and automatic pro‐
longation of every intervention merely to soothe others' consciences.
Previous wishes, competent assessment, treatment burdens, and pro‐
tection from outside interests become important. Concrete cases re‐
quire expertise and procedures, not a verdict extracted from a page of
philosophy.
What responsibility can someone unable to understand a norm have?
Not the same culpability as someone understanding and deliberately
violating it. But others may need to prevent dangerous behavior. Lim‐
iting risk does not require turning the protected or restrained person
into someone guilty. The distinction between protection and punish‐
ment becomes essential here.
Carers must also be seen. Someone providing continuous support
needs rest, income, relationships, and help. Recognizing a dependent
person's dignity without recognizing their carer's life creates a false
solution: it transfers the entire social duty into one home. Ethics of
care illuminates precisely these relationships and the distribution of
support work [39].
Yet carers' exhaustion must not become devaluation of those receiving
care. The problem may be inadequate organization of assistance, not
someone's “excessive existence.” Solidarity must broaden the circle
supporting the relationship, not ask one party to disappear from it.
Those unable to formulate “I have a right” pose a severe test for the
philosophy of freedom. If freedom matters only when eloquently
claimed, it remains the privilege of those with a voice. A fairer society
must hear subtle preferences, protect interests unable to defend them‐
selves, and recognize that needing others does not mean belonging to
them.

PART IV · WHAT DO WE OWE OTHERS?
14. The Suffering Stranger: What
Can They Ask of Us?
A child is in danger, and you can help at little cost. You did not en‐
danger them. You do not know them. You signed no contract with
their family. Yet the intuition that you should do something seems
strong. Now move the child far away and turn assistance into a contri‐
bution through an institution. What changed morally, and what
changed only psychologically or practically?
Peter Singer's argument in “Famine, Affluence, and Morality” requires
preventing harm when we can do so without sacrificing something
comparably important. Distance and the fact that others could con‐
tribute do not simply eliminate the obligation. Subsequent debate con‐
cerns, among other things, how demanding the principle becomes and
where duty ends [40]. It is a normative argument, not a result ob‐
tained by measuring the brain.
Its force comes from the inconsistency it exposes. If I accept that a
minor interest of mine must yield to a nearby endangered life, I must
explain why an unseen person's life should weigh radically differ‐
ently. Distance can change information, effectiveness, and the causal
relationship to assistance. But alone it seems no reason to diminish
the person's value.
The most serious objection is not that distant suffering is imaginary. It
is that needs are enormous in number. If every personal interest must
yield to greater suffering, one's own life seems to become a perman‐
ent reservoir of resources for others. Holidays, art, time with friends,
and almost any project can appear morally suspect luxuries. We will
address this in the next chapter; an accusation of selfishness cannot
dismiss it.
A defense of individual freedom can start from the distinction
between not harming and being obliged to help. If every other's need
creates a right over my time, in what sense am I still a separate person
46


with my own ends? Some libertarian versions emphasize limits on co‐
ercion and rights over oneself and legitimately acquired property.
This does not necessarily imply generosity has no moral value [21].
The argument matters: assistance's value does not automatically justi‐
fy forcing it in every form. Morally criticizing refusal differs from
building an apparatus capable of unlimited coercion. But the distinc‐
tion between not harming and not helping does not alone tell us what
to do in an easy-rescue case. Nor does it answer situations where our
advantages depend on rules contributing to others' vulnerability.
A contractualist perspective asks whether we can justify a rule per‐
mitting refusal to those affected. Could one reasonably defend a rule
allowing me to avoid a minor inconvenience while leaving someone
else to suffer severely, although intervention would be safe and effect‐
ive? This is not merely adding up happiness. It examines the reasons
each party can invoke and their relative weight.
A care perspective begins elsewhere: life consists of dependency rela‐
tionships, and noticing and responding to need is not a sentimental
appendix to morality. Feminist ethical traditions have criticized care
work's invisibility and the idealization of a wholly independent agent
[39]. But care needs rights and fairness to avoid turning caring people
into compulsory providers for every shortfall.
There can also be justification through benefit and connection. If I live
in an order from which I gain and which contributes to harm, my ob‐
ligation no longer resembles only philanthropy toward a stranger. De‐
bates on global justice distinguish assistance, contribution to harmful
institutions, and obligations of reform [41]. Establishing a concrete
contribution requires evidence; declaring every beneficiary guilty of
every worldwide harm is unfair.
These arguments can converge without becoming identical. One per‐
son may help because suffering matters, another because a rule per‐
mitting refusal would be impossible to justify, another through a
caring relationship or acknowledgment of their own contribution. In‐
stitutions can sometimes operate on practical convergence even when
people do not share a metaphysics.
47


There are reasons not to intervene immediately in the first way that
occurs to us. We may harm through incompetence, encourage an ab‐
usive relationship, needlessly duplicate existing help, or ignore the
person's will. Respect does not demand impulsiveness. It requires tak‐
ing need seriously alongside effectiveness, consent, and the limits of
our expertise. Rejecting a method is not automatically rejecting every
obligation.
Suffering does not grant us general authority over someone's life.
Helping pay for food does not give me a right to control their relation‐
ships. An institution offering support should not collect every intimate
detail merely because the beneficiary is dependent. Good assistance
must attend not only to what it offers but also to the power it creates.
Does responsibility toward suffering limit our freedom? Sometimes,
yes. Accepting an obligation to help makes certain uses of time or re‐
sources no longer morally indifferent. Legitimately translating the ob‐
ligation into a common rule may also involve actual coercion. Denying
the cost by definition—saying only selfishness, not freedom, is limited
—would be dishonest.
Yet cost alone does not decide justification. Prohibiting assault limits
one possibility for aggressors to protect a much more important pos‐
sibility for victims. Similarly, some support obligations can sustain
conditions without which others' freedom remains nominal. The ques‐
tion is not whether any limit arises but whether it is justified, propor‐
tionate, and fairly distributed.
Nor should “help makes us all freer” be exaggerated. Sometimes bene‐
fits are mutual; at other times someone bears a cost without an equi‐
valent gain. Solidarity need not be defended solely through a promise
that everyone immediately benefits. Some things are owed precisely
because vulnerable people cannot offer sufficient return.
The answer we defend is therefore affirmative but not unlimited: we
have strong reasons to recognize obligations toward serious, prevent‐
able suffering, especially when help is accessible, effective, and inex‐
pensive, or when we have a special role or contribution. No general
right of the world to consume our entire life follows. What follows is
that absence of guilt is not morality's endpoint.
48


Strangers need not become my friends for their pain to matter. But
neither must I cease to be a person to take it seriously. The real diffi‐
culty is building a world where this encounter does not depend on the
accidental sacrifice of whoever, one day, did not look away.

15. Too Responsible to Be Free?
A woman finishes her workday, cares for a parent, responds to a
friend in crisis, and contributes to a cause she believes in. That even‐
ing, she reads about other suffering she could reduce. The question is
no longer whether she cares. It is whether she is still allowed to stop.
A morality demanding constant attention can end up penalizing moral
sensitivity itself. The indifferent retain time for themselves; the attent‐
ive always find another obligation. We do not claim every generous
person ends up this way. We observe a structural problem: if respons‐
ibility grows without limit with willingness to notice it, conscientious
people can become bearers of every social backlog.
Philosophy discusses this difficulty under excessive demandingness
and the distinction between duty and what goes beyond it. An act can
be admirable without its omission being culpable. Where to draw the
boundary remains contested, especially before serious preventable
suffering [40]. No short formula can eliminate every difficult case.
An apparent solution is “everyone should do their part.” It respects
fairness among contributors. But what happens when others do not
do their part and someone remains in danger? Can the available per‐
son say “I have met my quota” although they could intervene at little
cost? The victim is not guilty of cooperation's poor distribution.
We must distinguish emergencies from permanent organization. In an
emergency, there may be a strong reason to do more than my fair
share. Over the long term, however, this excess must not become nor‐
mal. Interveners should receive support, costs should be redistrib‐
uted, and the mechanism producing the shortfall changed. Heroism
can resolve a situation; it must not become a staffing plan.
Duty toward those close to us adds another complication. Is it justified
to care for my child before a stranger who might benefit more from
the same hour? Special relationships are not mere valueless accidents.
They create trust, dependency, and promises. Yet preference for those
close to us cannot justify every harm to others. The distance between
natural care and unlimited privilege requires argument.
50


We can view our own life not only as a competing interest but as a
condition for the moral agent's continuity. Rest, friendship, play, and
personal projects are not necessarily tolerable leftovers after the
world has been repaired. They are part of what we seek to protect for
people generally. A morality demanding their abolition among the
willing risks destroying in practice a good it affirms in principle.
The argument has limits. Not every consumption becomes essential
because I call it a “personal project.” If huge resources serve a minor
advantage while a small contribution would prevent serious harm, in‐
voking one's own life may mask convenience. A reasonable limit must
compare the need's severity, cost, effectiveness, existing obligations,
and distribution of contributions.
Then comes manipulation through guilt. Someone may say: “If you do
not do exactly what I ask, you do not care.” The formula erases altern‐
atives and turns disagreement over method into a character verdict.
Responsible people also need freedom to demand evidence, refuse an
unsuitable role, and propose another way to help.
At the same time, we cannot turn every reproach into “manipulation.”
Sometimes others rightly remind us of an obligation we would prefer
to forget. The difference is not whether the message makes us uncom‐
fortable, but whether its reasons are sound, its demand proportionate,
and our person respected. Moral discomfort can be information, but is
insufficient evidence in either direction.
Unequally distributed care can create a false image of independence.
Someone can devote themselves entirely to a profession because an‐
other supports their domestic and relational life. Feminist ethics of
care has made this work and its organization visible [39]. The conclu‐
sion is not that every relationship must be accounted for by the
minute, but that some people's freedom must not be described as
though it had no supporters.
Imagine a couple where one can accept any business trip while the
other organizes every choice around caregiving. Sincere agreements
and different preferences may exist. Yet assessing their freedom re‐
quires examining whether the agreement can be renegotiated, the
work is recognized, and the alternative is practicable. Yesterday's con‐
sent must not become a lifelong sentence to a role.
51


A decent institution should reduce situations in which goodness and
freedom enter extreme conflict. Support services, clear procedures,
shared tasks, and the possibility of asking for help are proposals to be
evaluated through effects and costs. They do not eliminate human de‐
pendency, but can prevent its becoming private captivity.
There is no universal number of hours or perfect moral percentage
resolving every life. We can nevertheless use a checking question:
could people with comparable needs, relationships, and limits sustain
over the long term the demand I consider binding on myself? If not,
perhaps I have discovered not decency's minimum standard but ex‐
ceptional sacrifice or an organization that needs changing.
Freedom to stop is not freedom not to care. It may enable a form of
care that does not exhaust itself in resentment. Mature responsibility
does not mean promising everyone never to disappoint them. It
means making sustainable promises, recognizing real obligations, and
not concealing behind your own endurance the failure of those who
should contribute alongside you.

16. The Researcher: The Right to
Discover and the Obligation to
Answer
A researcher sees two results on screen. The first supports the hypo‐
thesis, the second weakens it. Future funding depends on success.
Nobody explicitly asks them to cheat. Yet they know which result will
be welcomed enthusiastically and which will complicate the story. Re‐
search freedom is at stake not only in spectacular prohibitions, but
also in the cost of saying the world did not confirm our hopes.
Researchers have responsibilities toward society, but not because they
are morally superior. They have them because they produce claims
and tools others may use without being able to verify the entire pro‐
cess themselves. Specialization creates an asymmetry: experts see
risks and limits the public does not. From this asymmetry arise reas‐
ons for transparency and care, not a general right to direct others'
lives.
The first responsibility is epistemic: not presenting mere hope as a
result, distinguishing observation from interpretation, and enabling
verification appropriate to the field. The European Code of Conduct
for Research Integrity, revised by ALLEA in 2023, formulates prin‐
ciples of reliability, honesty, respect, and accountability, assigning
tasks to institutions as well as individual researchers [42]. A code is a
normative reference point; its existence does not guarantee behavior.
Does this responsibility limit some freedoms? Yes: researchers are not
morally free to fabricate data for a cause they consider good. But evid‐
ential discipline also enables a deeper intellectual freedom. Without
it, debate is occupied by those able to produce the most convenient ap‐
pearances. Freedom to investigate does not mean immunity from de‐
mands to show a claim's origin.
The second responsibility concerns research participants. The 1979
Belmont Report centers respect for persons, beneficence, and justice,
discussing consent, risks, and distribution of research burdens [43].
53


Scientific interest does not turn participants into available material.
Nor does promised future benefit justify every cost imposed on those
who did not choose it.
The third concerns foreseeable consequences of the tools created.
Imagine a method that can improve medical analysis but also enable
discriminatory surveillance. It does not automatically follow that the
method must be banned or published without precautions. Analysis
must address risk severity, accessibility of harmful use, beneficial
use's value, possible restrictions, and who bears errors.
Responsibility for dual use cannot be reduced to an individual oath.
Researchers can modify publication of details, seek independent as‐
sessment, or warn of limits, but funding institutions, implementing
companies, and authorizing bodies have their own decisions. If every
obligation is pushed onto a paper's author, others' power becomes in‐
visible.
Nor may society demand only comfortable results from researchers.
Social responsibility does not mean confirming dominant ideology,
funders' agendas, or majority preferences. Sometimes the most im‐
portant contribution is an unpleasant, well-supported claim. Protect‐
ing academic freedom and demanding integrity are not adversaries;
both are needed so pressure does not decide truth.
Heroic rhetoric nevertheless has a risk: authors can portray every
methodological criticism as persecution and every funding refusal as
censorship. Not every project has a right to public resources, just as
institutional success does not prove scientific value. Assessment cri‐
teria must be justified, open to criticism, and adapted to objectives
without confusing popularity with truth.
Basic research presents a special difficulty. We cannot require every
project to demonstrate its exact ultimate utility beforehand, because
exploration involves the unknown. But the unknown does not exempt
rigor: we can demand clear questions, appropriate methods, examina‐
tion of hypotheses, and reporting what failed. Uncertainty of outcome
and seriousness of process must be assessed separately.
Researchers may also bear responsibility for communication. If a
small study becomes “proof that people are not free” in a headline, a
leap has occurred between finding and narrative. If correlation be‐
54


comes individual destiny, an essential limit has been lost. Good com‐
munication means not eliminating uncertainty to avoid confusing the
public, but translating it into intelligible form.
This book itself must be judged by the same criterion. A philosophical
example does not become fact because it convinces. A cited study does
not validate every normative conclusion built around it. A claim about
what we should do that uses empirical findings also needs moral
premises: why the effect matters, who has rights, and what cost is ac‐
ceptable. Science can inform these choices without producing every
value alone.
Those paying for research have a legitimate interest in resource use,
but do not acquire ownership of truth. Participants have a legitimate
interest in protection, but cannot demand altered results to avoid all
discomfort. Technology's beneficiaries may have reasons to help rem‐
edy its damage, even without causing every error. We have different
roles, not one universal, undifferentiated obligation.
Institutions invoking responsibility should create its conditions: time
for verification, data retention under relevant rules, protection for
honest reporting of problems, and public correction of errors. ALLEA
treats the research environment and institutional practices as parts of
integrity [42]. Demanding methodological heroism in a system re‐
warding only victory stories is insufficient.
There is also responsibility to acknowledge the limits of one's expert‐
ise. Excellence does not make a physicist an ultimate authority in eth‐
ics. Philosophers cannot determine a treatment's efficacy through an
argument's elegance. Expertise has a domain and conditions. Prestige
transferred without examination can become intellectual domination.
But expertise's limits must not silence experts as citizens. Researchers
can contribute to public discussion while specifying the role from
which they speak. They can offer data, formulate hypotheses, and de‐
fend values. Responsibility also means not blending these activities so
personal opinion appears an inevitable consequence of the laborat‐
ory.
55


The final answer is therefore twofold. Researchers owe society care
for truth, people, and plausible consequences; society owes them con‐
ditions in which truth can be discovered and spoken. Research free‐
dom is not a caste privilege. It is a precious public possibility, corrup‐
ted both by ideological coercion and by evasion of accountability.

17. The Philosopher: What Do We
Owe Because We Can Think?
Someone spends an evening discussing whether we have obligations
toward those who suffer. On leaving, they encounter a person asking
for help. Does having thought more about the problem make them
more responsible? Perhaps they know more arguments. Perhaps they
also know more elegant ways to evade their conclusions.
The capacity to philosophize does not automatically produce virtue. It
offers tools for clarification but also rationalization. An argument can
discover a real inconsistency or invent a convenient exception for its
author. The question is not whether thought has value but what oblig‐
ations arise from possessing a tool that can change how people justify
power and conduct.
The first obligation is not to exploit confusion. If a word has several
meanings, say which you use. Do not begin with freedom as absence
of violence and end, without warning, with freedom as personal satis‐
faction. Do not start from causal responsibility to assign blame, or in‐
voke absence of blame to deny every duty to help. Clarity does not re‐
solve every conflict, but prevents some victories obtained by changing
the rules during the game.
The second obligation is to address opponents at their strongest. De‐
fenders of limits on assistance are not necessarily indifferent to suf‐
fering. Those demanding redistribution are not necessarily hostile to
freedom. Religious people are not incapable of doubt, and skeptics are
not automatically immoral. Without this discipline, philosophy be‐
comes a more sophisticated way to speak only to those approving us.
The third is not to confuse ingenuity with importance. We can invent
spectacular paradoxes that make us forget ordinary but serious prob‐
lems. A thought experiment's value also depends on what it illumin‐
ates. If a scenario simplifies someone until their real life becomes ir‐
relevant, the solution's elegance may be purchased by eliminating the
difficulty.
57


This is not a demand for every idea's immediate utility. Abstract re‐
flection may later change the language in which we recognize prob‐
lems. No philosopher can guarantee a question's effect beforehand.
But they can explain why it deserves inquiry, what it assumes, and
what would count as an objection. Freedom to explore must not be
confused with a right to demand admiration for every obscurity.
Who can philosophize? Not only degree holders. Parents wondering
whether their protection stifles a child, workers discussing an order's
legitimacy, and poor people challenging how they are judged formu‐
late philosophical questions. Academic expertise can bring history
and precision, but does not monopolize the ability to notice injustice.
There is nevertheless an advantage in time available for reflection.
Those able to read, compare, and write without immediate survival
pressure possess a possibility others lack to the same extent. Their en‐
tire life does not thereby become a debt to repay. There is a reason for
modesty and sometimes accessibility: not turning received resources
into walls of prestige.
Philosophers can respond socially through teaching, writing, particip‐
ating in deliberation, or constructing tools of criticism. Not everyone
must choose the same path. The obligation to contribute must not
eliminate diverse talents and projects. But professional freedom does
not justify indifference toward uses of one's arguments when effects
are plausible and near.
Responsibility for an argument differs from responsibility for every
reader who distorts it. Authors cannot control every interpretation.
They can avoid foreseeably dangerous ambiguities, correct uses re‐
versing their meaning, and refuse to present fragile claims as definit‐
ive truth. Demanding more could impose impossible self-censorship;
demanding less would leave communication unaccountable.
Philosophy can also become an alibi for delay. “The problem is com‐
plex” can be true and simultaneously used to avoid obvious help. We
need no complete theory of justice before opening a heavy door for
someone unable to open it. Some local obligations remain sound even
when ultimate foundations are disputed.
58


Conversely, moral certainty can become an alibi against thought. “We
already know what is good” can close investigation of a policy's con‐
sequences. Right intention does not guarantee method. Responsible
philosophers must hold together urgency of action and willingness to
correct, without using either to cancel the other.
For Sartre, retreat into a role can conceal choice and personal parti‐
cipation [19]. Intellectuals' roles may say: “I only formulate ideas.”
Sometimes this boundary is legitimate; sometimes it conceals a contri‐
bution to legitimizing harm. We must examine what was said, in
which context, with what intention and possibilities of anticipation,
not distribute guilt by mere association.
Those unable to sustain complex reasoning do not owe silence to
those who can. The ability to win a dispute is no title of ownership
over another's life. A decent justificatory system must explain, as far
as possible, to those affected why it demands something. When ex‐
planation becomes inaccessible, the need for representation and over‐
sight grows rather than shrinks.
This book's position is that cognitive advantage creates primarily an
obligation of honesty, not unlimited moral authority. Seeing more op‐
tions may make some omissions harder to excuse. But nobody must
become a universal savior because they can think. Philosophers too
remain finite people with lives they are not obliged to consume en‐
tirely in an abstraction's name.
Perhaps philosophy's best test is not whether it leaves us doubt-free,
but whether it makes our own justifications harder to fool us with.
Leaving the conversation, the person encountering suffering needs
more than a theory. They must recognize when theory stopped clarify‐
ing and began protecting them from answering.

18. God Before His Own Creation
Imagine a being able to create a world inhabited by creatures capable
of suffering. It knows many consequences beforehand and can modify
the world's rules. What responsibility does it have? Does creating the
creatures give it unlimited rights over them, or instead produce a spe‐
cial obligation toward the vulnerability it made possible?
The question proves neither God's existence nor nonexistence. It ex‐
amines the consequences of hypotheses. We must distinguish a finite
god, very powerful but limited, from classical theism's God, described
through attributes such as omnipotence, omniscience, and perfect
goodness. Religious traditions do not all understand these attributes
identically, and some conceptions of divinity do not fit at all the model
of a personal agent deciding events.
For a finite god, assessment may partly resemble assessing a very
powerful ruler. What did it know? What could it prevent? What risks
did it create? What would intervention have cost? The greater the
power and knowledge, the less available some human excuses be‐
come. But unlimited responsibility does not follow if the being has
real limits acknowledged by the hypothesis.
For an omnipotent, perfectly good being, the difficulty intensifies. The
problem of evil asks how evil and suffering coexist with such attrib‐
utes. The literature distinguishes the logical problem, concerning com‐
patibility, from evidential arguments asking how plausible such a
God's existence remains in light of observed evil [44]. Rejecting a strict
contradiction does not mean explaining every concrete suffering.
A free-will defense holds that the existence of free agents may permit
moral evil. In the formulation associated with Alvin Plantinga, a de‐
fense seeks to show possible compatibility, not necessarily describe
God's actual reasons. Theodicies invoking character formation and re‐
sponses based on human knowledge's limits attempt other routes,
each with its difficulties [44]. Here our analysis begins, rather than
ends.
60


The first question is whose freedom is protected when evil is permit‐
ted. If aggressors exercise their will, victims may lose safety, bodily in‐
tegrity, and a future. A freedom-based justification must explain not
only the value of aggressors' choice but the cost imposed on victims'
freedom. Otherwise “freedom” is applied selectively.
Moreover, stopping aggression does not seem to eliminate all the ag‐
gressor's moral possibilities. In human life, we can prevent an inten‐
tion's execution without erasing it from the mind. Someone can desire
harm and be unable to accomplish it. By analogy, the will's value
alone does not immediately demonstrate the necessity of every suc‐
cessful evil act. Theistic defenders may invoke intervention's global ef‐
fects, but these require argument, not assumption.
The second question concerns suffering not directly resulting from a
human agent's choice: illness, catastrophes, organisms' vulnerability.
A world of stable laws may matter for action, learning, and responsib‐
ility. Yet stability's importance does not automatically imply that pre‐
cisely this level and distribution of suffering are necessary. Could oth‐
er coherent rules preserve the relevant goods at lower cost? The ques‐
tion remains.
We cannot construct an easy proof merely by imagining a pleasanter
world. We may not see every relationship between conditions, con‐
sequences, and life's possibility. But invoking complexity alone like‐
wise does not prove the existing world optimal or justified. It shows a
limit of knowledge requiring symmetrical application: we know
neither all objections nor all justifications.
The third question concerns the idea that suffering builds character.
Some difficulties offer opportunities for courage, solidarity, and mat‐
uration. But an opportunity is not a complete justification. Who may
impose serious harm on someone to give a third person the chance to
be generous? Victims cannot be reduced to the universe's teaching
material.
Religious defenders may reply that life does not end with visible exist‐
ence and that a broader perspective changes assessment. This is a pos‐
sibility internal to some beliefs, not a fact established by this philo‐
sophical argument. Even accepting future compensation, it remains
61


debatable whether subsequent reparation justifies every harm per‐
mitted now. In human relationships, promised compensation grants
no general right to harm.
Perhaps the analogy with human relationships is mistaken? A theolo‐
gian may argue that the creator–creature relationship is incompar‐
able to that between two finite persons. The objection deserves seri‐
ous attention. But if divine goodness becomes wholly detached from
every intelligible meaning of care and justice, another difficulty
arises: what do we actually assert when saying God is good? A differ‐
ence of scale must not be confused without argument with reversing
moral meaning.
Power is not legitimacy. A being's ability to punish does not prove the
punishment just. Its ability to create does not prove the creature mere
property. In a thought experiment, an inventor producing beings with
subjective experience would have reasons for responsibility precisely
because they created those beings' dependence. The analogy does not
settle theology, but shows why “I made you” alone is an insufficient
moral argument.
There is also the problem of divine freedom. Can a perfectly good be‐
ing choose evil? If not, is it less free? The answer depends on the no‐
tion of freedom. If freedom means every logically imaginable alternat‐
ive, evil's impossibility seems a limit. If it means uncoerced action ac‐
cording to a perfectly rational and good nature, that limit may not be
interpreted as deprivation. Once again, the dispute shows why we
cannot use the word without defining it [22].
Knowledge of the future adds another tension. Knowing what
someone will do is not identical to causing their action. Yet supposedly
infallible knowledge raises questions about an alternative future's
possibility. Neither “it knows, therefore it compels” nor “it only
knows, therefore no problem exists” is enough. Compatibility between
knowledge, time, and freedom requires a more precise model than
these formulas offer.
What practical conclusion can we draw without deciding between be‐
lief and nonbelief? If someone can reduce suffering, a possible divine
justification of the world does not automatically justify inaction. Their
62


intervention may belong to the good the tradition demands. And if
God does not exist, absence of a cosmic guarantor makes suffering no
less real and assistance no less important.
Likewise, someone's invocation of divine will does not automatically
grant the right to control others. The assertion remains one made by a
finite person in a world of interpretation and disagreement. Religious
freedom must include freedom from religious coercion. Faith can mo‐
tivate care; it must not substitute for publicly justifying power.
We have resolved neither God's existence nor the problem of evil
here. We have identified a criterion surviving a change of scale: pos‐
sessing power over vulnerable beings does not eliminate the need for
justification. It may make it more urgent. If this criterion seems too
severe for gods, we should at least be cautious when granting humans
exceptions we could not defend even before a suffering child.

PART V · REALITY, POWER, AND PRICE
19. Is Freedom Real or Merely a
Useful Story?
If we sought freedom in a brain, we would find tissues and processes.
In a constitution, we would find signs. On the street, we would see
people and behavior. Would that mean freedom does not exist? Only if
we assumed everything real must exist like a stone.
A promise has no weight in itself, yet can change behavior. A border is
not merely a natural line, yet can stop someone. Money, offices, and
rules are classic examples of social realities whose existence depends,
in disputed ways, on practices, institutions, and recognition [33]. Be‐
ing socially constructed does not mean being effectless or subject to
individual imagination's whim.
Freedom must be examined on several levels. There are capacities
and possibilities: I can walk, understand, refuse, choose. There are in‐
stitutional relationships: I have a right, protection, an avenue of ap‐
peal. There is subjective experience: I feel the author of my action.
There is also normative judgment: we consider these possibilities de‐
serving protection. No level can be wholly replaced by another.
Feeling free does not guarantee freedom's existence. I might feel un‐
restricted because I do not know the barriers or dare not imagine al‐
ternatives. Conversely, I may feel the burden of an endorsed obliga‐
tion without being dominated. Experience matters, but must be com‐
pared with options, resources, and power relations.
Declared rights likewise do not guarantee effective possibility. An in‐
stitution may say anyone can appeal, but the procedure may cost
more than the person can afford. Showing the paper is insufficient;
we must observe use. At the same time, a right's practical uselessness
in a context does not prove its formulation worthless. It can provide a
basis for challenging that context.
Can freedom be measured? Some components can: access time and
cost, prohibitions' existence, ability to change providers, frequency of
reprisals within a documented setting. We can describe decisions
64


where the person is heard and situations where they are not. But ag‐
gregating these into one score requires weights and judgments. No
natural unit tells us how many commercial options compensate for
losing freedom of conscience.
This measurement limit does not make analysis useless. Temperature
and pain are measured differently, yet both can matter. For freedom, a
multidimensional picture may be more honest than rankings conceal‐
ing tradeoffs. Above all, distribution must be examined: a high aver‐
age can coexist with a minority's practical captivity.
Responsibility also has social reality. Saying “this task falls to you” can
change expectations, behavior, sanctions, and trust. But social effects
do not prove moral justification. An unjust rule can be very real. A so‐
ciety's assignment of blame to a group does not make that assignment
true or legitimate.
Here we encounter the metaethical question: do we discover moral
truths independent of our opinions, or construct principles through
reason, agreement, and practice? We will not settle this dispute by de‐
claring it meaningless. For this book, observing that both families of
positions can distinguish a rule from its justification is enough. Lack
of unanimity does not make all arguments equivalent.
For instance, “the powerful may do anything” may be a stable rule in
a context, but faces the objection of the person exposed to arbitrari‐
ness. “Nobody need justify restricting another” eliminates the very
framework within which those affected could challenge it. A defense
of freedom can start from shared vulnerability and a demand for
reasons irreducible to force, without claiming to have discovered a
moral particle.
But what if freedom is merely a useful story for coordination? Then
we must ask useful to whom and in what sense. A story making
people accept obligations can serve cooperation or exploitation. Utility
is an insufficient verdict until we specify beneficiaries, costs, and pos‐
sibilities of challenge. A system can work well for administrators and
badly for those administered.
Some realities would also persist without the word “freedom.” Anim‐
als can be captive, people prevented from leaving, individuals with or
without alternatives. But vocabulary changes what we can identify,
65


claim, and justify together. Calling a situation “domination” instead of
“protection” can open a different moral inquiry; the label does not
guarantee the inquiry is right, but can make the question possible.
It would therefore be too simple to say freedom was invented and for
that reason can be canceled without loss. Humans also invented pro‐
cedures for recognizing injustice, repairing harm, and limiting power.
Their value must be argued through what they protect and how they
can be justified, not denied because of their human origin.
It would be equally simple to treat freedom as an eternal object with
one obvious definition merely discovered by history. We have seen
that definitions select different aspects and can serve different in‐
terests. The concept must be corrected through contact with experi‐
ences earlier formulations excluded. Universality does not mean the
first formulation is untouchable.
The position adopted here is practical realism in a descriptive sense,
not the name of a new philosophical school: possibilities, constraints,
and dependencies are real; concepts organizing them are constructed
and revisable; justifying norms remains an argumentative task. We
cannot abolish captivity by renaming it, but can begin abolishing it by
finding words that make it contestable.
The good question, then, is not only whether freedom exists. It is what
reality we recognize, what evidence we demand, and what we lose by
denying it. Sometimes those calling freedom a fiction do not deny that
people are locked up. They deny only that they should explain the
locked door.

20. Who Gains and Who Loses
from These Concepts?
Imagine a company, a family, and a state using the same sentence:
“You are free, therefore responsible.” In each case it can affirm per‐
sonal dignity: you are not an object; your actions matter. But it can
also conceal withdrawal of obligations: we called your situation a
choice, so everything following concerns you alone.
We must distinguish the benefits of justified principles, the benefits of
selectively defining them, and the benefits of merely invoking words
while practice contradicts them. Concept, application, and propa‐
ganda are not the same.
Freedom as protection most obviously benefits those exposed to arbit‐
rary power. They gain the right to refuse, challenge, and pursue their
own projects. The loser holds unjustified power. But that loss is not a
symmetrical injustice. Losing the possibility of coercing another per‐
son is not the same as losing protection against coercion.
There are mutual gains too. A predictable rule can enable cooperation
between people otherwise afraid of each other. Promises, professional
responsibilities, and remedies can make trust possible. In such cases,
limiting actions does more than redistribute a fixed good; it can create
conditions for more people to pursue their ends. This possibility is ex‐
plicable through cooperation's mechanisms, not proof that every rule
produces common gain.
Freedom of property provides space for projects and independence.
But property also distributes power to exclude. Analysis noticing only
owners' freedom may omit restrictions on those without access. Con‐
versely, analysis seeing only exclusion may omit property's security
and capacity for planning. The moral dispute concerns legitimate title,
limits, alternatives, and institutions, not a single label [21].
Retrospective responsibility can benefit victims by enabling demands
for explanations and reparation. But it can also benefit an institution
choosing a convenient perpetrator to avoid examining its own de‐
67


cisions. An accident portrayed solely as the last employee's careless‐
ness can leave untouched the system of incentives and pressures con‐
tributing to it.
Prospective responsibility can benefit those in need even without an
identifiable culprit. But it can disproportionately burden caring
people, relatives, or nearby professionals. Availability does not prove
someone must become permanently responsible. Proximity may cre‐
ate an initial obligation to intervene; it does not alone justify perman‐
ently allocating the cost.
Freedom of expression benefits dissenters, researchers, artists, and
critical citizens. It can also benefit powerful actors with far greater
amplification resources. The solution is not suppressing freedom in
the name of equalization, but examining access, transparency, and
possibilities of reply. Equal rights to speak and equal power to be
heard are different problems.
In families, these concepts can protect children and dependent part‐
ners. But “responsibility toward the family” can prevent exit from ab‐
use. Someone else can use “personal freedom” to abandon without ex‐
planation commitments on which vulnerable people depend. Neither
connection nor exit should be absolutized. Safety, promises, alternat‐
ives, and affected people must be considered.
For homeless people, formal freedom can matter: it protects against
becoming an institution's property. But invoking it can also serve re‐
fusal to create real possibilities. “They can go anywhere” does not help
when every place is practically inaccessible. Conversely, social re‐
sponsibility can justify support or invasive surveillance of beneficiar‐
ies. Remedies must be examined through the power they produce.
For disabled people, autonomy can mean recognition of their own
voice, while protection can provide access and safety. But a definition
reserving freedom for cognitive performance can exclude them, and
care defined without rights can infantilize them. Real gain lies in com‐
bining support and respect, not compulsory choice between abandon‐
ment and control.
68


For researchers, freedom enables exploration and responsibility en‐
ables credibility. But funders may call compliance with their agenda
“responsibility,” while researchers call refusal of all verification “free‐
dom.” Both abuses turn a public good into a private shield. The dis‐
pute must return to evidence, roles, and possibilities of correction.
For power, one great advantage of moral vocabulary is that it can
move control inside the person. An order says “do this.” One use of re‐
sponsibility says “a good person would do this without being asked.”
The latter can sustain authentic cooperation but also submission
without visible orders. Failing to see a prohibition is insufficient; we
must observe conditions under which refusal remains possible.
Those setting definitions thus wield power over distributing admira‐
tion and shame. If success is defined exclusively as merit, luck's bene‐
ficiaries may receive more authority than the facts justify. If failure is
defined exclusively as a structural effect, we may deny personal con‐
tribution and obligations of reparation. Serious analysis does not re‐
place one convenient determinism with another.
Another beneficiary is invisible: those who need not name their de‐
pendencies. They appear self-sufficient because supporting work and
infrastructure remain in the background. Asking who benefits must
also track what goes uncounted: carers' time, implementers' risk, the
cost borne by those waiting, and the voices of those not invited to
define the problem.
We can empirically test language's effects without claiming an experi‐
ment resolves morality. We could randomly assign participants to as‐
sess the same case presented as “individual choice,” “contextual con‐
straints,” or without a label. We would measure blame attribution,
support for assistance, and justifications, subsequently varying the
act's severity and the person's control. This is a research proposal, not
a result reported here.
Such a test might show vocabulary influences judgment. It would not
automatically establish which judgment is correct. That requires ex‐
amining relevant information and moral arguments. We must also
avoid concluding that interest explains every conviction: someone can
sincerely defend a principle reducing their advantages, and an argu‐
ment can be valid even if its author benefits from it.
69


“Who benefits?” is therefore a checking tool, not a universal method
of unmasking. Using it to reject every idea through its author's sup‐
posed interest abandons argument. Never using it risks confusing con‐
venient definitions with neutral truths.
The most dangerous combination concentrates freedom among the
powerful and responsibility among the vulnerable. The most prom‐
ising expands the possibility of refusal while bringing obligations
closer to knowledge, control, and benefit. This orientation produces
no perfect formula either. But it shows where to look when a society
speaks beautifully of principles and distributes consequences badly.

21. Freedom Administered by
Machines
Suppose a system organizes your life so well you hardly need to
choose. It suggests jobs, information, relationships, routes, and pur‐
chases. It understands you enough to avoid many errors. Are you
freer because you save time, or less free because someone else selects
your possible world?
The scenario is deliberately hypothetical. It assumes neither an omni‐
scient system nor correct predictions. It reveals a problem: technology
can expand capacity for action while concentrating power over op‐
tions. Efficiency and autonomy can converge, but are not identical.
An assistant translating, explaining a form, or organizing information
can reduce a barrier. Someone previously wholly dependent on an in‐
termediary can gain control. But if the assistant conceals alternatives,
pursues unknown goals, or makes refusal costly, support can become
dependency. The question is not only how capable the system is, but
on whose behalf it uses that capacity.
Philosophies of relational autonomy show that independence must
not be confused with isolation: support and relationships can enable
self-government, while other relationships undermine it [45]. Applied
to technology, this distinction prevents assuming either that all auto‐
mated help enslaves us or that every reduction in effort liberates us.
Imagine a platform offering the same correct recommendation in two
ways. In the first, it shows relevant reasons, uncertainty, and alternat‐
ives, and users can refuse. In the second, it conceals having excluded
options that generate no platform revenue. Even if the final recom‐
mendation proves good in one case, the power structure differs. A fa‐
vorable outcome does not cancel the need for control over the pro‐
cess.
Another trap is responsibility through the last click. A person must
confirm a complex recommendation without time or information to
verify it. After an error, the organization says the decision was human.
71


But a hand on a button does not prove meaningful deliberation. Con‐
trol must be assessed through real possibilities of understanding, re‐
fusal, and intervention, not merely formal confirmation.
This problem is not exclusively technological. An administrative form
can transfer risk in the same way. In our scenario, however, techno‐
logy can reproduce it at scale and obscure it through complexity. Re‐
sponsibility must therefore be traced to those choosing objectives,
data, usage rules, and oversight conditions.
Delegation to a system does not eliminate creators' and operators' ob‐
ligations. If an organization uses a tool for an activity affecting lives,
choosing that tool is itself a decision. It does not follow that every de‐
veloper answers identically for every use. It follows that “the system
decided” does not conclude causal and moral analysis.
We can also imagine a highly persuasive system helping justify our
choices. It can identify real objections or produce, on request, the ex‐
planation absolving us. Cognitive freedom is more than access to an‐
swers. It is the possibility of encountering resistance from facts and
arguments, even when they contradict our preferred self-image.
Privacy here has value beyond concealing secrets. A space to explore
ideas, err, and reconsider without permanent assessment can protect
judgment's formation. In a hypothetical world where every hesitation
immediately produces a score, people may select publishable thoughts
before examining them. This is a potential risk requiring research, not
a universal diagnosis of technology users.
Total transparency is no simple solution either. Making every personal
detail visible can increase institutional power over people. More rel‐
evant is a justified asymmetry: those exercising authority should ex‐
plain decisions, while those affected should not have to expose their
entire lives to challenge them. Holding power accountable and sur‐
veilling the vulnerable are not the same project.
If one day artificial systems had relevant forms of experience, in‐
terests, or moral agency, additional questions would arise about their
own rights and responsibilities. This book assumes neither that exist‐
ing systems meet that condition nor that verbal competence alone
proves it. Producing an excuse is not alone evidence that someone ex‐
ists who morally understands excuses.
72


For the present argument, the criterion is simpler: technology should
also be judged by how much justifiable control it offers affected
people, not only by performance. Can I learn it intervened? Correct
relevant data? Challenge an outcome? Choose a reasonable alternat‐
ive? Who answers when I lack the means to discover what happened?
Automation can free time for research, care, and creation. It can also
turn people into nominal supervisors of processes they cannot direct.
The difference is not inevitably inscribed in “intelligence.” Architec‐
ture, institutions, and power distribution produce it. A machine easing
our lives is a gain; a machine invoked to make responsibility unlocat‐
able is a cover.

22. A Society That Does Not
Demand Heroism Every Morning
In an imaginary society, decent people must constantly be extraordin‐
ary. Teachers compensate for resource shortages with sleepless nights.
Workers risk everything to report danger. A daughter abandons her
life to care for a parent. Researchers sacrifice their jobs to publish un‐
comfortable findings. Society praises them and carries on unchanged.
The praise may be deserved. But stopping there makes heroism a
mechanism through which institutions avoid change. A decent order
should not make kindness useless; it should reduce situations where
ordinary moral behavior demands exceptional sacrifices.
From this book's analysis we can formulate a guiding principle: basic
freedom should be widely distributed, and additional responsibility
brought closer to control, knowledge, role, and remedial capacity. This
is no equation and produces no automatic policy. It is a rule of exam‐
ination preventing two shortcuts: “everyone is equally responsible”
and “only whoever pressed the last button answers.”
The first domain of application is protecting refusal. People should not
lose every means of existence to say no to abuse. This does not mean
every refusal must be cost-free. It means distinguishing justified con‐
sequences from reprisals making freedom nominal. In family, work,
research, and administration, exit and challenge must be real possibil‐
ities.
The second is distributing support. If care needs are treated solely as
private affairs, freedom may depend on family luck and one mem‐
ber's sacrifice. Organizational alternatives must be assessed through
access, continuity, quality, cost, and respect for preferences. Moving a
service from family to institution is insufficient if the person becomes
less heard there.
The third is education for judgment. A school of freedom should
provide appropriate choices, ask for reasons, and allow revision. It
should distinguish error from fraud, misunderstanding from ill will,
74


and resource shortage from lack of interest. These normative propos‐
als require pedagogical testing; we claim no single method can
achieve them.
A concrete test could compare two ways of handling mistakes in a
school project: one focused exclusively on sanctions, another on ex‐
planation, reparation, and a new plan. Assessment should track not
only immediate obedience but honest reporting, understanding con‐
sequences, recurrence of error, and protection of those affected.
Without such criteria, “autonomy” and “responsibility” risk remaining
pedagogical labels.
The fourth is organizing work and expertise. Tasks must come with
proportionate resources and authority. Those responsible for safety
need an effective way to stop unsafe processes. Those responsible for
a result's truth must be able to report unfavorable findings. Those re‐
sponsible for care must be able to request help before exhaustion.
The fifth is a culture of correction. Not every error deserves the same
reaction, nor does every acknowledgment conclude reparative obliga‐
tions. Mature institutions can say simultaneously: “Thank you for re‐
porting” and “We must remedy this.” Separating investigation from
public humiliation can promote truth-finding, but must preserve ac‐
countability for deliberate concealment and victim protection. This is
a design objective, not an excuse for opacity.
The sixth is making costs visible. Proposing a new freedom requires
asking who bears the risk. Proposing a new obligation requires asking
who receives time and means for it. Invoking efficiency requires ex‐
amining whether we reduced total cost or merely shifted it to people
with less power to protest.
This examination must include future generations. They cannot con‐
sent to our decisions or negotiate with us. We do not know their exact
preferences, but have reasons to preserve general conditions for lives
in which choices remain possible. Arguments about global justice and
responsibility across borders or generations show why nonparticipa‐
tion in a present contract does not automatically eliminate affected in‐
terests' importance [41].
75


Yet the future can be invoked abusively. A grand promise for hypo‐
thetical people does not justify every sacrifice imposed on existing
ones. We must compare present harm's certainty, benefit's plausibility,
the decision's reversibility, and alternatives. Respecting the future
does not mean turning the present into material without rights.
How do we know reform increases freedom? Not merely by asking its
administrators. Those affected must be heard, including those who do
not use it, abandoned it, or cannot formulate a complaint in the expec‐
ted language. Unintended effects and cases where protection produces
new dependency must be sought. A reform admitting no failure criter‐
ia demands faith, not evaluation.
A checking framework could track refusal's cost, access to alternat‐
ives, time lost obtaining a right, distribution of care tasks, responses to
appeals, and possibilities of repairing mistakes. These indicators are
no complete moral measure and must not be blindly added into one
score. They make visible questions solemn declarations can conceal.
There is also space a just society must leave unprogrammed. People
are not only producers of benefits, carers, citizens, or bearers of pro‐
jects. They also sit, listen to music, exchange an idea without results,
change their minds, or spend time in ways no indicator can justify. If
every moment must be optimized morally or economically, freedom
has become a performance task.
Protecting this space does not deny obligations. It recognizes that co‐
operation's purpose is not absorbing the person into cooperation. We
want institutions precisely so we need not negotiate every condition
of existence alone each morning, or save one another exclusively
through exhaustion.
This book offers no administrative paradise. Every system produces
conflicts between interests, costs, and values. Some choices will re‐
main tragic; some obligations heavy. What we can demand is that con‐
flicts be visible, contestable, and distributed without turning vulner‐
ability into guilt or power into a shield.
A free society is not one where nobody owes anything. It is one where
obligations can be justified to those asked to fulfill them, freedoms do
not depend on others' arbitrary goodwill, and those unable to defend
76


themselves remain within the circle of consideration. Its deepest re‐
sponsibility is to make room for lives that need not constantly prove
they deserve to be lived.

Epilogue. The Freedom Not to
Look Away
Let us return to the opening door. Someone has fallen. Four people are
nearby. They have unequal power, information, and costs of delay. Yet
none must be reduced to a label: the capable person, the dependent
person, the child, the frightened one.
Perhaps the first calls for help. Perhaps the second sees a solution the
others missed. The child can do something appropriate to their ability
without receiving the whole outcome on their shoulders. The
frightened person can stay a few minutes and may, in turn, need
someone to defend them from an unjust consequence of delay.
In a bad version of the story, rescue succeeds and the vulnerable per‐
son alone pays the cost. In a better version, immediate help is fol‐
lowed by care for those who enabled it. In a still better version, insti‐
tutions learn: access is repaired, procedures are clear, and decent in‐
tervention no longer depends on hidden sacrifice.
None of this requires believing people are the absolute causes of their
own lives. Nor assuming every obligation simple or every freedom
preservable intact. It requires seeing the difference between possess‐
ing power and deserving to exercise it, between needing help and be‐
coming the helper's property, between making mistakes and being re‐
duced to them.
If God exists, the question of goodness does not diminish. If not, suf‐
fering thereby receives no excuse. If ultimate free will remains dis‐
puted, our capacity to modify circumstances does not disappear. If
freedom is articulated through human concepts, the doors those con‐
cepts can open are no less real.
The most convenient freedom lets us avoid seeing costs transferred to
others. The most convenient responsibility is the one we can assign to
someone else. A book about both must make us less comfortable with
both conveniences.
78


We are not obliged to save the world alone. But perhaps we are ob‐
liged not to use total salvation's impossibility to refuse every reason‐
able share. And perhaps we may demand that this share not be set ex‐
clusively by those retaining power and outsourcing their duties.
Freedom is not only the possibility of leaving. It is also the possibility
of staying without submission. Responsibility is not only the obliga‐
tion to pay for the past. It is also the capacity to make the future less
captive to its consequences.
The door is still open. The question is no longer whether its sign tells
the truth. It is who can pass through, who can refuse, and who en‐
sures that one person's freedom is not built from another's disappear‐
ance.

Reading Note. How the Claims Can
Be Checked
In a book between science and philosophy, questions do not all de‐
mand the same evidence. A claim that a context changes behavior re‐
quires observation and appropriate methods. A claim that behavior is
unjust also requires normative argument. A claim that someone is
free may need both: knowing their alternatives and explaining why
those alternatives matter.
An experiment can isolate a difference between conditions without
reproducing an entire life. For every finding, ask who participated,
how participants were assigned, what was measured, and what the
result was compared with. A difference between groups does not
automatically become a rule for every member. Nor does failure to de‐
tect a difference prove the effect impossible in every context.
Correlation and explanation are not interchangeable. If two charac‐
teristics appear together, one may contribute to the other, both may
depend on other circumstances, or the relationship may be more com‐
plex. Even well-designed studies require separating measurements
from proposed interpretation. Waiting for a reward in a task is not
identical to one's entire character; an electrical signal is not identical
to all deliberation.
What exactly was randomly assigned also matters. An offer of support
is not identical to actually using it. Interview timing is not identical to
a life of poverty. Changing a variable in a narrow setting can inform a
social problem without resolving it. Extending the conclusion requires
separate justification.
Studies with differing results do not mechanically cancel one another.
Sometimes they examine different populations, measures, or inter‐
ventions. Sometimes one corrects an overgeneralized conclusion. The
useful question is not “which camp won?” but “under what conditions
does the claim hold?” Findings limiting an attractive explanation
therefore belong to the story, not to notes to be concealed.
80


Thought experiments serve another function. The open door, the child
breaking a cup, or the god designing a world are not samples. They
deliberately modify conditions to test a principle's consistency. They
can reveal contradiction or assumption, but do not tell us how com‐
mon a situation is in reality.
Finally, findings about an act's causes do not alone produce a respons‐
ibility verdict. That requires discussing control, knowledge, obliga‐
tions, and remedial possibilities. Explaining does not automatically ex‐
cuse. Rejecting an excuse does not mean having explained. This book
can be challenged in both registers: through better evidence and bet‐
ter arguments.

texts, specialist syntheses, empirical studies, and normative docu‐
ments. References to the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy indicate
synthesis articles, not full readings of every historical work they dis‐
cuss. Rights documents formulate normative standards; they are not
themselves evidence of universal compliance.

Glossary. Distinctions That
Prevent Short Circuits
Autonomy. The capacity and conditions for directing your life in a
way you can recognize as your own. It may require support, informa‐
tion, and nonabusive relationships; it is not synonymous with isola‐
tion.
Capability. A real opportunity to achieve a particular way of being or
acting. It differs from actual achievement: being able to choose a life
does not mean being obliged to choose it.
Compatibilism. The family of positions holding that a relevant form
of freedom can exist even if actions are causally determined. It does
not assume every caused behavior is free.
Coercion. Restriction of options through force, threat, or other relev‐
ant pressures. Not every inconvenience is coercion in the same sense;
the pressure's type and intensity must be specified.
Determinism. The thesis that relevant states and laws fix subsequent
development. It is not fatalism: in a deterministic system, efforts and
decisions can be among an outcome's causes.
Domination. Dependence on arbitrary or insufficiently controlled
power. It can exist even when the power holder is not currently inter‐
vening.
Right. A justified claim or protection within a moral or legal frame‐
work. Needing something and having a right to it are related but not
identical assertions.
Moral equality. Refusal to assign some people lesser fundamental
value. It does not imply identical capacities, needs, or concrete re‐
sponsibilities.
Incompatibilism. The family of positions holding that determinism
cannot coexist with the freedom relevant to the debate. It includes
both defenses of free will and forms of skepticism about it.
82


Negative freedom. Absence of others' interference in a relevant
sphere of action. Defining interference and delimiting the sphere are
themselves disputed.
Positive freedom. Self-government, control over one's life. It must
not automatically be confused with well-being or an authority's right
to decide someone's “true” desires.
Political libertarianism. A family of theories assigning central im‐
portance to individual freedom and strong property rights. It is not
metaphysical libertarianism concerning free will.
Moral luck. The problem of uncontrolled factors influencing moral
assessment of a person or act. Recognizing it does not automatically
eliminate every distinction between behaviors.
Paternalism. Intervention in someone's choice justified by their pur‐
ported good. Its forms and intensities vary; protective intention does
not conclude the analysis of legitimacy.
Causal responsibility. Contribution to producing an outcome. It can
exist without guilt and does not alone establish what reparation is
owed.
Retrospective responsibility. Assessment of an act already per‐
formed: explanations, reproaches, sanctions, or reparation. These re‐
sponses are not equivalent and can have different justifications.
Prospective responsibility. An obligation to prevent harm or per‐
form a future task. It can arise from a role, special capacity, or rela‐
tionship without prior guilt.
Structural responsibility. Responsibility linked to processes and in‐
stitutions to which multiple actors contribute. It does not mean every‐
one is equally guilty or nobody needs specific identification.
Decision-making support. Assistance in understanding, expressing,
and realizing preferences. It differs from automatically replacing
someone's will with their assistant's.
Theodicy. An attempt to justify evil's existence in a world created or
governed by a good God. A defense of logical compatibility pursues a
more limited objective.
