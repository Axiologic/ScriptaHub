The other half of invention
The History and Future of Social Technologies restores institutions to technological history.
Tools expand capability, while rules and shared expectations determine what that capability means.
The book follows their interaction through money, law, organizations, science, and digital systems.
Its central criterion is how arrangements detect mistakes and permit their own revision.
Readers gain a vocabulary for the less visible machinery shaping every visible invention.

The promise behind the object
Money appears to reside in objects or numbers until the supporting promises begin to fail.
Records, shared expectations, settlement, and enforcement help make the familiar object usable.
The book calls these configurations social technologies because they can be transmitted and revised.
Their participants interpret rules, so the arrangement is not a machine of obedient gears.
This example opens a history of coordination that hardware alone cannot explain.

Capability and constraint change together
The historical journey follows how material systems reshape institutions and institutions shape technical development.
A rule can become ineffective when the surrounding infrastructure or balance of power changes.
Greater scale can improve coordination while making local consequences harder to hear.
The recurring test is whether the arrangement preserves information and authority needed for correction.
AI enters this history as new capability whose social meaning remains a governing problem.

Read the living protocol
Begin with the missing half of technological history and the definition of social technology.
Then follow how a familiar institution combines records, roles, incentives, and enforceable expectations.
The full chronology supplies comparisons that a catalogue of inventions would leave disconnected.
It helps readers ask which rules must change when a powerful new tool arrives.
Read it to see the revisable social arrangements inside the technologies you already depend on.
