The machinery around the machine
The History and Future of Social Technologies shows what histories of tools often overlook.
Behind a ship's cargo stand contracts, credit, and rules for settling disputes.
These arrangements shape what machines make possible, and who benefits.
The book follows this relationship from early cooperation to artificial intelligence.
Its central question is whether people can correct the institutions coordinating their lives.

A coin carries an agreement
Consider an illustrative payment: the coin is present, but the seller refuses it.
The obstacle is recognition, rather than the coin's ability to change hands.
Monetary systems depend on shared units, accepted claims, and ways to settle balances.
The book uses money to explain how strangers cooperate without knowing one another.
But the same portable signals can give their issuers power over everyone depending on them.

The record can be wrong
The chapters on administration follow this bargain into records, offices, and unequal authority.
A receipt and an official ledger can disagree about the same payment.
The book asks whether the affected person can inspect and challenge the record.
Keeping a record and correcting its consequences are different institutional abilities.
This turns an old administrative problem into a test for digital systems.

Who can change the rule
Its future chapters apply that test to platforms, executable rules, and AI judgment.
A fast decision still needs accountable authority and a usable route of appeal.
The proposed safeguards keep rules revisable when technical systems become difficult to avoid.
Begin with The Double Technology of Civilization, then follow its questions into Protocol Society.
Read it to see which institutions your preferred technological future would actually require.
