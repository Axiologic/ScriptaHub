When success loses its sponsor
Artificial Impossibility asks why a technology's success can make it harder to support.
Its examples include an assistant that serves people by helping them buy less.
The book studies projects inside financing, ownership, and rules that select them.
Readers learn to distinguish weak ideas from institutions that cannot preserve their intended value.
These refusals can emerge from ordinary responsibilities, without requiring a conspiracy.

A useful empty basket
Imagine someone replacing a travel bag because its strap has torn.
The book's purchasing assistant would also consider repair and advise against unnecessary replacement.
In this illustration, a repair service makes the existing bag useful again.
The owner keeps the bag, and the new-bag basket stays empty.
That outcome can help the owner while removing the platform's expected sales commission.
The problem is a conflict between the intended benefit and the available revenue model.

Change the support, keep the purpose
The method starts with the valuable outcome before choosing a company or funding category.
It then asks what potential sponsors are actually responsible for delivering.
A subscription could support the assistant, but exclude people unable to pay.
Public or cooperative support raises different questions about authority, access, and continuing costs.
Readers can compare these arrangements without pretending that one payment change solves everything.

Which limits should move?
The wider map compares prevention, public infrastructure, research funding, and legitimate rights of refusal.
Its Outfinitist perspective treats limits as real, but sometimes movable.
Some restrictions protect privacy and freedom, even when removing them would simplify a system.
Begin with the introduction's method and the anti-consumption assistant example.
Then follow the chapter closest to the obstacle your own project encounters.
