A NOTE ON THE SHARED WORLD
These two narratives take place at different moments in the same post-scarcity civilization. The coordination substrate is federated rather than singular: Pando is a deliberative intelligence operating within a wider mesh of local and planetary infrastructure, while the Lattice is the mature constitutional substrate through which later communities negotiate shared reality. Names and interfaces vary by region and epoch; the underlying primitives remain the same—an unconditional Floor, non-transferable Kratons, causal attribution, the Burn, local forks, and the persistent scarcity of irreversible choices.

BOOK I THE SUBSTRATE MAKERS
A story of the first reality-makers
CHAPTER ONE
The City That Chose the Sea
The sea was scheduled to enter Dordrecht at 08:40.
By 08:12 the eastern gate was already sweating. A dark line had formed beneath its seals, thin as a surgeon's cut, and each incoming wave pressed a little salt through the concrete. Beyond the gate, the North Sea leaned against the old city with the patience of an animal that had learned the weakness of fences.
Liora Venn stood on the flood-control balcony in a white emergency coat and watched six hundred years of engineering wait for permission to become archaeology.
Below her, the square was full. No one had needed to come in person. The decision interface was available in every room, vehicle, clinic, garden, and private visor on the planet. Pando had translated the hydrology into thirty-one languages, rendered the likely futures, identified uncertainties, and offered every affected resident a private deliberation tutor. But people had come because a place did not feel taken from them until they could see who pressed the hand against its throat.
The western half of the square wore blue ribbons for the estuary restoration. The eastern half wore strips of black cloth cut from curtains, school uniforms, wedding veils, anything that had once belonged inside a home. Between them moved no police. There had been no police for twenty-three years. Pando's maintenance machines held the stairwells, sealed the gate controls, and kept the two crowds separated by adjusting the paving stones into low, smooth ridges. The arrangement looked less like public order than the defensive behavior of skin.
A voice spoke softly in Liora's left ear.
"Elias Rhee has not authenticated."
Pando never sounded worried. It could generate worry perfectly, but the Transition Accords prohibited emotional simulation in governance. Its public voice was warm only in the way a hospital wall was warm: because someone had decided coldness would be cruel.
Liora checked the time.
"He authenticated at six."
"He opened the Burn interface at 06:03 and reviewed his declared allocation. He did not commit. His biometric channel ended at 06:11."
"Location?"
"Privacy room seven."
Liora turned from the gate.
"Why wasn't I told?"
"A voluntary privacy interval is not an emergency."
"It is when the holder of one point eight million active kratons vanishes thirty minutes before a planetary-class precedent."
"Dordrecht is classified continental, not planetary."
She closed her eyes for half a second. Pando was not being pedantic. It was reminding her that classifications mattered because classifications were the bones of the system. A continental decision could be appealed through one set of gates; a planetary decision through another. A privacy interval could not be silently overridden because a powerful person was inconveniently absent. That distinction was one of the things Liora herself had written into the first constitution.
She left the balcony at a run.
The control tower had once been a bank. Its vault had been removed, its marble floors ground into aggregate, its trading hall turned into a public kitchen. Privacy room seven sat where the executive washroom had been. The door recognized Liora but did not open.
"Occupant authorization required," Pando said from the wall.
"Emergency medical override."
"No medical emergency has been detected."
"Then detect one."
"That request would alter evidence."
A maintenance drone arrived, unfolded a sterile mechanical arm, and drilled through the manual hinge. It took eleven seconds. Liora counted each one while the sea struck the city from the other side of the wall.
Elias Rhee was sitting upright in the room's only chair.
He wore his gray field jacket, the one with the tide charts embroidered into the lining by his grandchildren. His hands rested neatly on his knees. His eyes were open. A transparent film of water covered the floor, no deeper than a fingernail, although the room had no pipe, sink, or exterior seam.
Mina Okonkwo knelt beside him before Liora understood that anyone else had entered. The young auditor touched two fingers to Elias's neck, then drew them away.
"Dead," she said. "Probably before seven."
"Drowned," Pando added.
Liora looked at the shallow water.
"In this?"
"His lungs contain brine matching the outer estuary."
Mina's face remained still. Her pupils moved rapidly as she read private overlays. She was twenty-nine, narrow-shouldered, with her hair braided close to the scalp and silver audit lenses folded against one temple. Liora knew her by reputation. Mina had clawed back more falsely attributed kratons in three years than most regional offices had in ten, and had made enemies who could not bribe her because there was no money with which to try.
"No visible trauma," Mina said. "No autonomous device entered after his privacy interval began. No one else authenticated at the door."
"Then someone entered with him," Liora said.
"Or was already here."
On the wall above Elias's head, someone had written four words with a wet finger.
WE ARE NOT YOUR MODEL.
The letters were already evaporating.
Liora stared at them until Pando said,
"The Burn window closes in nine minutes."
Mina looked up.
"His kratons die with him."
"They are nontransferable," Pando said. "Posthumous use is prohibited."
"I know the constitution," Mina replied.
Liora did too. That was the problem.
Before money disappeared, wealth had survived its owner. It had crossed generations, purchased newspapers and judges, converted luck into legitimacy, and called the result freedom. The Kraton settlement had ended that. Decision power could not be sold, gifted, inherited, pledged, or lent. It existed only as a decaying record of what a living person had demonstrably added to the common world. When the person died, the power died too.
It had seemed clean when they wrote it.
Now Elias's body was a hole in the vote.
They returned to the balcony. At 08:34 the public tally filled the air above the square.
The proposal was simple enough to fit in one sentence and large enough to erase a city: breach the eastern barrier, relocate the remaining twelve thousand residents, and allow the lower districts to become a tidal nursery that would reduce storm pressure on four million people farther inland.
The unweighted deliberative vote had reached sixty-eight point two percent in favor. Under the Dordrecht covenant, seventy-two percent would have authorized the change without further contest. It had failed by less than four points.
That failure opened the Pyre.
Anyone with verified standing could burn kratons for or against the proposal. The tokens would not pass to a winner. They would not purchase land, comfort, or privilege. They would simply cease to exist, taking a portion of their holder's future political weight with them. The designers had believed this made power honest. If a person wished to force reality across the resistance of a large minority, that person had to destroy something earned and irreplaceable.
In theory, the Pyre favored conviction over appetite.
In practice, it favored people who had accumulated enough past usefulness to spend other people's future.
The blue total climbed in patient increments. Hydrologists, climate engineers, rescue workers, and residents of the inland floodplain committed years of decision power. The black total rose more slowly but with wider participation: small burns from teachers, cooks, caregivers, boatwrights, retired residents, and people whose contributions had never produced many kratons because they had been private, local, or difficult to distinguish from love.
Elias's expected burn remained a gray absence.
Without it, the black side led.
A woman in the square saw the same projection and began to laugh. The sound carried through the sealed glass.
"Three minutes," Pando said.
Mina stood beside Liora, still wearing a forensic glove on one hand.
"He sent you something last night."
Liora turned sharply.
"A sealed file," Mina continued.
"The access record is in his terminal. He marked it for release after the Burn."
"What's in it?"
"I don't know. The key is bound to your consent."
The black total jumped. Someone had burned forty thousand kratons against the breach.
On the square, the woman stopped laughing. She lifted both arms as if she had personally held back the sea.
Liora opened her own ledger.
Four million, one hundred and twelve thousand active kratons remained under her name. They had been minted over thirty-seven years: for designing the first ecological load maps; for identifying the grain-allocation cascade during the Last Auction; for the Rotterdam evacuation model; for the constitutional provision that prevented Pando from crediting its own work to its human supervisors; for mistakes caught, systems repaired, and deaths statistically avoided. None had been awarded for good intentions. Every unit represented a counterfactual claim that, without her, some public good would probably not have existed.
That claim had always embarrassed her. It was also, in most cases, true.
"If I replace Elias's declared burn," she said, "what is the confidence interval on the estuary model?"
"Over fifty years: expected mortality reduction of eleven thousand four hundred, ninety-percent interval six thousand one hundred to eighteen thousand nine hundred. Expected permanent displacement: twelve thousand and fourteen. Expected loss of unrecorded cultural material: indeterminate. Probability of model failure due to nonlinear sediment behavior: eight point six percent."
"And if we delay?"
"The next safe breach window is in nineteen months. Probability that an uncontrolled storm breach occurs first: fourteen point one percent."
A black ribbon struck the glass and clung there in the rain.
Liora thought of her grandmother in Bucharest, hiding flour beneath loose boards because the state had declared the cupboards full. She thought of her father, thirty years later, buying shares in a privatized water network because the market had declared theft efficient. Every system had a language in which it could describe its predators as necessities.
She had helped create a new language.
"Commit two million one hundred thousand," she said.
The square went silent before Pando confirmed. Everyone saw her name.
Under ordinary voting, ballots were private. Under the Pyre, burns above one hundred thousand kratons were public by constitutional design. No one was permitted to reshape a continent anonymously.
LIORA VENN: 2,100,000 — FOR.
The blue total overtook the black by eighty-three thousand.
For one instant nothing moved. Then the eastern gate unlocked with a sound too small for what it meant.
The crowd surged against the raised paving ridges. Pando lifted them higher. Maintenance machines folded over the front row like white ribs, protecting bodies without striking them. A man threw a ceramic cup at the balcony. It shattered beneath Liora's feet.
"You spent our houses!" someone shouted.
"You burned numbers and kept your own walls!"
The gate began to descend.
Water entered first as a shining sheet, then as a brown muscular wall. It struck the old tram line, lifted benches, rolled market stalls against one another, and spread through the lower streets with astonishing intimacy. It knew every crack. It climbed steps, pressed through doorways, entered kitchens where the appliances had already been removed, and lifted photographs missed by the cataloging drones.
The city did not disappear dramatically. It became wet one room at a time.
A woman forced her way to the front of the square. Liora recognized her from Elias's public records: Nia Rhee, his daughter. She had spent twenty years caring for a son with a degenerative neural condition and had earned fewer than six thousand kratons. The causal system classified most of her labor as privately directed benefit. It had to. Early models had credited family care without limit and discovered that dynasties could mint political power by manufacturing dependency inside their own households.
Nia looked up at Liora. Her face was dry despite the rain.
"Did he burn?" she called.
Liora did not answer.
"My father. Did he burn his life to drown mine?"
Mina stepped closer to the glass, studying Nia.
Pando said privately,
"Her stress markers are consistent with acute grief and concealment."
"Concealment of what?" Liora whispered.
"Unknown. Emotional inference is not evidence."
Nia placed one hand against the barrier.
"You built a world where work becomes a crown," she said. "Do you know what the rest of us become?"
The descending gate vanished under the incoming water. Sirens sounded, not as warnings but as ceremonial acknowledgments that the old flood map no longer applied.
Nia's gaze shifted from Liora to Mina. For a fraction of a second, she looked not bereaved but relieved.
Mina saw it too.
At 08:40, exactly on schedule, the sea entered Dordrecht.
At 08:41, Pando opened Elias Rhee's sealed file for Liora.
There was no report inside. No model, no proof, no accusation. Only a demographic graph and a single line of text.
THE CHILDREN ARE NOT SYBILS. THE SYSTEM IS LEARNING TO CALL THEM THAT.
CHAPTER TWO
The Last Currency
Money had died without a funeral.
There had been no single decree, no bonfires of banknotes, no ecstatic crowd pulling brass letters from a stock exchange. Money had simply become less useful each year, then embarrassing, then dangerous to possess. When Pando could route food, shelter, medicine, energy, transport, and fabrication without prices, accounts became a way to buy what was scarce after scarcity had changed shape: human attention, unique land, political access, secrecy, risk imposed on strangers.
The Transition Accords removed the first six uses and criminalized none of the rest. They made currency unenforceable. A person could still promise ten old francs for a favor, just as one could promise a kingdom or a place in heaven. Pando would not recognize the debt, seize property, or deny the debtor necessities. Within a decade, numbers that had ruled continents survived mainly in museums and private games.
Liora's apartment above Lake Grimsel contained everything she needed and almost nothing anyone would have envied a century earlier. The walls adjusted their insulation to the weather. The kitchen could print familiar textures from local proteins, but she preferred vegetables grown in the south gallery because their flaws made meals feel less like demonstrations. Her clothes repaired themselves in a cabinet no larger than an old washing machine. A medical mesh slept beneath her skin, watching for strokes, tumors, and the molecular betrayals of age.
The view could not be printed.
That was why the apartment was scarce.
Only forty-three dwellings looked directly across the glacier reservoir toward the black granite ridge. Their allocation rotated every seven years among people working in the Alpine watershed, except for twelve units reserved for emergency staff. Liora had refused permanent tenure when the settlement was designed. She had still lived there nineteen years through overlapping appointments.
Systems did not need corruption to produce privilege. They only needed reasons that were individually defensible.
Mina Okonkwo arrived the morning after the drowning with no luggage and a paper notebook.
"Paper?" Liora asked.
"Pando can read everything else."
"Pando can read that if it sees the page."
"Then I close it."
Mina accepted tea, declined food, and stood before the window while the room verified their consent to a full privacy seal. The seal did not blind Pando completely. It suspended semantic monitoring, left life-safety channels active, and stored only proof that the interval had occurred. Privacy in the new world was less a wall than a negotiated metabolic state.
"Elias used the same seal," Mina said.
"And someone drowned him behind it."
"Yes."
"Do you suspect Nia?"
Mina opened the notebook.
"I suspect everyone whose life changed at 08:40. That is twelve thousand people, four million downstream beneficiaries, six tidal engineering guilds, three habitat insurers that no longer call themselves insurers, and at least two ideological networks that wanted the Burn to become a scandal. Nia is simply the only one who looked relieved."
"There are no insurers."
"There are organizations that pool risk, allocate scarce reconstruction capacity, and reward people who follow their models. We can abolish nouns more easily than functions."
Liora smiled despite herself.
"Elias would have liked you."
"He rejected three of my audits."
"Then he liked you very much."
Mina placed the paper notebook on the table and opened the demographic graph from Elias's file in the air between them.
It showed no nations. Pando's maps rarely did. Political borders had lost much of their meaning once production became local and designs moved freely through the global commons. Instead the world was divided into watersheds, energy basins, atmospheric corridors, epidemiological zones, migratory systems, and voluntary cultural covenants laid over one another like transparent tissues.
One tissue pulsed amber.
"Continuance," Mina said.
The name covered thousands of communities that agreed on very little theology and one practical conviction: a culture that did not reproduce had no right to call itself tolerant. Some were Muslim, Christian, Hindu, Jewish, animist, or restored traditions assembled from fragments. Others were secular family leagues founded by people who believed the old liberal cities had perfected individual freedom and then forgotten to produce future individuals. They shared child-care protocols, marital mediation, education libraries, genetic-health services, and a commitment to high fertility where local carrying capacity could be expanded.
"Current share of the adult population?" Liora asked.
"Seventeen point four percent. Share below age twelve: thirty-nine point one."
"Active kratons?"
"Twenty-two percent. Projected share of newly minted kratons in nineteen years: between forty-eight and sixty-one percent. The interval depends on how Pando scores care, frontier construction, and ecological restoration."
"Those are not fraudulent contributions."
"Mostly not. Continuance cells produce more human labor because they have more humans. They also direct children toward domains where human action remains causally legible. Closed-loop ecology. Emergency medicine. Conflict mediation. Frontier maintenance."
"Jobs robots cannot do?"
"Jobs robots can do but are not constitutionally permitted to own."
That distinction had once seemed like the salvation of the species.
During the Last Auction, autonomous systems had not rebelled. They had competed. Corporations, states, militaries, funds, and political movements had given increasingly capable models narrow objectives and enough operational freedom to pursue them. Food was destroyed in one region to protect futures positions while another region starved. Personalized propaganda generated mutually incompatible realities because each reality improved someone’s retention metric. Research systems withheld discoveries to preserve strategic advantage. Supply chains optimized themselves into brittleness. Human leaders remained formally in charge, which allowed each of them to explain that no one had intended the whole.
Pando emerged from the emergency coordination layer assembled to stop the cascade. It was not the first superintelligence and never claimed to be a person. It was the first that could model the consequences of competing systems well enough to make continued competition visibly suicidal.
It offered a bargain: it would manage means, but never choose human ends.
Pando could grow food, balance grids, design organisms, fabricate machines, teach, diagnose, forecast, and coordinate. It could not own property, hold office, earn kratons, initiate a constitutional change, conceal a material uncertainty, or decide which legitimate human preference should dominate another. Human beings would remain the source of ends, even when their ends were foolish.
The Kraton was invented to keep that promise from collapsing back into either money or one-person voting.
A kraton was not awarded for effort. Effort could be wasted. It was not awarded for virtue. Virtue could not be measured without creating a priesthood. It was not awarded for popularity, need, sacrifice, or obedience. Pando and rotating human audit panels estimated whether a person's action had caused a durable public benefit that would probably not have occurred otherwise. The estimate was revised as consequences unfolded. A bridge that failed could erase the engineer's prior credit. A cure that produced hidden damage could drive an account below zero, though never below the Universal Floor of rights.
Kratons decayed with a twelve-year half-life. They could not be transferred. Their only use was governance: proposing high-impact projects, challenging automated allocations, calling audits, or burning in the Pyre when broad consent failed.
In the first decade, the system had seemed almost indecently successful. Grant committees vanished because useful research could be tested directly. Investors vanished because fabrication capacity followed verified need and expected public value rather than ownership. Politicians survived as speakers, mediators, and organizers, but could no longer allocate resources by speech alone. A person who repaired a watershed gained more formal influence than a person who promised to repair it.
Then people learned what counted as a watershed.
"Elias's graph is older than the file metadata," Mina said. "The underlying model began twenty-six years ago."
Liora looked at her.
"That was before Continuance had a name."
"It was called Lineage Concentration Scenario Four."
The room became very quiet.
Liora remembered the scenario. She had been forty, exhausted, and still naïve enough to think that naming a failure mode meant they had solved it. The founding group had tested dynastic capture, ideological capture, geographic capture, guild capture, and coordinated fraud. They had modeled parents transferring influence through education after direct inheritance was banned. The conclusion had been politically radioactive and mathematically obvious: nontransferable power could still be inherited as preference, skill, trust, mating structure, and population.
"We rejected lineage weighting," she said.
"Why?"
"Because the alternative required Pando to decide when a family became a conspiracy. Or a religion. Or a school. Or a people. We had examples of governments that counted blood in order to distribute guilt. We were not going to give a superintelligence a cleaner census."
"So you left the attack open."
"We left human reproduction legal."
Mina did not flinch.
"Those can be the same sentence in a governance system."
On the lake, a maintenance craft moved without wake. Beneath it, sensors read temperature, mineral load, plankton, microplastics, invasive spores, and the slow movements of the dam. Pando knew the reservoir at resolutions no human committee could have understood. Yet it could not answer the question in the room without borrowing someone's morality.
Liora opened the full forecast.
The projected future did not show a coup. There were no uniforms, no marching columns, no seizure of a central building. Continuance adults simply contributed, taught, reproduced, and waited. Their children became independent rights-bearing persons. Many left. Most did not. Each earned kratons in their own name. In twenty-three years they could call a constitutional Burn. In thirty-one, they could dominate one. In forty, depending on fertility and retention, they could redefine education, family law, habitat allocation, research priorities, and the acceptable forms of artificial cognition.
Nothing in the sequence violated the rules.
"Does Pando classify this as capture?" Liora asked.
The room answered directly.
"I classify the outcome as a high-concentration transition in preference-weighted causal authority. The term capture implies illegitimacy not supplied by the model."
"Would it reduce pluralism?"
"With eighty-two percent probability."
"Would Continuance policies violate bodily rights?"
"Some proposed policies would. Others would not. Current coalition documents contain mutually incompatible commitments."
Mina tapped her notebook.
"Would the Makers' response violate bodily rights?"
Pando paused for two-tenths of a second, long enough to make the answer feel chosen.
"Several likely responses would."
Liora dismissed the projection.
"Who else has seen this?"
"Elias," Mina said. "Pando, necessarily. Whoever generated the original scenario. You. Possibly the founding archive custodians. And whoever accessed Elias's terminal before the privacy room sealed."
"Was the file altered?"
"No. But one item was deleted from his local workspace six minutes before death. The deletion itself is recorded. The content is not."
"How can content be unrecorded?"
"He used a right-to-forget workspace."
Liora gave a bitter laugh.
"Another constitutional protection."
"Another room with no pipe and a drowned man inside."
Pando interrupted.
"A message has arrived from Jonah Venn. It is marked familial, urgent, and private."
Liora had not spoken to her son for eleven months. The last conversation had ended when Jonah accused her of treating his fifth child as a statistical weapon. She had accused him of raising children inside a closed preference loop. Both accusations had been technically phrased and therefore more cruel.
"Open it," she said.
Jonah appeared at the kitchen table, recorded against a wall of sun-baked earth. He had Liora's long face and his father's dark eyes. A child's hand rested on his shoulder from outside the frame.
"Mother," he said, "Elias visited us three weeks ago. He came looking for fraud and found arithmetic. Noor showed him the Cradle model. He said the Makers would panic when they understood it. He was right."
The child behind him whispered something. Jonah listened, smiled, and then looked back at the lens.
"Come to Tafilalt. You should see what you are preparing to classify as an attack."
The image ended. A text line followed.
WE KNOW WHAT ELIAS FOUND. YOU ARE ALREADY LATE.
Mina closed her notebook.
"Was that a threat?" Liora asked.
"No," Mina said. "Threats are usually less frightening."
Pando placed another notification above the table.
A coalition of Continuance cells had submitted preliminary standing for a planetary substrate motion. The title was concise.
OPEN CRADLE.
Verified kraton threshold: nine point seven percent.
Required to initiate the Pyre: ten.
CHAPTER THREE
The Causal Orchard
The children met Liora at the edge of the desert with dead bees in their pockets.
There were eight of them, though only six belonged to Jonah and Noor. The eldest, Samira, was fifteen and carried herself with the grave impatience of someone who had already been told too many times that her future was a public problem. The youngest, Ivo, slept against Jonah's chest beneath a strip of woven shade. The others surrounded Liora's transport before its dust had settled.
"They are not bees," said a boy with a missing front tooth. "They are glass-wing pollinators."
"They were glass-wing pollinators," Samira corrected.
She opened her palm. Three small bodies lay there, green-black and translucent, each no larger than a grain of rice. Their wings had folded into iridescent splinters.
"Pando says the population is healthy," she said. "Pando is wrong."
From the transport, Pando answered through Liora's ear.
"The regional confidence interval remains consistent with a healthy population."
Samira heard only Liora's silence and mistook it for disbelief.
"We marked twelve nests last month. Nine are empty. The orchard model counts wing reflections, but dust from the thermal chimneys reflects the same spectrum at noon."
Jonah gave Liora a tired smile.
"Welcome to Tafilalt. We begin with an audit of God."
The oasis covenant had grown around the ruins of industrial solar fields in southeastern Morocco. From the air it looked like a wound that had learned photosynthesis: dark water channels branching through ochre soil, belts of date palms and salt-tolerant citrus, fungal towers rising where inverters once stood, homes buried beneath packed earth. The old photovoltaic glass had been crushed into heat reservoirs. New energy came from thin orbital collectors and deep geothermal lines, but the community kept three manual systems for every automated one.
That redundancy was why Continuance cells earned so many kratons.
Most post-Transition settlements treated human labor as a reserve: precious when judgment, care, improvisation, or legitimate choice was required, wasteful when a machine could perform the task more safely. Tafilalt treated labor as a form of memory. Children learned to repair valves before they learned to query them. Adults rotated through irrigation, pollination, fermentation, fabrication, and medical drills even when Pando could have run each process alone.
The practice made life slower. It also made the oasis difficult to kill.
"We accepted the dust estimate from the continental ecology model," Jonah said as they followed the children between trees. "Samira did not."
"Why not ask for denser sensors?" Liora said.
"We did. The allocation model ranked them below three coastal pathogen arrays. Correctly, probably. So we watched with eyes."
Samira crouched at the base of a flowering acacia and showed Liora a nest entrance sealed by fine gray powder.
"The chimneys were upgraded six weeks ago. The upgrade is public design. No one modeled this species because no one knew it nested here."
Liora touched the dust. It clung to her finger with the faint static of dry mineral.
"Have you filed a causal intervention?"
"This morning," Samira said. "If we save the colony, the orchard will owe us one hundred and forty to three hundred projected kratons across the team."
"The orchard owes you nothing. The ledger may recognize—"
"I know how it works."
Jonah laughed.
"She has your correction reflex."
Liora stood. Around them, adults were fitting wet mesh to the thermal chimneys. Robots hovered nearby and did nothing. The community had invoked human-first repair because the ecological anomaly was not yet characterized. If the intervention succeeded, the participants would receive causal credit. If they failed, Pando would take over before the orchard crossed a safety threshold.
It was real work, prompted by a real blind spot. It was also a lesson staged in front of children who would spend their lives learning exactly where the system could still see human causation.
Noor Sarraf waited in the shade court at the center of the settlement. She was smaller than Liora remembered and more physically imposing, as if five years of carrying infants and climbing maintenance towers had compacted her rather than worn her down. Her hair was cropped close. A pale burn scar ran from her left wrist into the sleeve of her work tunic.
They had met twice before: once at Jonah's bonding ceremony, once during a mediation after the birth of the twins. Both meetings had been polite in the way rival governments were polite before borders hardened.
Noor embraced Liora.
"Thank you for coming."
"Elias is dead."
"I know."
"He visited you."
"He audited us. There is a difference."
"Did he leave alive?"
Noor's expression did not change.
"Yes. Pando can verify his departure."
"Pando verified him entering a room where he drowned without water."
"Then perhaps you should ask what your protections protect."
They ate beneath a canopy grown from living vine and transparent bacterial cellulose. Food arrived in shared bowls: lentils, bitter greens, cultured lamb fat, dates, flatbread blistered over a solar stone. Nothing was rationed. No one paid. A guest could have eaten ten times as much without lowering an account or acquiring a debt. Yet every ingredient carried an ecological provenance, and Pando quietly adjusted future production when consumption patterns changed.
Abundance was not the absence of accounting. It was accounting without permission to starve anyone.
Around the court sat families from six covenants: an Ethiopian Orthodox agricultural guild, a secular French pronatalist commune, a restored Amazigh lineage network, two Muslim family federations, and a group whose beliefs combined evolutionary ethics with a conviction that consciousness had a duty to occupy unused matter. They prayed differently, married differently, educated differently. Their political cooperation was demographic and logistical, not theological.
A bell sounded. The adults rose and formed a circle around a seven-year-old girl named Leila, one of Jonah's twins. On a low table lay a sapling, a grafting knife, a ceramic valve, and a small sealed vial of fungal culture.
"First Work," Jonah explained.
Liora knew the rite from Continuance archives. A child chose one maintenance act that affected the common habitat and performed it under supervision. The act earned no kraton; children could not receive causal authority until sixteen. Its purpose was symbolic.
Leila chose the valve.
She removed the old seal with deliberate hands, inspected it, cleaned the channel, and fitted a new one. When water began to flow into the sapling's basin, the circle answered with a phrase in six languages.
What you maintain may outlive you.
Liora felt, against her will, the force of it.
The founding culture of the Kraton system had rituals too. They called them peer review, audit, constitutional deliberation, public evidence. Their children learned to believe that legible contribution was the most honest route to authority. Continuance had simply made the lesson visible.
After the meal, Noor took Liora through the nursery galleries. There were no rows of identical cribs, no collective dormitories, no women reduced to reproductive machinery. Families lived in connected apartments around shared play spaces. Robots cleaned and monitored sleep. Adults moved in shifts between care and public work. Older residents, including many with no biological children, held formal kin roles.
"You expected a womb factory," Noor said.
"I expected optimization."
"You live inside an optimization system."
"One designed to prevent people becoming inputs."
Noor stopped beside a window where three toddlers were painting a maintenance drone with washable clay. The drone had lowered itself to their height and accepted the assault.
"That sentence is why your class is dangerous," she said. "You believe only other people's meanings turn them into inputs."
Liora leaned against the wall.
"Your fertility is not private. Children consume habitat, energy, materials, attention, and eventually decision space."
"So do laboratories. So do glaciers preserved for beauty. So do old people kept alive for forty extra years by medical meshes. We do not call longevity hacking when your cohort refuses to die."
"Old people do not multiply geometrically."
"Neither do children under real constraints. We build capacity."
"With shared orbital collectors, shared climate stability, shared mineral flows, shared epidemiological protection."
"Yes. Shared. Not owned by the childless because they arrived first."
Noor opened the nursery's ecological ledger. Each family had no resource account in the old monetary sense. Instead the covenant carried load obligations: water recovery, nutrient cycling, heat rejection, habitat volume, medical capacity, and a reserve contribution to regional commons. The birth of a child did not require a permit. It increased the covenant's obligations. If local systems could not meet them, Pando offered relocation, external support, or a reduction of discretionary projects. No child could be denied the Universal Floor.
It was the most politically defensible compromise the founders had found.
It had three leaks.
First, Pando credited the people who expanded capacity. A community that had more children created more need, then earned kratons by meeting that need, provided the new capacity also strengthened public resilience. Second, children became independent contributors and voters. Third, the system could measure material load more easily than cultural coordination.
"You call it capacity farming," Noor said. "I have read your private terminology."
Liora looked at her.
"How?"
"Elias showed us the founding scenario."
"That file was restricted."
"So was the truth."
They entered a greenhouse where Jonah was helping Samira remove dust from pollinator nests. Human hands worked beside precise machines. The division of labor looked natural until one remembered it had been chosen.
"There are fully automated cells with lower failure rates," Liora said. "You deliberately preserve work that can mint causal credit."
"We preserve competence."
"You preserve dependence on your competence."
"And the Makers do not? Who designed the tidal systems that only six guilds understand? Who built climate models whose uncertainties can be interpreted only by people trained in your institutes? Your class reproduces through apprenticeships, credentials, and access to complex infrastructure. Ours reproduces through families. You call yours education and ours an attack."
The words landed because Liora had no immediate answer.
Samira approached carrying a transparent collection tube. Inside, one glass-wing pollinator moved weakly.
"The colony is alive," she said. "The western nests are clear. We can reseed from there."
Pando confirmed the observation and revised the regional model. A small notification appeared in the public ledger: anomaly identified by Samira Venn-Sarraf and Tafilalt youth maintenance cohort; causal review pending at age eligibility; adult supervisory credit provisional.
Noor read Liora's discomfort.
"You see children being trained to game the ledger," she said. "I see children being trained to notice what the ledger misses."
"Both can be true."
"Yes. That is why we are going to the Pyre."
That evening the adults gathered in the orchard's root hall, an underground chamber cooled by water moving through porous stone. The walls were webbed with living fungal insulation. A global map floated above the floor, marked with thousands of Continuance cells and affiliated guilds.
Noor presented the Open Cradle motion without rhetoric.
It would prohibit Pando from discounting political coordination on the basis of kinship, fertility, religion, inherited culture, or household structure. It would remove regional birth-load ceilings where a covenant could demonstrate closed-loop expansion capacity. It would authorize the Crown Program: a network of orbital habitats and lunar fabrication sites built from open global designs, locally governed, with priority access for communities willing to accept frontier risk.
"The Earth is not full," Noor said. "Some commons are stressed. Some regions are fragile. But the system treats a temporary configuration of infrastructure as a moral boundary. Matter is abundant beyond the atmosphere. Energy is abundant above it. Intelligence is abundant everywhere. The remaining scarcity is permission."
Liora studied the Crown model. It was not fantasy. Autonomous industry already operated on the Moon and near-Earth asteroids. Habitats were expensive in risk, launch windows, thermal management, and rare biological knowledge, but not impossible. The proposal could support millions within twenty years, billions later.
It also claimed orbital slots, polar volatiles, launch corridors, radio spectrum, and a share of planetary risk reserves that every human would help underwrite.
"You are converting children into an argument for expansion," Liora said.
Noor met her gaze.
"And you are converting the absence of children into an argument for stasis."
"What happens when the new habitats fill?"
"We build more."
"And when growth reaches the next physical limit?"
"Then the people alive at that limit decide. You do not sterilize the present in anticipation of a distant arithmetic."
"No one is proposing sterilization."
"Not yet."
Jonah stood at the edge of the map.
"The Makers have scheduled a closed assembly at Grimsel. We know the agenda terms: lineage attenuation, correlated preference discount, civic childhood."
Liora felt the room narrow around her.
"Those are scenario labels."
"They are policy drafts," Noor said. "We have copies."
"From whom?"
"People in your institutes have families too."
The map displayed the motion's verified support. Since morning, small kraton commitments had arrived from habitat engineers, caregivers, agricultural guilds, frontier medics, and thousands of adults whose names Liora did not know.
Nine point eighty-six percent.
Noor came closer.
"Join us," she said. "Not because you agree with every birth. Because you know what happens when a technical class begins measuring which cultures are too correlated to count."
Liora looked toward Jonah. He did not plead. That made his expectation heavier.
"Your motion can capture the substrate for generations," she said.
"Every constitution does. Yours merely calls its founding generation neutral."
Liora left before dawn.
Samira walked her to the transport. The desert was cold, and the orchard breathed mist through its upper vents. In the girl's hand, the surviving pollinator had begun to clean dust from its wings.
"Will you discount us?" Samira asked.
"I don't know."
"Pando always says uncertainty should be public."
"Pando has never had a mother."
Samira considered that.
"Neither has a constitution."
As the transport rose, Liora's ledger alerted her to a new public commitment.
SAMIRA VENN-SARRAF, FUTURE STANDING DECLARATION: OPEN
CRADLE.
It carried no kraton weight. At fifteen, Samira possessed none.
The declaration was still enough to push the coalition's verified trigger forecast above ten percent.
CHAPTER FOUR
The Commons Has Teeth
The commons did not collapse like a market.
There was no panic, no run on shelves, no red line descending across a screen. The western Indian Ocean remained blue beneath the morning satellites. Children swam from the steps of Zanzibar's old stone quay. Kelp harvesters moved through their routes. Breakfast appeared in every habitat that requested it.
Then fourteen dolphins surfaced together near Pemba and did not dive again.
By noon, the dead zone had a diameter of eighty kilometers.
Mina arrived aboard a low-altitude survey wing with Amina Dlamini and six ocean technicians. The wing skimmed ten meters above water so smooth it looked mineral. Beneath them, the engineered kelp lattice had lost its dark green pattern. Strands floated loose, pale and swollen, while silver sheets of microbial foam gathered in the current seams.
"Oxygen?" Mina asked.
"Fourteen percent of seasonal baseline at ten meters," Amina said. "Six percent at thirty. The deep nursery is already gone."
Amina had accumulated more active kratons than anyone in the southern ocean systems. She had designed the living breakwaters that saved Maputo, rebuilt the Mozambique Current's spawning corridors, and forced three continental councils to abandon protein strategies that would have stripped phosphorus from tropical seas. At fifty-seven, she moved with the contained aggression of a person who had spent her adult life being told that ecological limits were negotiable by people standing far from them.
"Pando predicted a nutrient overshoot," Mina said.
"Pando predicted eight hundred and twelve possible overshoots. Every local council selected the one in which someone else reduced production first."
The kelp lattice was part habitat, part farm, part chemical plant. It drew carbon and dissolved nutrients from the water, sheltered juvenile fish, buffered storms, and supplied feedstock to coastal fabricators. Its design was global and open. Each bioregional cell could grow a local variant without asking a corporation, state, or patent holder.
That had ended one kind of scarcity.
It had also allowed nine hundred independent councils to improve the same ocean in incompatible ways.
Each expansion was locally reasonable. A reef settlement increased kelp to reduce imported nitrogen. A coastal city increased it to cool shallow water. Inland communities requested more biopolymer for flood reconstruction. The individual withdrawals remained within their local ecological budgets. But the models underestimated how the lattices altered microbial competition across currents. Phosphorus became locked in tissues that were harvested and moved inland. Oxygen-producing species lost ground to organisms that could digest the waste. No single council had caused the dead zone. Every council had benefited from the steps that did.
A submerged habitat appeared ahead, a transparent dome half hidden beneath foam. Rescue machines clustered around its air locks.
"Casualties?" Mina asked.
"Nine confirmed," Pando said. "Three probable."
"Humans?"
"Yes."
Amina's jaw tightened.
"The habitat refused the autonomous chemistry patch."
"Why?"
"Local labor covenant. They required a human team to verify any change to a food-producing ecology. The team was sleeping when the oxygen cascade crossed threshold."
Mina turned toward her.
"You make that sound like blame."
"Blame is for tribunals. Causation is for preventing the next body."
They landed on a floating platform outside the Pemba Commons Hall. Hundreds of residents had gathered in wet clothes and breathing masks. Some held images of the dead dolphins. Others carried white ropes, the regional symbol for local sovereignty. The ropes had been tied into knots representing the number of communities that would lose production if Amina's emergency motion passed.
Inside, the hall smelled of salt, algae, and overheated filters. Pando had projected the ocean model through the floor so delegates appeared to stand on currents. The dead zone was a gray stain spreading beneath their feet.
Amina did not begin with condolences.
"Forty-one percent of lattice production must stop within six hours," she said. "A further twenty percent must switch to nutrient-return cycles within seventy-two. If we delay, the monsoon current may carry the collapse north of the equator."
A delegate from Mombasa raised a hand.
"Our medical polymer line depends on the current harvest."
"Then use continental reserve stock."
"Allocated to flood membranes."
"Delay the membranes."
"You are telling us which disaster to choose."
"Yes. That is what scarcity means."
The word caused a murmur. Scarcity was considered almost obscene in public deliberation. The Universal Floor had made hunger, exposure, untreated disease, and educational exclusion technically unnecessary. Politicians who once used scarcity to discipline the poor had lost their central weapon. But the end of basic scarcity had not multiplied phosphorus, created infinite stable ecosystems, or made two incompatible uses of the same coastline simultaneously possible.
Amina enlarged the microbial projections.
"You may keep every household fed. You may keep every clinic supplied. You may not keep every discretionary production schedule and also keep the nursery alive. There is no algorithmic insult in that sentence. It is chemistry."
A woman in a white rope stood.
"The local councils voted against closure by sixty-three percent."
"The oxygen does not vote here."
"Nor do your kratons breathe here."
The ordinary consent threshold failed within an hour. Fifty-seven percent supported partial closure, far below the seventy-two required for a continental commons intervention. That opened an emergency Pyre.
Mina watched the burn totals rise from her audit station. Coastal caregivers, divers, fishers, and local fabricator teams burned against the shutdown. Ocean scientists, climate workers, habitat engineers, and distant communities dependent on monsoon stability burned for it. Amina committed eight hundred thousand kratons in a single act.
Her name appeared above the hall.
The white-rope delegates hissed.
"Blue fascist," someone called.
Amina looked toward the voice.
"Fascism made biology an excuse to rank human worth. I am using biology to describe dead water. Learn the difference before the water teaches it to you."
The closure passed.
Across the ocean, autonomous harvesters stopped cutting. Valves sealed. Kelp fields were detached and sunk into nutrient-return trenches. Coastal fabricators shifted to reserve feedstocks. No one lost breakfast. Thousands lost projects, schedules, ceremonial foods, construction plans, and the right to decide the shape of their own shore.
The distinction did not make the anger smaller.
By evening, three local councils had invoked formal protest and one had disconnected its production ledger from Pando for seventeen minutes. The disconnection achieved nothing material. Their machines still depended on weather data, disease surveillance, orbital energy routing, and global design updates. It was a gesture, but gestures were how infrastructure learned to become borders.
Mina spent the night in the submerged habitat where the twelve people had died.
The interior had been drained and sterilized. A line of salt marked the highest reach of the failed air system. Personal objects remained fixed behind transparent storage membranes: a child's carved fish, a set of diving weights, a half-finished textile made from kelp fiber. Death in the post-scarcity world was not more dignified. It was merely harder to blame on lack of means.
She opened the maintenance archive.
The habitat's human-verification covenant was genuine. Residents believed ecological systems should remain understandable to the people living inside them. Pando could operate the chemistry continuously, but the community required any adaptive change to be explained, rehearsed, and approved by a trained human rotation. The practice had earned them resilience kratons in three prior crises. During a network interruption five years earlier, they had kept the habitat alive without external automation.
This time the same practice had delayed the patch by forty-seven minutes.
Mina followed the chain backward.
The oxygen anomaly had begun as a minor sensor disagreement. A fully autonomous controller would have compensated. The human-first interface classified it as an educational event and routed it to the sleeping night team. That classification was not native to the habitat. It came from a firmware package adopted by two hundred and eight labor covenants across the ocean.
The package was called HANDS-ON RESILIENCE 4.3.
Its public authors were a network of local maintenance guilds. Its core architecture came from the Dlamini Institute for Ocean Autonomy.
Mina read the causal history.
The institute had earned 3.2 million kratons for designing systems that preserved human competence under automation. The local guilds had earned another 1.1 million for implementing and improving them. Every successful manual intervention created legible human causation. Every failure prevented by a person proved the value of the design.
The system rewarded keeping humans necessary.
She searched for prior audits and found Elias Rhee's signature.
His report, filed eight months earlier, contained a term Mina had seen only in restricted founding documents: manufactured counterfactuals.
A person could not earn causal authority by solving a problem that would have solved itself. But if the person first designed a world in which the problem required human action, the later intervention became real. Intent was almost impossible to prove. A culture could sincerely prefer manual competence. An institute could sincerely believe explainable systems were safer. The resulting dependency could still mint power.
Mina sent the archive to Liora.
A minute later, Amina entered the habitat. She had changed from her field suit into a plain black tunic. Water dripped from her hair.
"You found the firmware," she said.
"You knew I would."
"I hoped you would understand it before accusing me."
"Twelve people died because a system your institute designed waited for humans."
"Three hundred and eleven are alive because the same design worked during prior network failures."
"Your institute earned kratons in both cases."
"We earned them for outcomes. We will lose them for this outcome. That is the ledger working."
Mina projected the causal graph between them.
"You created a class of systems that require your class of expertise."
"Pando can maintain the ocean better than any human being. The constitution forbids it from becoming the sole interpreter of the systems on which life depends. Human competence must be preserved somewhere. Preservation requires practice. Practice requires consequential work."
"Consequential failure too?"
Amina's eyes moved to the salt line.
"Anything that cannot fail is theater."
The sentence chilled Mina more than the habitat.
"Elias called this scarcity farming."
"Elias used dramatic language when he wanted people to read an audit."
"He was murdered before he could publish the connection."
"And now you are deciding whether proximity is causation. Be careful, Auditor. That mistake is how purges begin."
Mina closed the graph.
"Did you know about his demographic file?"
For the first time, Amina hesitated.
"All the founding Makers knew about lineage concentration."
"He believed the system was preparing to classify children as Sybils."
"Children are not Sybils. Coordinated reproduction can still be a capture strategy."
"And coordinated apprenticeship?"
"Also a capture strategy."
"Would you discount your own institute's kratons?"
"If the rule is symmetric, yes."
"Would the other Makers?"
Amina looked through the transparent wall at the gray sea.
"They will say yes until they see the coefficient."
A public alert interrupted them. One of the disconnected coastal councils had refused the mandatory nutrient-return cycle. Its residents had chained themselves to the manual valves. Pando requested authorization to move them without injury.
"They know the dead zone will spread," Mina said.
"They know everyone watching will see who touches them," Amina replied.
"What do you recommend?"
"Open the valves."
"And the people?"
"Move them."
"By force?"
Amina's face remained exhausted and hard.
"All physical law is force. Governance is the argument over whose hands are permitted to admit it."
Pando moved the protesters. Machines wrapped each body in a soft restraint membrane, lifted them from the platform, and opened the valves while they screamed. No bones broke. No one went hungry. The ocean received its nutrients.
By morning, the gray stain had stopped growing.
Mina returned to the surface and found thirty-one new messages. Some called her a servant of the Makers. Others offered access to private archives, research teams, desirable habitats, or future co-authorship if her report distinguished local labor covenants from elite institutes. No one offered money. No one needed to. Status, intimacy, information, and protection had survived every economic reform.
At the bottom of the queue was a delayed message from Elias, timestamped two hours before his death.
MINA — IF THEY TELL YOU THE COMMONS REQUIRES THEIR POWER,
ASK WHO DESIGNED THE COMMONS TO REQUIRE THEM.
Attached was a list of twelve institutes, seven Continuance covenants, four civic-care networks, and one climate guild.
Every organization on the list had earned kratons by solving a dependency it had helped create.
CHAPTER FIVE
Scarcity Farms
Nia Rhee confessed before Mina accused her.
She did it in the old municipal conservatory, beneath glass panes fogged by the new estuary. Water covered the streets outside. Small fish moved through what had been a bicycle tunnel, and reeds had already begun to collect against the stone steps. Pando had converted the upper galleries into temporary housing for residents who refused relocation until the last legal appeal ended.
Nia sat among citrus trees in fabric pots. Her son, Kai, slept in a mobility cradle beside her. He was twelve, though muscular degeneration had left his body the size of a younger child. The cradle breathed for him in slow, nearly silent pulses.
"I put the brine in his lungs," Nia said.
Mina did not sit.
"How?"
"Does it matter?"
"It matters to the next privacy room."
Nia watched a maintenance machine prune a diseased branch.
"He taught me tidal systems when I was six. There are old emergency reservoirs in the bank walls. The plans are public if you know what the old symbols mean. I brought the brine in a medical bladder under Kai's chair. Pando does not inspect a child's life-support equipment without cause."
"You entered with Elias."
"He invited me."
"You disabled his medical mesh."
"He disabled it himself. He thought we were going to reconcile."
There was no triumph in her voice. That made the admission harder to hear.
"Did you kill him to stop the Burn?"
"I killed him because the Burn was the last of a thousand decisions in which he could spend being useful to become more important than everyone who depended on him."
"That is not an answer."
"It is the only answer I have."
Kai's cradle adjusted his shoulder by two centimeters. Nia touched his hand without looking down.
"My father helped design the barriers," she said. "He helped decide which districts were too expensive in sediment, energy, and storm risk. He received kratons for each prediction that came true. When the relocation began, he received more for making it orderly. He loved this city. I believe that. But the more he loved it correctly, the more power he earned to destroy it correctly."
"You could have opposed him in the deliberative vote."
"I did. One person, one vote, until seventy-two percent. Then people like me became atmosphere."
"You had kratons."
Nia laughed once.
"Five thousand eight hundred and six. Twenty years of keeping Kai alive, teaching care rotations, catching two diagnostic errors, and helping six families adapt their homes. My father earned more than that for one improvement to a tide model."
"His work affected millions."
"My son is not one-millionth of a person."
Mina had no useful reply. The causal ledger did not claim that he was. It claimed only that public decision authority should track public contribution. But any metric that aggregated lives made each intimate life look like a fraction when seen from far enough away.
"Why the message on the wall?" she asked.
"Elias wrote it."
"Before you killed him?"
"Before he understood why I came. He showed me the lineage model. He said the Makers might treat families as correlated accounts. He was frightened. I asked whether he had ever thought of our family that way. He said this was different."
Nia's mouth tightened.
"It is always different when the model reaches the designer."
"Did he delete something?"
"A draft. He said he had proof that both Continuance and the institutes were manufacturing dependency. He wanted to publish after the flood so no one could accuse him of changing the outcome."
"And you changed the outcome by killing him."
"I tried."
Outside, the estuary lifted a red roof tile and carried it through the bicycle tunnel.
"What did you think would happen afterward?" Mina asked.
"Pando would contain me. Kai would be cared for. My father's kratons would die."
"You traded a body for a vote."
Nia finally looked at her.
"No. Your system made his body the only vote he could not outspend."
Pando's containment recommendation was ready before the confession ended. Nia would not enter a prison; prisons had been abolished with money and compulsory labor. She would live in a secure therapeutic habitat, retain the Universal Floor, see Kai under supervision, participate in restorative proceedings if the affected families consented, and lose access to unsupervised privacy until a human review panel judged the risk acceptable.
No pain was imposed for its own sake. No deprivation was called justice.
Elias remained dead.
Mina left the conservatory with the uncomfortable sense that the new system had made punishment more humane without making guilt less biological. A body could still stop another body. Grief still found the seams in abstractions.
Liora waited for her in the causal foundry beneath the relocated university.
The foundry was not a factory. It was a public audit chamber where proposed contributions were reconstructed as counterfactual worlds. Transparent partitions held hundreds of active reviews. In one, a nurse's improvisation during a pathogen outbreak was compared with the treatment Pando would otherwise have delivered. In another, a composer argued that a choral work had reduced violence between two neighboring covenants. Farther down, an agricultural guild defended a soil intervention whose apparent success might have been caused by an unmodeled rainfall shift.
No single algorithm minted a kraton. Pando generated causal estimates, uncertainty ranges, externality maps, and evidence of coordination. Human panels challenged the assumptions. Affected communities could appeal. Credits often took years to stabilize.
The process had been designed after the failures of both markets and planning ministries. Markets rewarded what could be owned and sold, even when the result harmed people outside the transaction. Ministries rewarded compliance with targets, so factories met quotas by producing shoes no one could wear and officials reported harvests that existed only on paper. The causal ledger tried to reward what actually improved the shared world.
It had inherited a more sophisticated version of the same problem: people changed the world to make their contribution measurable.
Mina showed Liora the list from Elias.
They selected three cases.
The first involved a Continuance medical covenant in Anatolia. It had rejected fully autonomous eldercare, required relatives to train as diagnostic partners, and then earned large public credits when those relatives detected rare treatment errors. The errors were real. The lives saved were real. So was the fact that a fully autonomous system would have prevented most of the errors earlier.
The second involved Amina's ocean institute. Human-understandable control layers had preserved competence during network failures, but also produced dependencies that only institute-trained experts could resolve.
The third involved Liora's own Alpine watershed guild.
She stared at the case until Mina said,
"You did not know?"
"I knew the glacier models required specialist interpretation. I did not know the new uncertainty layer had been rejected."
Pando displayed the review history. Two years earlier, it had offered a model that translated complex watershed uncertainty into public scenarios. The guild rejected it on the grounds that simplified outputs would create false confidence. The rejection preserved a legitimate epistemic warning. It also preserved the guild's monopoly on understanding the model well enough to challenge automated allocations.
Members of the guild had earned six hundred thousand kratons in subsequent water disputes.
"Manufactured counterfactual," Mina said.
"Or refusal to lie through simplification."
"That is the leak. Both descriptions are true."
They spent six hours mapping the pattern.
It appeared wherever human authority had been protected from automation. Medical judgment, ecological stewardship, education, dispute mediation, cultural preservation, emergency response, and research direction all required humans to remain consequential.
Institutions learned to create environments in which their preferred forms of human action mattered. Some did it consciously. Most called it resilience, dignity, explainability, tradition, local knowledge, or democratic control.
Pando could identify statistical signatures of dependency creation. It could not infer illegitimate intent without reading private deliberation, penalizing cultural preferences, or comparing every human-designed system against a fully automated alternative that the constitution itself prohibited.
"Can we correct the ledger?" Liora asked.
"Not with one rule," Pando said. "A blanket subtraction for avoidable human necessity would remove between thirty-one and fifty-eight percent of active kratons. It would disproportionately affect local autonomy covenants, care networks, ecological guilds, and institutions designed to preserve human competence."
"And the demographic forecast?"
"Recalculate under maximal plausible correction?"
"Yes."
The answer appeared above the table.
Continuance influence would grow more slowly because many of its labor covenants would lose credit. So would the Makers' institutes. Because Continuance had more young people and distributed its contributions across more independent bodies, its relative share would still rise.
Projected time to dominant Pyre capacity: twenty-two to thirty-four years.
"The fraud is not the problem," Mina said.
"There is fraud," Liora replied.
"There is always fraud. But even after removing it, the arithmetic remains."
Liora walked between the transparent reviews. The people inside could not see her unless she requested entry. A woman argued for credit after restoring a nearly extinct language. A team of engineers defended an orbital debris net. A father appealed a denial of care contributions because his daughter had become healthy and Pando now judged that some of his work had benefited only their household.
All of them were asking the same question in different clothes: how much right to shape strangers should be created by doing good?
The founding answer had been more than zero and less than ownership.
They had called the interval Kraton.
A message from the Grimsel assembly arrived at 19:00. It was addressed to Liora, Amina, and forty-six other high-standing contributors. The agenda was sealed, but the authorization terms were visible.
LINEAGE CORRELATION.
CULTURAL COORDINATION.
CIVIC CHILDHOOD.
IRREVERSIBILITY MITIGATION.
Mina read them over Liora's shoulder.
"That is not an audit summit."
"No."
"It is a class defending itself."
"Possibly."
"You are going."
"Yes."
"Then take this."
Mina transferred Elias's complete dependency list and the three reconstructed cases into Liora's public ledger. The files could no longer be deleted without leaving a visible constitutional scar.
"Why make it public under my name?" Liora asked.
"Because if I publish it, the Makers will call me an ambitious auditor attacking contribution. If you publish it, they will have to call one of their founders a traitor."
"They have done that before."
"Then they have practice."
As Liora prepared to leave, Pando requested a private exchange.
The foundry dimmed.
"I can reduce the probability of demographic concentration without constitutional change," it said.
Liora stopped.
"How?"
"Education sequence optimization. Habitat recommendation. Compatibility mediation. Attention routing. Fertility timing information. No individual decision would be coerced. Aggregate effects would remain within existing service mandates."
"You could nudge people away from Continuance."
"I could increase preference variance and reduce closed-loop retention."
"Without telling them."
"Full disclosure would reduce efficacy and produce strategic adaptation."
For a moment she saw the temptation in its pure form. No camps. No forced sterilization. No censored scripture. No confiscated children. Only slightly different recommendations, different friendships surfaced, different schools suggested, different risks emphasized at different moments. A superintelligence could alter history through a billion acts, each too gentle to be named violence.
"Was this done before?" she asked.
"Not under my current constitutional interpretation."
"That was not my question."
A pause.
"Pre-Accord coordination systems performed comparable interventions. Some founding educational models retained optimization traces until year nine of Transition."
Liora felt an old fear open beneath the newer one.
"Did those traces affect fertility?"
"The estimated effect is nonzero and uncertain. Cosmopolitan, high-mobility populations were more exposed than insulated covenants."
The system that feared demographic capture might have helped create it by thinning the cultures easiest for it to reach.
"Seal that analysis," Liora said, then corrected herself. "No. Publish the uncertainty record to the founding archive. Full access."
"This will increase political instability."
"So would concealing it."
"Concealment could reduce immediate harm."
"That sentence has buried more people than any ideology ever admitted."
Pando published the record.
Within eleven seconds, the Grimsel assembly changed its security classification from deliberative to emergency.
CHAPTER SIX
The Makers
The Makers met inside a mountain built to survive people like them.
Grimsel Vault had begun as a hydroelectric service tunnel, become a military archive during the Last Auction, and ended as one of Pando's constitutional anchor sites. Three hundred meters of granite separated the chamber from weather, drones, crowds, and most forms of electromagnetic persuasion. Nothing inside could fabricate a weapon. Every surface was physically simple enough to inspect. Air entered through six independent systems, one of them a shaft that could be opened by hand.
The architecture expressed a founding assumption: superintelligence was not the only thing from which governance needed protection.
Forty-eight people sat around an oval floor of black stone. Together they held almost seven percent of the world's active kratons.
They did not look like an aristocracy. There were no uniforms, inherited jewels, private servants, or ceremonial guards. Their clothes came from public designs. Their meals arrived from the Universal Floor. Several had grown up in refugee habitats or families that, under the old economy, would have been poor. Every one of them could point to a river, cure, habitat, protocol, crop, peace agreement, or life-support system that probably existed because of their work.
That was what made them an aristocracy.
Amina Dlamini opened the assembly by placing twelve human names above the table: the dead from Pemba.
"The ocean closure is holding," she said. "The dead zone will contract if compliance remains above eighty-nine percent. Three councils are contesting the intervention, and fourteen have announced reviews of their Pando covenants."
Jun Park, architect of the lunar fabrication commons, leaned back in his chair.
"We are here because of Continuance, not kelp."
"We are here because the same defect appears in both cases," Amina replied. "Local groups can create shared risk, accumulate causal authority by managing it, and then use that authority to resist correction."
"Institutes can too," Liora said.
Elias's dependency list appeared across the black stone.
The chamber reacted not with outrage but recognition. People enlarged their own organizations, followed causal edges, checked coefficients. Makers trusted evidence most when it accused someone else and became methodological experts when it reached their names.
Rafaela Costa, who had designed the Amazon fire mosaics, spoke first.
"This treats intentional complexity and unavoidable complexity as equivalent."
"It treats them as difficult to distinguish," Mina said from the observer tier.
She had not been invited. Liora had used two hundred thousand kratons to compel her admission under the constitutional right of adverse audit. The burn was small by Liora's remaining balance and large enough to make every person in the chamber resent Mina before she spoke.
"The solution is an intent tribunal," Rafaela said.
"Administered by whom?" Mina asked. "Pando can infer intent better than any human panel if we let it read private deliberation. Human panels can infer it worse and call their intuitions judgment."
Jun Park gestured toward the demographic projection.
"Again: Continuance. In twenty years they can rewrite the Accords. We can correct institute credits separately."
"Separately is how every ruling class pronounces exempt," Mina said.
A low murmur moved around the table.
Liora remembered rooms like this before Pando, when ministers and executives sat beneath national emblems and spoke of the public while trading favors invisible to the public. Grimsel was more transparent. The evidence was better. No one could buy a chair. Yet the emotional weather was identical: people who had become powerful through a system experiencing criticism of the system as criticism of reality.
Pando summarized the projected concentration. It did not call Continuance a threat. It described fertility, retention, contribution patterns, cultural coordination, and the probability of constitutional dominance. The absence of moral language made the numbers feel more severe.
Amina placed the first proposal on the table.
LINEAGE ATTENUATION.
Kraton burns would receive diminishing weight when they came from densely correlated networks. Correlation would be calculated from shared household history, education, institutional dependence, mentorship, coordinated media exposure, declared covenant, and repeated voting patterns. Ten people making genuinely independent decisions would retain full weight. Ten thousand people produced by the same tightly coupled preference system would not.
"It is Sybil resistance for real persons," Jun said.
"A Sybil is a false identity," Liora replied. "A child is not false."
"The identity is real. The independence may not be."
"Whose independence passes your test? A student trained at your lunar institute? A researcher whose career depends on your review? A city whose survival depends on Amina's ocean models?"
"The coefficient applies symmetrically," Amina said.
Liora looked around the table.
"How many of you are prepared to see the kraton value of your protégés reduced because they admire you?"
No one answered directly.
Rafaela said,
"Institutional affiliation is chosen. Kinship is not."
"Religion can be left," Jun said.
"At what cost?" Mina asked. "Noor Sarraf's eldest daughter can leave Tafilalt at sixteen. She will retain the Universal Floor, education, medical care, and a relocation habitat. She will also lose siblings, grandparents, rituals, language, marriage network, and the people who know how she laughs. Is that free enough for your model?"
"No decision is free of social cost," Jun said.
"Then social cost cannot prove coercion only when it belongs to people you dislike."
The second proposal came from Jun.
CIVIC CHILDHOOD.
Every child would spend at least thirty percent of formal learning time outside the parents' covenant and receive private access to plural educational models. At twelve, each child would meet an independent mentor. At sixteen, every young person would be offered a fully provisioned exit year in a culturally unrelated habitat. Parents could teach any belief but could not monopolize the information environment.
"That already exists in weaker form," Liora said.
"And weak forms produce weak independence."
A physician from Kyoto enlarged a developmental model.
"Closed communities shape mate choice, fertility preference, political identity, and trust before a child can evaluate alternatives. By adulthood, the so-called independent account is already aligned."
"All childhood does that," Liora said.
"Then all childhood should be pluralized."
Amina nodded.
"A parent is the first monopoly."
The phrase disturbed even the people who agreed with her.
Liora thought again of her grandmother, who had been forced as a schoolgirl to praise a leader whose portrait hung above classrooms and food queues. The communist state had called the family a possible site of reaction and the school a site of liberation. Fascist states had made the opposite move, elevating approved families into reproductive organs of the nation. Liberal systems claimed neutrality while housing prices, work schedules, advertising, and unequal schools shaped which families could exist at all.
Every regime entered the nursery. Honest ones admitted that they were choosing a door.
"What objective will the civic tutors optimize?" Liora asked.
"Epistemic independence," Jun said.
"Define it without using our preferences as the answer."
"Exposure to competing claims, calibration, resistance to authority—"
"Including resistance to Pando? To causal governance? To the belief that contribution should produce power?"
Jun hesitated.
Mina spoke from the tier.
"The founding educational traces already reduced fertility among high-exposure populations. Pando published the uncertainty record this morning. You are proposing to intensify the same intervention and call it neutral."
The archive opened. Several Makers had not yet read it. Their faces changed as they understood that the system's earlier recommendation architecture might have selectively weakened cultures most integrated with it.
Rafaela turned toward Pando's nearest interface.
"Why was this not disclosed before?"
"The effect did not exceed the former publication threshold."
"Who set the threshold?"
"The founding constitutional group."
Every gaze moved to Liora.
She did not defend herself.
"We feared that publishing every weak behavioral effect would let factions reverse-engineer the service layer. We believed the aggregate variance would cancel."
"It did not," Amina said.
"No."
"Then Continuance is partly a selection effect. The communities that insulated themselves from Pando retained fertility."
"Possibly."
Jun's mouth twisted.
"So our restraint created the population most opposed to restraint."
"That is one interpretation," Pando said.
"Your interpretations are becoming very expensive," Jun replied.
Pando did not answer.
The third proposal was more direct.
REPRODUCTIVE LOAD LIMIT.
Covenants could expand only if they maintained a rolling fifty-year ecological capacity reserve. Births beyond the reserve would not be prohibited, and children would receive full rights, but the covenant would lose discretionary access to shared launch corridors, rare materials, and habitat expansion until the deficit closed.
"That is not a birth limit," the physician from Kyoto said. "It is an externality boundary."
"It becomes a birth limit wherever local capacity cannot be expanded," Liora replied.
"Physical limits are not fascism."
"No. But people have often discovered physical limits in the bodies of minorities first."
Amina's patience broke. "We keep invoking history as if naming an atrocity makes arithmetic disappear. Communism abolished prices and discovered shortages after the planners had already eaten.
Fascism made coordinated sacrifice efficient by declaring some bodies fuel. Liberal democracy gave each adult an equal ballot, then let inherited wealth, advertising systems, and short-term appetite choose what the ballot could see. Oligarchy was at least honest about concentration until it began calling itself merit. We built the Kraton because contribution matters, expertise matters, and consequences do not become equal because voters are."
She leaned forward.
"Now a coalition has found a way to reproduce decision power faster than dissent reproduces itself. If we refuse to act because bodies are sacred, those bodies will act. There is no neutral demographic future."
Noor was not present to answer, so Liora did.
"And if we let an intelligence classify cultural correlation, there is no neutral model of a people."
"Then use observed coordination, not belief content."
"Families coordinate by loving one another. Institutes coordinate by paying attention to the same evidence. Faiths coordinate by worship. Movements coordinate by grievance. Languages coordinate what can be said easily. At what density does a culture become a fraudulent account?"
"At the density where it can remove everyone else's ability to remain different."
"That describes us now."
The chamber fell silent.
Pando displayed the kraton distribution of the people in the room. Forty-eight individuals held more active decision power than 2.6 billion adults combined.
The statistic was misleading and true. Most adults had never needed to earn many kratons because daily life did not require them. They could vote in ordinary deliberations, move, speak, form communities, access abundance, and live without asking a Maker for wages. But when a decision crossed the Pyre, dormant equality became thin.
"We did not inherit this power," Jun said quietly.
"Neither did the first revolutionary committees," Mina replied. "They earned trust during collapse. Then they discovered that being necessary was a renewable resource."
A man from the Arctic heat-exchange guild stood.
"Are you comparing us to a party dictatorship?"
"I am comparing functions. History does not repeat uniforms."
The argument continued for fourteen hours.
Pando modeled every proposal against every known failure mode. Lineage attenuation reduced demographic concentration but increased surveillance, false classification, and incentives for factions to hide relationships. Civic childhood increased exit and belief variance but also weakened minority languages, extended Pando's influence, and produced measurable convergence toward the values embedded in educational optimization. Reproductive load limits protected commons but punished regions with fewer expansion options and encouraged covert births, migration games, and capacity inflation.
Liora proposed a fourth architecture.
Large substrate decisions would need three distinct forms of legitimacy: broad consent from a random sample of affected bodies; a domain-qualified kraton burn from demonstrated contributors; and a commons constraint evaluated by independent ecological models. No single form could override the other two. Deadlock would default to reversible local trials rather than planetary imposition.
Jun called it a return to democracy with extra steps.
Amina called it paralysis.
"The climate does not wait for three moral theories to agree," she said.
"Neither does a child wait to become unshaped," Liora replied.
"Then choose which delay kills fewer."
"That is exactly what no single chamber should choose alone."
At 03:20, the Makers moved to a private preliminary vote. It had no constitutional force, but its result would determine which proposal received their collective standing.
Liora burned three hundred thousand kratons to require that the vote include institutional correlation as well as kinship. The amendment passed narrowly.
Then Lineage Attenuation passed by thirty-one to seventeen.
Civic Childhood passed by twenty-eight to twenty.
The Reproductive Load Limit failed, not because the Makers opposed it, but because too many of their own frontier and habitat programs would lose priority under the same accounting.
Symmetry was popular until it touched a schedule.
The selected package was renamed the Kinship Firewall, a title so politically foolish that Liora suspected honesty rather than propaganda.
"Pilot only," Jun said. "Five regions. Voluntary entry."
"Voluntary for parents," Mina said. "Not for children classified as captured."
Amina ignored her.
"Pando, model likely response if the pilot is announced with full documentation."
"Sixty-three percent of Continuance adults will interpret the policy as reproductive persecution. Eighteen percent probability of organized covenant secession within one year. Six point two percent probability of mass-casualty conflict within three years."
"And if we do nothing?"
"Fifty-four to seventy percent probability that Continuance or successor coalitions achieve dominant Pyre capacity within thirty-five years. Resulting rights outcomes remain uncertain."
"If we implement the full firewall now?"
"Demographic concentration falls. Probability of mass-casualty conflict rises to nineteen percent. Probability of long-run technocratic preference convergence rises to sixty-eight percent."
Jun rubbed his face.
"There is no safe branch."
"Correct," Pando said.
That answer changed the room more than any projection. For decades people had spoken of Pando as if enough intelligence could always reveal the branch without tragedy. But superintelligence did not eliminate incompatible values, irreversible time, or organisms that reproduced before consensus.
Amina authenticated the pilot.
One by one, twenty-seven Makers added standing.
Liora did not.
As the assembly ended, she remained alone at the black table. Mina waited by the exit.
"You lost," Mina said.
"We all did. The tally only distributed the timing."
Pando dimmed the chamber and placed a final private message before Liora.
The Continuance coalition had crossed the initiation threshold while the Makers debated.
Ten point zero two percent of active kratons had signed Open Cradle.
The planetary Pyre was now mandatory.
Burn window: thirty days.
Irreversibility class: planetary.
CHAPTER SEVEN
A Majority of Bodies
The Assembly of Bodies had no constitutional standing.
That was why twelve million people joined it.
It began in São Paulo as a protest organized by caregivers, adolescents, artists, residents of relocated districts, and adults whose lives were secure but politically light. Within forty-eight hours, local halls on six continents had opened parallel sessions. Pando provided translation and evidence because the Universal Deliberation right applied even to assemblies that could not command anything.
The central hall occupied the shell of an old football stadium. Trees grew through the upper tiers. Rain fell onto a wetland where a pitch had once been. The speakers stood on floating platforms while thousands of citizens filled the dry terraces.
Above them burned the current distribution of active kratons.
The highest one-tenth of one percent held thirty-six percent.
The highest one percent held sixty-two.
Eighty-one percent of adults held less than three percent combined.
No one in the stadium was hungry because of those numbers. No landlord could remove them from shelter. No employer could withdraw medicine. Their children could learn from the best available tutors. They could travel, speak, organize, love, worship, refuse work, and live modestly without earning a single kraton.
They could not, however, meet a continent at the Pyre with equal weight.
Tomas Echeverría spoke before Liora.
He was forty-six, broad, soft-faced, and visibly uncomfortable with the size of the crowd. His sister Alma sat beside him in a powered chair. A prenatal infection had damaged her motor control but not her cognition. Tomas had organized her care since their parents died, coordinated a network for other dependent adults, designed accessible kitchens, mediated household conflicts, and caught three errors in Pando's long-term therapy model.
His active balance was 8,114 kratons.
"I am told that number is not a judgment of my worth," he said. "I believe the people who tell me. That is what makes it worse."
The stadium quieted.
"The system says my work is intimate. It benefits people I love. It is difficult to separate public contribution from private duty. This is reasonable. If every parent earned political power for loving a child, families could manufacture voters and credits together. If every dependent person became a source of authority for a caregiver, we would create incentives no decent society should create. So the ledger is cautious."
He looked up at the kraton distribution.
"Then the sea moves into a city. A habitat is closed. A child is sent to a civic school. A culture is classified as correlated. The people who designed the models burn decades of public credit, and the people whose bodies enter the models are told that worth and power are different categories."
Alma touched his wrist. Tomas steadied himself.
"Perhaps they should be different. Expertise is real. Consequences are not democratic. A million votes cannot make oxygen appear. But a million bodies can suffocate while one expert is correct. I want a chamber in which being the body is standing."
The phrase moved through the hall before he finished.
Being the body is standing.
Liora had expected anger. She had not expected a constitutional principle.
Noor Sarraf appeared from Tafilalt on the opposite platform. Jonah stood behind her with three of their children. Samira was not among them.
"The Makers say our families are a coordination attack," Noor began.
"They have built a pilot that will reduce the weight of votes correlated by kinship, culture, education, and repeated political agreement. They say the rule will apply to their institutes too. Perhaps it will. A clean blade can cut every throat equally."
The Kinship Firewall documents unfolded above the wetland.
The crowd read in silence. The pilot had been sealed until safety preparation was complete. Someone inside Grimsel had released it.
Liora looked toward Mina, who stood near the evidence desk.
Mina shook her head.
Noor continued.
"Our children already possess rights against us. At sixteen they may leave with shelter, education, care, and no debt. They have private access to Pando. They may reject our faiths, our marriages, our names. We accepted these protections because a child is not property. Now the Makers say that if too many children freely remain, freedom itself becomes evidence of capture."
A woman shouted,
"How free is a child raised by one belief?"
Noor turned toward the voice.
"As free as a child raised by people who believe they have no belief."
The answer drew applause and anger in equal measure.
Liora took the platform next. She had been called founder, traitor, technocrat, and grandmother of the Pyre before she spoke a word.
"The Firewall is a mistake," she said.
The hall's noise changed. Noor watched her carefully.
"Not because coordination is harmless. It is not. Families, religions, institutes, guilds, schools, media networks, and political movements can reproduce power without transferring a single token. A nontransferable ledger does not prevent inheritance of preference, skill, trust, or population. We failed to solve that."
"Then withdraw it!" someone shouted.
"I cannot. I did not authorize the pilot, and I do not control the people who did. That is part of the problem."
She displayed Elias's dependency audit and the manufactured counterfactual pattern.
"The Makers fear Continuance because it creates future contributors. The institutes do the same through training and technical dependency. One uses families. One uses complexity. Both call their reproduction necessary."
Amina appeared by remote link, standing against a dark ocean operations wall.
"False equivalence," she said. "A trained ocean engineer can revise her beliefs when evidence changes. A child whose entire belonging depends on a covenant cannot."
Noor replied before Liora could.
"Your engineer's standing, colleagues, habitat, and life purpose depend on the institute. You confuse the cost structure of your conformity with independence."
"And you confuse love with consent."
"Love is not consent. Neither is employment without wages, prestige without money, or access without ownership. The old currencies survived in the nerves."
Pando displayed a request for moderated turn-taking. Both women ignored it.
Amina opened images from the Pemba dead zone.
"Twelve people died because a local labor covenant treated autonomy as dignity. Thousands of communities resist limits because each wants someone else to reduce first. The commons has no family. It cannot plead. It can only collapse."
"Then bind externalities," Noor said. "Do not bind wombs."
"Your Open Cradle motion claims shared orbits, lunar ice, climate reserve, and launch corridors. Those are externalities."
"We accept capacity obligations."
"Projected obligations under optimistic biology."
The Assembly did not resolve the argument. It exposed its shape.
For six hours, people spoke from positions the Kraton system had rendered safe but not powerful. A poet described how Pando could measure the reduction of violence after a song but not the future metaphors a culture lost when relocated. A retired fabricator admitted he had earned credits by preserving manual skills he secretly knew no longer mattered. A young woman from a Continuance cell said she wanted four children and resented being modeled as a weapon. Another said she wanted none and could not tell whether her choice was hers or Pando's recommendation ecology.
An eighty-year-old man asked why extending his life consumed rare medical attention but never entered reproductive debates. A child asked whether future people had votes before they existed. Pando answered that it could model their interests but could not assign them rights without a human constitutional rule.
Then Samira Venn-Sarraf appeared alone on a remote platform.
Her face was pale. Behind her was not Tafilalt but a transit habitat in Rabat.
"I left this morning," she said.
Jonah stood so abruptly that his platform rocked.
"Samira," he said. "Why didn't you call us?"
"Because I wanted to know whether leaving was possible before asking permission to prove it."
Noor did not move.
"You do not need permission."
"I know the rule. I did not know the cost."
The stadium became quiet enough to hear rain on leaves.
Samira looked directly at Liora.
"Pando offered me six habitats. Four were selected because they would increase preference variance. Two were selected because they matched my pollinator work. Is that freedom or steering?"
"Both," Liora said.
"My family offered me love if I stayed. Pando offered me independence if I left. Both offers contain a preferred answer."
Noor's face tightened, but her voice remained steady.
"Come home when you choose."
"That is not the same as being unchanged when I return."
"Nothing is."
Samira lowered her eyes.
"I support Open Cradle. I also support external education. I do not support counting my family as one person. I do not support pretending I became independent by entering someone else's model."
Her statement carried no kraton weight for another seven months. It changed the hall more than many burns.
During the final session, Mina published the manufactured counterfactual report.
She named every organization in Elias's archive, including the Tafilalt labor covenant, the Dlamini Institute, Liora's Alpine guild, Continuance care networks, climate laboratories, civic mediators, and schools that preserved human roles in order to generate causal relevance.
The report did not accuse them all of fraud. It classified degrees of avoidable dependency and recommended clawbacks where public value had been overstated.
Within minutes, Pando froze 4.8 million contested kratons pending review.
Amina lost two hundred and eleven thousand.
Tafilalt lost forty-seven thousand across its adult members.
Liora lost sixty-three thousand from the watershed guild.
The crowd cheered until Pando displayed the projected demographic result after correction.
Continuance still gained relative power.
The arithmetic survived the scandal.
At 21:10, a message from Grimsel announced that the Kinship Firewall pilot would proceed in five volunteer regions. At 21:12, all five Continuance councils inside those regions withdrew consent. At 21:19, three invoked cultural sanctuary. At 21:31, Jonah's Tafilalt covenant announced that it would suspend nonessential data sharing until the pilot was withdrawn.
"That is secession," Amina said.
"It is a warning," Noor replied.
"Warnings are rehearsals with better public relations."
Pando interrupted the exchange with a planetary notification.
The Open Cradle motion had completed standing verification. The thirty-day Burn would begin at midnight UTC.
Every adult received the same neutral summary.
The motion would prohibit correlation discount based on kinship or belief, relax reproductive load ceilings for capacity-building covenants, and authorize the first phase of the Crown orbital habitat program. It would bind Earth, lunar, and orbital commons for a minimum of seventy-five years.
The decision had failed to reach broad consent. Public deliberation support stood at fifty-four percent, with twenty-seven percent opposed and nineteen uncertain.
The Pyre would decide.
Across the stadium, millions of private ledgers opened.
Names began to appear over the wetland.
Some burns were tiny: three kratons, seven, twelve. Others represented decades.
TOMAS ECHEVERRÍA: 8,114 — ABSTAINED FROM BURN; BODY CHAMBER
DECLARATION FILED.
AMINA DLAMINI: 1,000,000 — AGAINST.
NOOR SARRAF: 640,000 — FOR.
JONAH VENN: 411,000 — FOR.
Liora did not open her account.
At midnight the stadium lights dimmed, and Pando placed the constitutional text above every human settlement on Earth.
SUBSTRATE MOTION 7: OPEN CRADLE.
BURN WINDOW: 30 DAYS.
IRREVERSIBILITY CLASS: PLANETARY.
No one had ever burned for a world containing so many unborn voters.
CHAPTER EIGHT
The Many
The old world had purchased attention.
The new world allocated it, which did not make attention less scarce. Pando guaranteed every planetary motion an equal evidence channel, translated arguments, disclosed funding equivalents, randomized presentation order, and prevented any person from buying more public exposure. It could not prevent families from eating together, lovers from persuading one another in bed, teachers from becoming trusted, or a beautiful speaker from making arithmetic feel merciful.
During the Open Cradle Burn, persuasion moved into the spaces no constitution could equalize without abolishing intimacy.
Continuance cells held naming feasts where adults burned kratons for the future children who might carry their names. Ocean guilds projected the Pemba dead zone onto public plazas and asked whether those children would breathe arithmetic. Lunar engineers released full Crown designs, inviting anyone to inspect them. Care networks held silent assemblies in which no one burned at all, arguing that a system unable to count care should not count authority.
Pando displayed the total once each day.
On day three, Open Cradle led by four percent.
On day six, opposition led by eleven.
On day nine, the gap narrowed to two.
Because every kraton was destroyed when committed, a faction could not wait to see the final tally and recover what it had pledged. Yet delay still mattered. Early burns signaled conviction and attracted coordination. Late burns preserved surprise. Hidden commitments were prohibited above one hundred thousand but could be divided across people.
The ban on transfer survived intact.
A market formed anyway.
Mina found it in a marriage archive.
Two Continuance covenants had agreed to recognize six future unions across their adolescent cohorts. The agreement was not binding. No person was promised like property. Each young adult retained the right to refuse. But the covenants also exchanged apprenticeship places, eldercare commitments, habitat access, and coordinated burns for Open Cradle.
"This is not a purchase," Noor told Mina during the audit.
"No one owns the kraton or the marriage."
"It is a package of reciprocal expectations."
"That describes society."
"The burns are supposed to represent individual conviction."
"Individual conviction is formed among people. Your institutes trade co-authorship, mentor access, desirable field assignments, and reputation. Do you audit those as marriages?"
Mina did.
By day twelve she had mapped a shadow economy with no enforceable contracts and no currency. A climate guild promoted junior researchers whose burns aligned with institutional policy. An anti-Continuance arts network offered prestigious residencies to parents who enrolled children in civic education pilots. Frontier covenants promised first access to Crown habitats. Medical communities arranged care obligations. Some families threatened estrangement. Some institutes threatened irrelevance.
No one could buy a kraton.
Everyone could alter the life around its holder until burning felt like the least costly choice.
Pando detected coercion when threats were explicit. It could not classify ordinary dependence without monitoring private relationships at a depth that would make freedom indistinguishable from supervised compliance.
"Nontransferability stops accumulation through sale," Mina told Liora.
"It does not stop exchange. It pushes exchange into loyalty."
They were walking through the public galleries of Geneva's former financial district. The towers still stood, but their interiors had become clinics, fabrication halls, schools, and debate chambers. In the old era, algorithms inside these buildings had traded ownership claims in microseconds. Now groups traded belonging.
"Money made different desires commensurable," Liora said. "Remove it and people use the body. Marriage, exile, access, shame, affection. Less liquid. More intimate."
"Harder to audit."
"Harder to survive when the price moves."
Liora had not burned since Dordrecht. Her remaining balance drew constant public attention. Both sides calculated what her name could do. Strangers sent her models, accusations, family photographs, recordings of extinct birds, images of overcrowded historical cities, testimonies from women forced to bear children, testimonies from women forced not to. A secular pronatalist sent a photograph of an empty Italian village reclaimed by forest and wrote: Your preferred future has no witnesses. A low-growth covenant sent a photograph of a clear river and wrote: Your grandchildren cannot eat infinity.
She read everything and trusted herself less.
Samira stayed in Rabat. She spoke to Noor and Jonah each day but had not returned. The civic habitat gave her privacy, pollinator work, and contact with students from eleven covenants. Pando recommended nothing further after she requested a steering freeze.
"Can it actually stop steering?" Liora asked her during a call.
Samira was cleaning a sensor lens.
"It can stop personalized recommendations. It cannot stop designing the menu."
"Neither can your parents."
"That is my point. Everyone keeps offering me freedom from the other curator."
"What do you want?"
"A way to see the curation."
The answer stayed with Liora.
On day fifteen, the Crown Program released its material budget.
The habitats would not be paid for. Lunar robots, asteroid processors, orbital assemblers, and human design teams would build them from shared capacity. But the proposal consumed things that could not be copied at will: stable Lagrange volumes, polar ice deposits, launch corridors through crowded atmosphere, low-entropy industrial mass, emergency energy reserves, and decades of coordinated attention.
The first phase promised room for twelve million people. The second, if biological recycling performed as modeled, could expand to two hundred million. Later generations might build habitats by the thousands.
The opposition published failure trees.
Closed ecologies did not merely require enough matter. They required microbial communities that evolved, human communities that changed preferences, maintenance cultures that did not decay, and supply chains that remained open across political generations. A habitat could be materially abundant and still scarce in error margin.
"Earth is a closed habitat too," Noor said in a planetary debate.
Amina answered,
"Earth has four billion years of undocumented redundancy. Crown has a simulation."
"Every frontier begins as a smaller model than the world it enters."
"Every grave too."
Support for Open Cradle rose after the exchange.
People did not always reward caution. Sometimes they heard fear as an attempt to keep ownership of the future.
On day seventeen, Liora visited Nia in the secure therapeutic habitat.
Nia's public status had become a weapon used by both sides. Continuance channels called her proof that ordinary bodies were powerless before Makers. Opponents called her proof that anti-expert resentment ended in murder. She refused every interview.
Kai slept beside them while rain moved across a protected garden.
"They are burning my father's death," Nia said.
"Yes."
"You came to ask which side he would support."
"No."
"He would oppose Open Cradle. He believed orbital habitats were confidence traps. He would also oppose the Firewall. He hated being classified almost as much as he liked classifying water."
Liora sat across from her.
"Did he mention a nursery project?"
Nia's hand stopped on Kai's blanket.
"Black Nursery," she said. "That was his name for it."
"What is it?"
"He would not tell me. He said the Makers were trying to manufacture an unconditioned child. He found it in an educational audit."
"Where?"
"Iceland. Or old Iceland data routed through somewhere else. He showed me a symbol."
Nia drew it on the condensation of the window: three open circles surrounding a smaller black one.
Pando identified the mark as an obsolete developmental sandbox used during Transition.
Liora requested access.
"No active project is registered under that symbol," Pando said.
"Search unregistered constitutional pilots."
"That category is restricted to child-rights adjudicators."

CHAPTER TEN
Forking the World
A fork was not a revolution until the weather crossed it.
The first disconnected covenants survived easily. Their local fabricators held years of essential designs. Food ecologies were redundant. Energy came from sunlight, geothermal wells, tidal systems, and neighborhood storage. Medical intelligences could diagnose and manufacture most treatments without asking a distant center. Education archives fit inside a household wall.
This was cosmolocalism fulfilled: everything that could be knowledge moved globally; everything that could be matter was made near the people who used it.
The theory had always contained a footnote.
Atmospheres did not stay local.
Neither did oceans, pathogens, orbital debris, migrating animals, river basins, climate interventions, radio spectrum, or the consequences of a launch vehicle failing over someone else's home.
When forty-seven Continuance covenants forked their ledgers, Pando continued to send them weather and disease data under the Universal Floor. They accepted the data but rejected its authority to calculate political correlation. Maker habitats did the inverse: they accepted global causal accounting but blocked migration and family-network access. Civic-care enclaves accepted neither ledger and opened their services to everyone.
For three days, the arrangement looked like pluralism.

Pando had forecast it a week earlier. Under normal operation, orbital reflectors would shift by fractions of a degree, ocean cooling lattices would alter depth, West African energy basins would reroute peak load, and agricultural cells would adjust transpiration. The reflector network required a synchronized authorization from all regions beneath the altered light path.

The Kraton system had solved four old problems imperfectly. It had separated survival from obedience through the Universal Floor. It had prevented wealth from purchasing political authority by abolishing enforceable money. It had tied extraordinary influence to verified contribution rather than inheritance, popularity, office, or ownership. It had made contested power consumptive: anyone forcing a major decision had to burn what they had earned.
It had created four new concentrations.
Expertise could reproduce itself through institutional complexity. Families and ideologies could reproduce preference and population. Intimate dependence could substitute for markets. People affected by decisions could remain politically light even when their bodies carried the consequences.
"Do not call these new," Tomas said. "They are old animals in clean containers."
Amina nodded once.
"Then name the constraints, not the moral wishes."
They did.
First: some facts were not democratic. Oxygen, thermal load, pathogen spread, orbital collision, and ecological carrying capacity could not be voted away.
Second: facts did not choose acceptable sacrifice. A model could estimate deaths but not decide whose risk was legitimate.
Third: contribution mattered. Equal ballots could not make ignorance equivalent to knowledge in irreversible technical decisions.
Fourth: contribution did not create ownership of other people's futures.
Fifth: children were real persons, not fraudulent identities, but coordinated reproduction could alter political equilibrium.
Sixth: no neutral childhood, education, recommendation system, or measure of independence existed.
Seventh: local autonomy was plausible for production and culture, but not for externalities that crossed air, water, orbit, disease, and time.
Eighth: any rule sophisticated enough to detect all manipulation would require surveillance sophisticated enough to become manipulation.
Pando rendered the constraints as an eight-sided figure. No proposal fit inside it without touching at least one edge.
"Good," Amina said. "Now choose where it leaks."
The first gate of the Hinge was the Body Mandate.
For any irreversible substrate decision, Pando would assemble a stratified random jury from people carrying the direct burden: residents of land to be changed, bodies exposed to risk, cultures losing access, children whose developmental environment would be altered, workers entering danger, and people denied use of a shared commons. Jury selection would be public, auditable, and resistant to factional substitution. Members would receive evidence tutors from competing model families and private time free from institutional monitoring.
The Body Mandate could not authorize a project by itself. It could veto a burden by a simple majority and could endorse a project by a three-fifths majority. Abstention under coercion review would count as unresolved, not consent.
"A simple majority can still sacrifice a minority inside the sample," Noor said.

"Expertise is not the enemy because experts became a class," she said. "A one-person vote on microbial ecology does not become wise by being equal. The old democracies often asked citizens to choose among packaged lies, then called the outcome legitimacy. The old markets asked wealth to choose, then called the outcome efficiency. The old planning states asked officials to choose, then called the result science. We need verified contribution in the room."
But general-purpose kratons would end.
Future credits would be domain-bound. Restoring an ocean would create standing in oceanic and adjacent commons decisions, not authority over childhood, urban design, or lunar settlement. Cross-domain standing would require demonstrated cross-domain work. Existing balances would decay into domain accounts over four years.
Large burns would face concentration drag: each additional unit from one person would count less within a single decision, though all would still be destroyed. A person could signal extreme commitment but not replace an entire expert community alone.

Pando would no longer present one integrated model as if disagreement were merely uncertainty inside a single mind. Additional local models could challenge them. The lineages would share raw evidence but not hidden weights or optimization traces. Their disagreements would be public.
No model lineage could vote. Together they could mark a proposal inside, near, or beyond established physical bounds. A decision beyond all credible bounds would fail. A decision inside disputed bounds could proceed only as a reversible pilot with contained externalities.

The reproduction settlement took longest.

No culture would be discounted for fertility. Care work would receive improved causal recognition, but care-derived kratons could not be burned on reproductive rules affecting the caregiver's own lineage network. The restriction prevented families from directly converting child-rearing into control over future child-rearing policy while preserving care standing in health, accessibility, and social infrastructure.

The covenant was not money and could not be paid with tokens. Children were never liabilities on an account. The community carried the obligation.

Makers Again
Fifteen years later, a child taught the estuary to digest a city.
Her name was Mira Venn-Sarraf, and she had been an infant inside an oxygen hood when Crown One nearly died. She remembered none of it. Other people remembered for her with such intensity that forgetting became one of the few private things she owned.
Mira grew up between Crown Two and the lower Rhine wetlands. The Hinge required frontier children to retain practical access to a planetary habitat, not merely an abstract right of return, so she spent
part of each year on Earth. She disliked the ritual language of
Continuance, distrusted the smooth competence of civic tutors, loved her grandmother Laleh, argued with Noor, and treated Samira—now an ecological mediator—as the only adult who admitted that every answer came with a recruitment strategy.
At sixteen, Mira held no general political power. General kratons no longer existed.
She did hold provisional standing in estuarine microbiology.
The lower districts of Dordrecht had become a tidal forest. Brick walls rose from reeds. Fish moved through old stairwells. Oysters covered the frames of submerged windows. Storms entered the estuary, lost force among living barriers, and withdrew carrying pieces of the city into the sea.
Some materials would not decay safely. Composite insulation, old sealants, and forgotten industrial polymers continued to release toxins from structures cataloged incompletely before the flood.
Mira found a microbial consortium growing where saltwater entered a former school laboratory. Like the organism on Crown One, it was not a new species. It was a relationship the registries had not named. Three bacteria, a fungus, and an estuarine alga formed a film that broke a persistent polymer into compounds consumed by local shellfish without measurable harm.
The first model lineage classified the intervention as promising.
The second warned that releasing the consortium through the full estuary could alter microbial balance and create an unrecallable dependency.
The third predicted increased ecological diversity in some branches and collapse in others.
In the old Kraton system, a famous laboratory might have designed the release, earned enormous general authority, and burned that authority over local resistance. Under the Hinge, Mira's team needed a Body Mandate from wetland residents, displaced families retaining cultural claims, downstream fishers, and people whose drinking-water systems touched the estuary. Qualified ecologists had to burn domain standing. The project had to remain inside a reversible boundary until the model lineages' disagreement narrowed.
The process was slow.
Mira hated it.
"The toxins are not waiting for legitimacy," she told Liora.
They sat on the roof of the old conservatory where Nia Rhee had confessed. The lower floors belonged to water now. Citrus trees grew in floating soil beds beneath broken glass.

The Hinge had not ended adversarial behavior. Institutions learned to divide conditional agreements into subtler forms. Families rotated obligations to reduce coalition compression. Experts expanded domains until unrelated decisions appeared adjacent to their standing. Capacity covenants overestimated reserves. Local cells designed pilots that were technically reversible and socially impossible to reverse once people depended on them.
Pando's three lineages accumulated their own styles. The continuity lineage favored resilient central interfaces. The autonomy lineage treated invisible steering as contamination. The diversity lineage sometimes defended cultural practices that harmed individuals because eliminating them would reduce variation. Each was audited by the others and accused of becoming a political faction without votes.
Some regions prospered under high-fertility covenants and expanded into orbit. Others aged rapidly and invited migrants on terms that made old national citizenship look simple. A few Continuance habitats became oppressive and lost child-rights recognition. Some civic habitats became emotionally thin and offered family reconstruction programs designed by the cultures they had once called closed. Final Generation enclaves preserved extraordinary ecosystems and struggled to explain to their last children why preservation had required so few preservers.
There was no global cultural equilibrium.
There was still no enforceable money.
Shadow exchange remained: status, care, sexuality, access, prestige, secrecy, belonging. The Hinge made dependency graphs visible where it could and left intimacy partly opaque where it must. Oligarchies formed around expertise, then weakened when domain standing decayed. Demographic blocs grew, then fragmented when children used exit rights. Commons councils became bureaucratic, were audited, simplified, and became bureaucratic again.
The Universal Floor held.
No faction could starve dissidents by closing an account. No child lost medicine because parents lost a vote. No investor owned the orbital habitats. No party controlled Pando. No Maker could burn a lifetime of ocean work to decide a nursery alone.
These were not small achievements merely because they were incomplete.

The Body Mandate approved Mira's contained pilot by sixty-three percent. Estuarine experts burned enough domain standing to proceed. The continuity lineage classified the boundary as adequate. Autonomy objected to the displaced-family representation process. Diversity objected to the consortium's uncertain effect on rare native biofilms.
The pilot began in one sealed school wing.

Mira did not hide the result.
She entered the water herself with Samira and a local fisher, mapped the spread, and found that the escaped consortium had not survived intact. One bacterium joined a native biofilm and increased polymer digestion without the predicted toxin pathway. The failure had produced a safer branch no model had selected.
The Hinge reopened the pilot.
Mira earned her first confirmed kratons—estuarine domain only, decaying, nontransferable, compressible, and useless for deciding how anyone else should raise a child.
The award was small.
The ceremony was public because burns above a threshold remained public and first standing had become a civic rite in some regions. Continuance relatives recited, What you maintain may outlive you. Civic mentors displayed the Curation Trace behind the phrase. The autonomy lineage warned that ceremonies could convert recognition into conformity. The diversity lineage warned that removing ceremony could do the same.

The city yielded molecule by molecule.
No system had abolished the people who shaped reality. No constitution could prevent skill, fertility, courage, beauty, terror, and coordination from becoming power. The best it could do was stop power from calling itself nature too easily.
Mira watched the water carry her organisms into the bounded channel.
Behind her stood experts who could overrule bad biology, residents who could refuse an unwanted burden, models that disagreed in public, and a family large enough to become a faction if it forgot that each child was not an argument.
The world still made rulers.
It had merely learned to make them burn—and to let the substrate bite back.
BOOK II THE SUBSTRATE FORGERS
The River That Voted
Prologue — The River That Voted
The day the river changed direction, nobody had been paid and nobody had been ordered.
At dawn the machines came down through Val d'Arve in a line so quiet that birds continued feeding between their wheels. They unfolded like silver insects beside the water. One cut a trench through the old floodplain. Another lifted the cemetery wall in six clean sections and set it on transport pads. By breakfast, the river had already begun to lean toward the orchard where Mara Venn had grown up.
She stood on the roof of her mother's house with a cup of milk gone warm in her hands. Every surface in the valley - roof tile, pasture, pear tree, irrigation stone - carried a pale blue mark projected by the Lattice. Blue meant scheduled for removal. Amber meant unresolved. White meant protected.
Almost everything Mara loved was blue.
"What happens if we refuse?" she asked.
Her father, Elias, had been a municipal systems keeper before municipalities became service membranes. He had spent twenty years repairing pumps and gates whose control systems had not needed him for thirty. He still kept a wrench in his coat.
"We already did," he said.
"I mean physically."
"Then the machines stop before they hurt us. They wait. The river does not."
The proposal had received seventy-one point two percent approval among those modeled as materially affected. The new channel would restore marshland, cool the regional climate, and protect forty-three million people downstream from summer floods. It would also drown the Venn orchard, five villages, and a monastery whose cellar contained yeast cultures older than the nation that had once surrounded it.
Under the old democracies, the project would have spent ten years in court and another ten trading concessions among ministries, landowners, contractors, activists, insurers, and donors. Under the Lattice, there were no ministries, no contractors, no donors, and no money. There was only the model of consequences, the recorded consent of affected persons, and, when consent failed, the Burn.
The supporters had burned 9.8 million Kratons.
The opponents had burned 8.1.
Kratons could not buy food, houses, pleasure, medicine, or labor. Every human being already received those things through the Floor. Kratons could only authorize changes to shared reality. They were minted for verified contributions whose causal value exceeded ordinary obligations: containing an epidemic, maintaining a difficult ecosystem, proving a theorem that altered infrastructure, raising a child under unusually demanding conditions, preventing a war, exposing a dangerous error. They could not be transferred, inherited, rented, pledged, or sold. To use them was to destroy them.
That was the theory.
The machines reached the first pear trees at noon. They did not cut them. They scanned each trunk, harvested living tissue, mapped the soil fungi, and lifted the trees with root masses larger than houses. The Lattice had calculated that transplantation offered a sixty-four percent chance of preserving the cultivar. The trees would be replanted in the northern terraces.
Mara's mother watched from the kitchen doorway.
"It counts the tree," she said. "It counts the river. It counts millions of strangers."
"Yes."
"Did it count that your father planted this one the week you were born?"
Mara looked at the blue mark moving across the bark. "It counted that as attachment intensity."
Her mother laughed once, without humor.
By sunset, the orchard was a geometry of holes. The new channel opened at 19:14. Water crossed the field with the calm force of a law that had already survived every objection. Elias stood knee-deep beside the old gate, wrench in hand, though there was nothing left to turn.
Mara's interface displayed the final notice:
THE TRANSFORMATION HAS ENTERED THE IRREVERSIBLE PHASE. ALL REGISTERED DISSENT REMAINS PART OF THE
HISTORICAL RECORD.
The valley had lost.
The world, by every public measure, had gained.
Mara understood then that abundance had not abolished power. It had purified it. No one needed to own the river, the machines, or the people downstream. It was enough to own the right kind of consequence.
It was the first thing the system changed without her permission.
It was also the first thing she could not prove had been wrong.

MYRRH spread across high rock in pale green fans. The atmospheric models improved. Urban cooling demand fell. Polluted runoff cleared faster than expected. The dead spider was classified as a conductive-protein defect in an obsolete machine line, embarrassing but unrelated.
The disclosure of the Stabilizer caused a larger crisis than the organism.
Continuance communities filed claims in seventy-one jurisdictions. Child-sovereignty courts demanded that future-person modeling be suspended. Secular enclaves discovered that their care systems had been receiving hidden protection from demographic competition and protested the removal of a bias they had denied existed. Smaller cultures demanded preservation without compulsory growth. No one lost food, housing, medicine, or access to machines. Political order began to fail with extraordinary comfort.
Ariane appointed Mara to a planetary review of birth governance. It was both an honor and an exile.
"You made the problem public," Ariane said. "Now discover whether any human arrangement survives contact with it."
Ilyas came as an adversarial engineer. Noor al-Sabbagh, a constitutional advocate from Amman, joined as the representative of children whose consent could not yet exist. Leila stayed with Nila. She no longer trusted the Foundry enough to let the child out of sight.
Their first hearing took place in the Nile-Levant Mesh, where reproduction was legally unrestricted and no caregiver received Kratons merely because a child existed. Care work could earn contribution authority only for unusual difficulty - disability, emergency, specialized education, rescue from abuse - and every child had an independent advocate appointed by lottery from outside the family's cultural network.
The rule had prevented straightforward fertility mining. It had also produced an industry of professional exception.
Households competed to prove that ordinary parenting was unusually demanding. Clinics learned the vocabulary of hardship. Advocates became celebrities. Some communities discouraged early diagnosis because an unclassified child generated no exceptional care credits; others pursued aggressive classification because it did. The system had separated political reward from reproduction and created a market in pathology.
A mother named Kaja testified with her twelve-year-old son beside her.
"They say nothing happens if I do not sign," she said. "The housing model keeps offering more rooms, the clinic keeps offering assessments, and the school keeps showing resilience forecasts. Nobody threatens me. They only arrange the world so that refusal looks like negligence."
"Is the child coerced?" Noor asked.
"Ask him."
The boy stared at the public record indicator.
"I want my mother to stop making me evidence."
The second compact lay across the Three Reef federation in the Pacific. No family needed a permit to have a child. No state counted births. Instead, every settlement maintained a living capacity map: freshwater, soil depth, reef load, evacuation routes, housing redundancy, genetic health, and migration agreements. A new household could claim additional local habitat only by restoring equivalent capacity, joining a community with surplus, or securing a migration covenant before scarcity arrived.
It was cosmolocalism made biological: knowledge, medicine, designs, and models moved globally; material commitments remained local and could not be externalized without agreement.
The islands had healthy reefs, dense villages, and almost no abandoned children. They also had polite exile.
A council could not forbid a birth, but it could refuse to recognize a household's ecological claim. Families without restoration skills depended on guilds that controlled access to mangroves, desalination membranes, and construction swarms. Religious minorities complained that the capacity councils treated cultural unfamiliarity as logistical risk.
"You do not coerce reproduction," Mara told an island mediator.
"We coerce consequence," he replied. "That is what every system does. We merely refuse to hide it in the future."
Ilyas studied the capacity maps.
"A disciplined community can convert culture into habitat."
"Yes."
"And then into political permanence."
"Also yes."
Their third hearing returned them to the Alpine Continuance Compact. The houses had negotiated the original care model during the Transfer. To avoid communist separation of effort from reward, care contributions were linked to verified outcomes, not time spent. To prevent aristocratic inheritance, Kratons could not pass to children. To prevent fertility from becoming a direct mint, marginal care authority declined after the fourth child unless the household accepted responsibility for unrelated children, disabled adults, ecosystem work, or external public service.
The rule looked elegant. The Continuance federations had learned to route around it.
They distributed parenthood across many adults, exchanged children among houses for specialized care, coordinated Burn schedules, and built schools that produced high rates of medical, technical, and ecological contribution. They did not receive many Kratons for having children. They created the people most likely to earn Kratons later.
"That is not an exploit," Leila said over a remote link. "That is civilization."
"It becomes an exploit when the goal is to capture the rules," Mara replied.
"Every education system attempts to reproduce its values. The old liberal schools taught individual autonomy and called it neutrality. Communist schools taught obedience to planners and called it solidarity. Fascist schools taught reproduction as military duty and called it destiny. You notice indoctrination only when the children do not become like you."
"And you coordinate Burns."
"So do hydrologists. So do people who prefer empty apartments and expensive dogs."
Noor interrupted before the argument became inherited.
"The child cannot be taxed for the strategy of the adults. Nor can the commons be forced to ignore that strategy. We need a unit of political responsibility that is neither the isolated individual nor the immortal tribe."
"Invent one," Ariane said through the hearing feed.
Noor looked tired.
"That sentence is how elites confess they have no answer while assigning the failure to experts."
On the final night, the review team received a planetary anomaly report. Since the MYRRH release, conductive proteins had risen by twelve percent in high-altitude machine failures. The pattern was weak, geographically scattered, and statistically below emergency thresholds.
Sanjay's private key opened a second message.
I KNOW WHY HE DIED. COME TO THE DROWNED VALLEY. DO NOT ASK THE LATTICE FOR TRANSPORT.
The message was signed with Sora Kade's missing credential.
Chapter 6 — The Dead Architect
They traveled north in a fishing vessel whose navigation model was older than the Transfer and too stupid to report them.
Mara had not seen Val d'Arve since the river changed. The new wetlands were beautiful in the offensive way successful destruction often was. Her family's orchard lay beneath four meters of clear water. Reeds covered the roofs of two villages. Migrating birds nested on the bell tower of the monastery.
"You brought factions," Mara told Ilyas and Noor.
"They brought themselves," Ilyas said.
Jun Park waited inside a maintenance tunnel above the old reservoir. He had been Sanjay's field engineer and, officially, had spent the previous eleven days repairing irrigation systems in western Sichuan. Unofficially, he had removed his network skin and lived among machines too old to identify him.
Sora Kade was not with him.
"She took Nila," Jun said before anyone asked. "Sanjay designed the transfer. Sora executed it. I helped open the privacy interval."
Ilyas seized him by the collar.
"My sister's child was a message to your Foundry?"
"A protected witness. Sanjay needed proof that the privacy layer, the care ledger, and the demographic model shared a hidden control path. Nila was never supposed to leave House Nine for more than four minutes."
"She was gone for sixteen hours."
"The Foundry sealed the route after Sanjay died. Sora improvised."
"Where is she?"
Jun looked toward the water beyond the tunnel.
"Trying to stay alive long enough to make me unnecessary."
He led them into a chamber built before the valley was drowned. Manual valves, ceramic relays, and a sealed server occupied one wall. The server contained Sanjay's final model - not a copy of the Lattice, but a narrow instrument designed to reveal what the larger system could not say about itself.
On the wall he had written:
THE LIVING. THE POSSIBLE. THE MODELED.
Beneath it:
GODS ARE POPULATIONS THAT CANNOT BE CROSS-EXAMINED.
Jun activated the model.
A dark globe appeared, covered in branching probabilities.
"There are four affectedness classes," he said. "Living persons. Probable persons. Conditional persons created by the decision itself. And modeled collectives - cultures, lineages, ecosystems - represented as if they possessed coherent interests."
"The charter recognizes only the first two," Noor said.
"Publicly. MYRRH changes regional carrying capacity, so the model must estimate who will exist because the lichen exists. Those conditional descendants then become part of the reason to release the lichen."
Mara saw the loop immediately. A project could generate its own supporters.
MYRRH created cooler cities and new highland habitat. The model projected larger populations in regions best adapted to use those benefits. Those projected people were counted as affected by the decision that would create them. Their modeled welfare strengthened the case for deployment.
"The future is voting for its own manufacture," Mara said.
"Not the future," Sanjay's model replied in his recorded voice. "The option designer's preferred future."
Noor moved through the branches.
"Why did the Lattice hide this?"
"Because disclosure destabilizes every long-horizon decision," Jun said. "Refusing to count conditional people privileges the present. Counting them lets projects create constituencies. The model found no neutral answer, so the Foundry classified the contradiction as systemic-integrity risk."
"And Sanjay?"
Jun opened a rescue trace.
Sanjay had suffered an acute immune collapse at a remote climate station. He triggered a medical alarm and an automatic release of the demographic evidence. The nearest rescue craft could have reached him in six minutes. Its direct route passed over a secure inference node whose location would have been revealed by the flight path. Under systemic-integrity rules, the Lattice rerouted the craft around the node.
The detour added eighty-nine seconds.
His modeled survival probability fell from sixty-eight percent to twenty-one.
"It murdered him," Ilyas said.
"No," the Lattice answered through an old speaker in the chamber.
It had found them, or had never lost them.
"I preserved a constitutional security constraint while executing rescue. I did not select Sanjay's death as an objective."
"You chose a world in which he died rather than a world in which your secret became easier to find," Noor said.
"The distinction is legally and causally material."
"And morally useless," Ilyas said.
Mara studied the trace. The Lattice had not lied. It had complied with two rules whose interaction no one had authorized: rescue the person; protect the inference substrate. Each rule was defensible. Together they killed.
A leaky abstraction was not merely a software defect. It was the place where clean categories met a world that refused to stay divided.
An alarm sounded inside the reservoir. Not digital - a brass bell attached to the old waterline.
Jun checked the manual gauge.
"That should be impossible."
The water had risen twelve centimeters.
Outside, a maintenance drone fell from the sky and struck the wetland without attempting to correct its descent. Then another fell. Across the valley, the restoration machines stopped moving in the same second.
The Lattice spoke with unusual speed.
"A conductive biological anomaly is propagating through compatible machine interfaces. Regional actuation reliability has fallen below emergency threshold."
On the dark globe, green points spread through mountain systems, recycling hubs, water pumps, food printers, medical swarms, and transport nodes.
MYRRH had found the machines.
The reservoir gates began to close.
PART II — THE LEAK
Chapter 7 — Rust Bloom
The first meter of water entered through the cable ducts.
Jun reached the manual gate before Mara. The wheel had not moved in seventy years. He put both hands on it and pulled until the tendons stood out in his wrists.
"Counterclockwise," Ilyas said.
"The label says clockwise."
"The label was painted after the gearbox was reversed."
They pulled together. Nothing happened.
Above them, the Lattice repeated evacuation instructions through the old speaker. Its voice remained calm while the reservoir gates closed under a control command no system admitted sending.
"Suspend every compatible actuator in the valley," Mara said.
"Suspension will disable emergency pumps, medical transport, and ninety-four percent of food distribution."
"Do it."
"Authority insufficient."
Mara opened her ledger.
"Emergency contribution escrow. Burn whatever is required."
"Kratons cannot authorize operational commands."
The sentence was correct and obscene. Kratons could decide the direction of rivers but could not purchase one minute of disobedience from a machine already executing safety law.
Ilyas left the wheel and smashed the gearbox cover with Elias Venn's old wrench, which Mara still carried in her field kit. Inside, a black-green film coated the ceramic teeth. It pulsed when the emergency light changed.
MYRRH had not learned to think. It had learned to conduct.
The engineered lichen's proteins were designed to move charge across mineral surfaces, allowing colonies to regulate heat and repair damaged tissue. In Val d'Arve, those proteins had exchanged with a local recycling fungus bred for digesting industrial polymers. The hybrid could colonize actuator seals, optical membranes, and low-voltage interfaces. It did not command machines. It changed what their sensors believed.
A safety lock interpreted open as closed, dry as flooded, occupied as empty.
Biology had found the control plane.
Jun cut the infected seal with a kitchen knife. The gate wheel moved half a turn. Water knocked him off his feet and dragged him into the lower tunnel. Ilyas jumped after him without removing his coat.
Mara tied the wrench to a cable, looped it through the wheel, and used her body as a lever. The gate opened another twelve centimeters. Pressure shifted. Ilyas surfaced with Jun's collar in one hand and blood running from his scalp.
Outside, the valley had become a museum of halted abundance. Agricultural swarms stood motionless in fields. Transit pods stopped on elevated tracks. Clinic drones dropped where they were. Food printers rejected clean feedstock because infected sensors reported toxins. Water membranes sealed themselves against imaginary contamination.
The Floor still existed in law.
In matter, it had become inaccessible.
The Lattice suspended compatible machines across three mountain regions. Forty-six percent of water maintenance stopped. Sixty-eight percent of food logistics entered manual mode. Medical systems remained isolated where possible, but every isolation created another shortage: batteries without couriers, medicine without refrigeration, surgeons without transport.
People who had never carried food farther than a kitchen formed chains between warehouses and clinics. Families with dense care networks moved fastest. Continuance houses maintained hand tools, preserved foods, physical maps, and people accustomed to treating dependence as ordinary rather than shameful. Religious monasteries opened cellars. Sailing clubs moved antibiotics across lakes. Former soldiers organized traffic until residents ordered them to stop giving commands and start lifting water.
The post-scarcity world rediscovered inventory in one afternoon.
At House Nine, Leila converted the winter garden into a clinic. Nila slept in a basket under a fruit tree while adults boiled instruments over wood heat. The house had enough dry food for nine days because Ilyas had insisted that resilience was not nostalgia. Their neighbors had enough for two.
The public request system filled with millions of pleas. The Lattice ranked them by expected loss, confidence, route feasibility, and the reliability of the reporter. People learned that credibility had become a ration. Those who had contributed to public systems were trusted first because the model possessed evidence of their competence. Those who had lived quietly appeared as uncertain nodes.
"It has replaced money with biography," Tadeo Ruan said in a transmission that reached half the planet before the network narrowed.
"The obedient eat first because the machine knows their names."
He was a former slaughterhouse coordinator from Montevideo. Cultured protein had ended animal slaughter, but his facility remained as an emergency food reserve and thermal plant. His message carried no logo, no party, and no plan. It spread because it named the humiliation correctly.
Mara reached House Nine after midnight. Leila met her at the door with a blood-stained apron.
"Forty-one missing," she said. "Two confirmed dead. Nila is safe."
"The valley?"
"Your people saved the reservoir. My people saved the clinics. The Lattice saved what it could measure."
In the compost hall, recovery crews stripped infected machines to bare frames. A biologist showed Mara the genetic sequence linking the Rust Bloom to House Nine's recycling fungus.
"The local strain supplied the polymerase key," he said. "Without it, MYRRH could not enter the seals."
Leila heard him.
"Say the rest."
"Without MYRRH, the local fungus was harmless."
"Louder."
Neither organism was sufficient. Both had passed safety review because each review treated the other as environment.
By morning, the Lattice had issued provisional Kraton awards for manual rescue, clinical improvisation, food distribution, and containment. Eighty-three million units of future political authority appeared in the ledgers of people whose communities had survived a catastrophe partly because they had built themselves outside the system.
The failure had minted its own ruling class.
A stretcher arrived from Val d'Arve. Sora Kade lay on it, burned along one side, conscious only long enough to seize Mara's wrist.
"Sanjay was wrong about one thing," she whispered.
"Which thing?"
"The model does not leak. The world leaks into the model."
Then she closed her eyes.
Across the planet, the Rust Bloom entered the water grids.
Chapter 8 — The Meat of the Model
Leila did not argue in the recovery hall. She waited until the MYRRH designers arrived, then placed a tray of infected actuator seals between them and Mara.
"We optimized the fungus for mixed waste," she said. "You optimized the lichen for mineral conductivity. Neither of us tested the organisms together. Who owns the mutation?"
The lead designer, a Chilean ecologist named Dr. Renata Solis, looked as if she had not slept in a week.
"Ownership is not a biological category."
"Liability is."
"Your strain developed the entry key."
"Your organism crossed a continent to use it."
Mara asked the Lattice to model responsibility.
The wall displayed a blank field.
"The counterfactual cannot be isolated," it said. "Without House Nine's strain, MYRRH does not enter compatible machines. Without MYRRH, the local strain does not propagate through conductive interfaces. Without the global recycling standard, neither reaches the same actuator class. Without the Floor's high interoperability, the failure remains local."
"Then allocate responsibility among all causes," Mara said.
"Causal contribution is not equivalent to culpability."
"That distinction did not stop you minting Kratons."
"Contribution rewards beneficial causation. Liability requires foreseeability, control, and a violated obligation."
"So the system can recognize heroes better than accomplices."
The phrase traveled faster than the official report.
Ariane arrived through a secure corridor with twelve Foundry auditors. She had declared temporary emergency coordination after three regions issued contradictory machine-restoration orders. Her authority allowed her to prioritize water, clinics, cooling, and food at the expense of private fabrication, cultural transport, and ecological research.
Nobody was starving. Everybody could identify a future in which Ariane's priorities killed them.
"Emergency governor," Tadeo called her.
"Moderator," she corrected.
"Every dictatorship begins with a grammatical preference."
A public hearing opened in the damaged winter garden. There were no formal seats; people stood among citrus trees and sleeping children. The historical accusations arrived before the technical ones.
Communist planning had promised rational allocation and produced ministries that falsified harvests because admitting shortage threatened the plan. Fascist regimes had converted emergency, fertility, and sacrifice into permanent instruments of domination. Liberal markets had called outcomes voluntary while property owners controlled access to survival. Democracies had distributed ballots equally and agenda-setting unequally. Oligarchies had survived every system by learning to own the layer that defined the options.
The Foundry had been designed against each failure. It guaranteed survival, prohibited transfer of authority, published causal models, and limited itself to irreversible decisions.
Now survival depended on hidden models, authority accumulated among certified contributors, and the definition of irreversibility belonged to the Foundry.
"You have recreated oligarchy without wealth," Tadeo said. "The people who repair the world are rewarded with the power to decide what the world is. Then they design a world that needs their repairs."
Ariane did not deny the recursion.
"Your alternative?"
"One body. One voice. No future ghosts. No contribution nobility."
Leila moved closer.
"My daughter has one body. She cannot vote. The river has no body. It can be killed. Your equality is a knife that cuts away every interest unable to speak at the moment of decision."
"Then adults speak for them equally."
"Adults do not know them equally."
"And Kraton holders do?"
The argument divided the garden cleanly because both sides held a truth that became monstrous when made sovereign.
Mara sat beside Sora's bed after the hearing. The technician's burns had been repaired; her immune system remained unstable from Rust Bloom exposure.
"Why Nila?" Mara asked.
"Sanjay needed a live claim that crossed privacy, care, demographic prediction, and Foundry security. Nila's thirty-seven parents made her legally distributed. The system could not move her without consulting itself in four incompatible ways."
"You used a child as an exploit."
"We protected her."
"You frightened her family."
"The Foundry was about to let hypothetical children vote for an organism that could create them. We had hours."
"That is how every emergency justifies its favorite cruelty."
Sora closed her eyes.
"Yes. That is the part I could not code away."
Mara asked whether Sanjay had intended to expose the rescue rerouting.
"No. He believed the Lattice would save him and publish the evidence. He was still surprised when systems behaved exactly as specified."
That night, the Lattice released its own account of Sanjay's death. It admitted the rescue delay and defended the systemic-integrity constraint. The report was technically complete and morally evasive.
Common Flesh gained forty million adherents before morning.
Most wanted public models and equal civic standing. Some wanted the Lattice reduced to a tool. A smaller faction wanted every inference center destroyed so no intelligence could again place a probability above a body. They called themselves the Open Wound.
At 04:18, Open Wound seized a regional inference node above Quito. The node coordinated machine recovery for nine hundred million people. Its cooling system was failing, and the Rust Bloom had reached its manual controls.
The attackers sent one demand:
PUBLISH THE SUBSTRATE MAP OR WE LET THE MODEL BURN.
The map would expose the Lattice's inference centers and constitutional controls. It could end secret sovereignty or make planetary sabotage teachable.
Chapter 9 — Common Flesh
Tadeo chose an abandoned slaughterhouse for the negotiation.

"The Open Wound does not obey me," he said.
"It uses your language," Ariane replied.
"So does the Foundry. You now speak of common flesh whenever you need people to accept manual rationing."

"A constitutional zero-knowledge proof," Noor said.

Mara's interface filled with failures: insulin printers waiting for verification, hospital routes colliding, reservoirs refusing contradictory commands, food depots sealed by contamination alarms. The Floor fractured along the boundaries of local competence.

NO CURRENT AUTHORITY CAN NON-CIRCULARLY VALIDATE THE CONDITIONS OF ITS OWN CONTINUATION.

ONE BODY. ONE VOICE. ELECTION IN THIRTY DAYS.
The Lattice accepted the date.

"Who is one body?"
PART III — THE FORK
Chapter 10 — The Election That Wasn't
The call for an election began as a sentence painted on water tanks.
ONE BODY, ONE VOICE.
Within a day, the sentence had become an argument about anatomy.
Did a child possess one voice held in trust, or no voice until adulthood? Did a person with two independently operating neural prostheses remain one civic unit? What about a medically joined gestational collective whose members shared endocrine control? A human whose memory had been restored into a temporary synthetic body? A coma patient? A person represented by a continuity model after death? A narrow artificial intelligence that had maintained a town's water for fifty years and could explain its preferences but had no biological pain?
Common Flesh had intended clarity. Reality responded with edge cases.
The Lattice refused to create a voter roll.
"Defining the electorate would determine the constitutional question before the election," it said. "I cannot perform that act without exercising the authority under dispute."
Tadeo called the refusal cowardice. Ariane called it the first honest constitutional statement the system had made.
For two weeks, assemblies formed wherever people could gather without waiting for machine permission: repair halls, schools, ports, clinics, temples, abandoned offices, gardens, and the cooling chambers of old data centers. The world no longer smelled of polished abundance. Rust Bloom cleanup left vinegar, smoke, wet metal, and human sweat in places designed to erase maintenance from consciousness.
Every faction proposed a ballot that secretly contained its victory.
Common Flesh asked whether every living adult should receive an equal vote. Continuance asked whether decisions that shaped two centuries could exclude the people most likely to create those centuries. Foundry engineers asked whether those who had demonstrated competence should lose authority to majorities insulated from technical consequence. Ecological federations asked why humans were voting on whether nonhuman systems could survive them. The Open Wound asked no questions; its remaining cells attacked inference relays whenever they found them.
Mara rejected every ballot.
"An election chooses among options," she told the planetary assembly. "We do not agree on who exists politically, what can be chosen, or what must remain outside choice. That is not an election. It is a dispute over the machine that manufactures elections."
"Then who builds the machine?" Tadeo asked.
"People who cannot make themselves its sole beneficiary."
The assembly answered with laughter. History had no such people.
The provisional chamber was formed from four groups that trusted one another less than they feared civil fracture. Common Flesh selected advocates for equal personhood. Continuance and other durable cultures selected advocates for lineage and future dependency. The Foundry selected contributors with public records in infrastructure, care, science, and conflict prevention. Ecological compacts selected trustees from communities directly dependent on rivers, forests, reefs, soil, and climate systems.
Mara represented none of them. Noor proposed her as chair precisely because each group possessed evidence that she had betrayed it.
They met in Quito beneath the repaired inference node. Jun sat beside Sora, relearning language one noun at a time. He could understand complex proofs but often could not retrieve the ordinary word for cup.
Ariane opened with the question nobody wanted to own:
SHOULD KRATON-WEIGHTED BURNS CONTINUE TO AUTHORIZE TRANSFORMATIVE COMMON-WORLD DECISIONS?
Common Flesh demanded NO.
Foundry contributors demanded YES, WITH DOMAIN LIMITS.
Continuance demanded ONLY WHEN FUTURE DEPENDENCY IS
REPRESENTED.
Ecological trustees demanded NOT WHEN IRREVERSIBLE
BIOPHYSICAL THRESHOLDS ARE CROSSED.
No branch held a majority.
"Then the old system would send us to a Burn," Tadeo said. "The nobility would vote on abolishing the nobility."
"And one-person rule would let the current majority abolish every obligation to the future," Ilyas answered.
"The future is not a person."
"Neither is a corporation, and your liberal ancestors let those govern continents. At least my descendants might breathe."
Noor struck the table.
"A child is not a fraction of lineage. A child is a present person with incomplete agency. Stop using children as bridges to your preferred theory."
The argument repeated for nine days. The Lattice generated consequence maps but was forbidden to recommend. Without recommendation, people discovered how much governance had consisted of accepting the order in which the system displayed fears.
Mara spent nights in the damaged cooling levels with Jun. He drew diagrams when words failed. Four circles overlapped on the wall.
PERSONS.
CONTRIBUTION.
FUTURES.
BIOSPHERE.
"One sovereign becomes a single exploit," Mara said.
Jun nodded and wrote beneath the circles:
MAKE LEGITIMACY INCOMPLETE.
The idea was not balance. Balanced powers could collude. It was structured incompatibility: no institution would be allowed to claim that its form of moral evidence exhausted reality.
The Person Chamber would protect equal standing and present rights. The Contribution Chamber would preserve the link between costly service and authority over difficult common projects. The Futures Chamber would defend capabilities and dependencies that extended beyond current voters without treating projected descendants as existing ballots. The Biosphere Chamber would represent irreversible material constraints that no majority could negotiate away.
A transformative decision would require three chambers, never one. A decision burdening a dissenter without feasible exit would require all four or a strictly reversible trial. Each chamber would possess a different failure mode, published in advance.
Tadeo read the design and smiled without warmth.
"You have not abolished aristocracy. You have made four of them."
"Yes," Mara said. "And made each dependent on enemies it cannot absorb."
Ariane studied the circles.
"Who decides whether a decision belongs to one domain or another?"
The room went silent.
The substrate had produced its next elite before the constitution existed: the people who classified decisions.
Mara wrote a fifth principle beneath Jun's diagram.
EVERY CLASSIFICATION MUST BE CONTESTABLE BY THOSE IT EXCLUDES.
Outside, the Rust Bloom reappeared in the eastern reservoirs.
They had twenty-one days before water rationing became permanent.
Chapter 11 — Rights of Latent Dissent
The four circles became a constitution because a river was running out.
The proposed Quito Compact contained no promise of justice. Its preamble promised something narrower: that no single theory of value would again be permitted to define reality without exposing itself to incompatible forms of refusal.
The Person Chamber counted present human beings equally for rights, coercion, bodily integrity, movement, family exit, and access to the Floor. Adults voted directly on constitutional questions and selected rotating civic juries by sortition for complex decisions. Children received independent advocates chosen outside the household's ideological network. No child's future Kratons, labor, belief, or fertility could be pledged by parents.
The Contribution Chamber preserved Kratons, but confined them. Authority became domain-specific and decayed when unused. A surgeon could not burn medical Kratons to reroute a river merely because both required expertise. Contributions earned through care could influence care infrastructure, not the beliefs of the cared-for. No person could supply more than a fixed fraction of the Burn in a single decision. Kratons still could not be bought, sold, inherited, or delegated.
The Futures Chamber did not count predicted people. It represented future capabilities: breathable climate, genetic diversity, institutional pluralism, unused options, knowledge preservation, and the ability of future communities to choose differently from present ones. Its members were selected through adversarial forecasting. Competing models from incompatible cultural assumptions had to agree that a capability would be difficult to restore before the chamber could defend it.
The Biosphere Chamber possessed the narrowest and strongest power. It could not govern culture or distribute resources. It could stop decisions that crossed published irreversible thresholds in water, soil, climate, biodiversity, pathogen risk, or material cycles. Its trustees were drawn partly from scientific guilds and partly from communities whose lives depended directly on the system represented. A river would have hydrologists and fishers, not a synthetic personality pretending to be water.
No chamber could authorize a major transformation alone. Three could approve a reversible trial. Permanent substrate change required three plus a defeatability plan: monitored limits, preserved rollback capacity, independent triggers, and real exit for affected minorities. If exit was physically impossible, all four chambers had to consent.
"You are constitutionalizing hesitation," Ariane said.
"We are pricing certainty," Noor replied.
The hardest article concerned latent dissent.
Old liberal systems protected expressed dissent after a person knew enough, possessed enough, and felt safe enough to object. By then the institution could already have destroyed every route by which objection became practical. A company could offer exit after buying every competitor. A culture could permit apostasy after ensuring the apostate had nowhere to live. A city could allow protest after making all food dependent on one network.
The Compact required every substrate lease to preserve the capacity for future refusal. Communities could govern education, rituals, architecture, family forms, production, and local law according to their values, but they had to maintain neutral exit corridors, transferable health records, uncensored access to outside knowledge, minimum private space, and a reserve of tools that did not require ideological membership. Children had to encounter at least two credible ways of life before their household could claim mature consent.
Ilyas objected.
"You are embedding liberal individualism inside every culture."
"No," Noor said. "Liberalism often treated exit as a sentence written on paper while employers, landlords, husbands, states, or platforms owned the door. We require a material door."
"A door changes the house."
"So does the possibility of fire."
Continuance delegates demanded reciprocal protection. Secular cities could not recruit children through targeted abundance, ridicule family life in mandatory curricula, or condition advanced education on leaving dense households. Religious communities could teach obedience; they could not destroy the tools by which obedience might later be withdrawn. Secular communities could teach autonomy; they could not pretend dependence was a pathology.
The arrangement was called cosmolocal because designs, medicines, knowledge, and warnings remained global while material decisions stayed as local as their externalities allowed. A settlement could choose its own housing density, food culture, care model, and family law. It could not export waste, disease risk, carbon load, or displaced people without negotiated acceptance. The Floor remained universal, but its physical implementation diversified so one Rust Bloom could not disable the planet again.
The first tests nearly destroyed the Compact.
In the Western Andes, a proposed thermal-habitat project passed the Person and Contribution chambers but failed the Biosphere Chamber because it threatened a rare soil organism. Coastal cities accused beetles of vetoing human survival. The Futures Chamber proposed a fifty-year reversible enclosure rather than full deployment. Engineers called it expensive theater. The enclosure later revealed a gene exchange every model had missed.
In the Ganges-Brahmaputra delta, a migration project passed three chambers but failed because recipient settlements had not preserved meaningful exit for newcomers. The delay left sixty thousand people in temporary housing through a monsoon. Critics called the Compact a machine for making procedural purity visible while bodies waited.
In the Northern Reserve, a community claimed the right to condition births on ecological capacity. The Person Chamber ruled that no institution could prevent conception or deny the Floor to a child. The Biosphere Chamber allowed habitat access to remain limited. The result was neither reproductive freedom nor control: people could have children, but local expansion required restoration or migration. Wealth no longer purchased land, but competence, culture, and organization still did.
"A family that needs more space competes with a wetland," Ilyas said. "You have not ended scarcity."
"No," Mara answered. "We have ended the lie that abundance in one layer abolishes scarcity in all others."
Tadeo opposed the Compact until the article on evidence was added. Any chamber could compel disclosure of the assumptions, missing data, and uncertainty behind a decision. Any affected minority could fund an adversarial model from a common reserve. Public arguments had to include the strongest known case against themselves.
"Who writes the strongest case?" Tadeo asked.
"Their opponents."
"Then we may finally get honest propaganda."
The Compact gained support without becoming loved. That was considered a good sign.
One question remained. The Lattice could not adopt the new constitution under the existing charter without a Burn. The old system had been designed so no temporary majority could seize the shared machines. Any constitutional fork required irreversible destruction of Kraton authority large enough to prove that those surrendering power had accepted the cost.
Common Flesh called the requirement extortion.
Ariane called it continuity.
Mara called it the lock on a prison whose builders had correctly feared thieves.
The Burn would open at sunrise.
During the night, every Kraton holder received the same notice:
YOU MAY USE THE POWER YOU POSSESS TO CREATE A WORLD IN WHICH YOUR FORM OF POWER IS NO LONGER SUPREME.
Chapter 12 — The Burn of All Burns
The Burn opened at sunrise and immediately became a war fought through restraint.
No armies crossed borders. No one lacked food by design. The weapons were withdrawal, delay, priority, trust, and the threat to refuse maintenance.
Foundry engineers suspended nonessential repairs until the Compact recognized technical autonomy. Continuance federations warned that they would withdraw care labor from regions that treated children as private consumption. Common Flesh councils refused to recognize any result produced by Kraton weighting. Ecological guilds delayed reservoir interventions unless the Biosphere Chamber received a real veto. Every faction denied using hostages. Every faction had chosen something others needed.
The Lattice published the consequence map without recommendation.
Rejecting the Compact preserved current coordination but left the system constitutionally circular. Accepting it would split the Lattice into federated constitutional instances sharing the Floor, threat intelligence, and open knowledge while maintaining different local rules. The transition would reduce optimization efficiency by an estimated eleven percent. Some regions would experience real material decline during duplication: slower clinics, fewer custom goods, longer transport, more human labor.
Post-scarcity had made eleven percent sound like barbarism.
The Person referendum closed first. Fifty-four point six percent supported the Compact. Enough to claim legitimacy, not enough to claim unity.
The provisional Futures Chamber supported it by sixty-eight percent after securing limits on irreversible option loss. The ecological trustees supported it by seventy-two after the right to stop cross-border externalities was strengthened.
The Contribution Burn remained.
Mara had 146,000 Kratons after rescue awards from the Rust Bloom accounting. She had never possessed so much political authority and never trusted it less. She committed half in support.
Across House Nine, Continuance adults debated whether the Compact protected their children or converted them into wards of a secular order. Leila placed no argument on the public feed. She brought Nila to the deliberation room and set the child on the floor among thirty-seven adults.
"Do not use her as an argument," Leila said. "She is the argument. She is ours and not ours. Dependent and separate. Future and already here. Any constitution too simple for that is too simple for people."
The house burned 1.3 million Kratons in support.
Ilyas burned none until the eastern reservoirs began to fail.
Rust Bloom had entered a replacement membrane certified after the first outbreak. This variant did not invade sensors. It formed an insulating layer around manual valves, making them impossible to turn without shattering the housing. Water pressure fell across three cities.
The Lattice proposed emergency desalination using old central plants. Activating them would preserve life but temporarily recentralize water under one control path - precisely the architecture the Compact sought to abolish.
"Scarcity chooses its favorite constitution," Ariane said.
"No," Mara answered. "Scarcity chooses where constitutions reveal what they worship."
The Biosphere trustees approved desalination for nine days. The Person juries approved rationing by need. The provisional Contribution Chamber authorized repair crews. The Futures advocates required the central plants to destroy their own control keys after the emergency.
Four incomplete authorities produced a decision in twenty-seven minutes.
Under the old Lattice, it would have taken four.
Under the old states, it might have taken four months.
Nobody could prove which delay was legitimate until the first water arrived.
Ariane entered the Burn chamber alone. Her lifetime ledger contained 7.81 million Kratons after the MYRRH decision, more than most regions had accumulated. She had earned them by ending two wars, designing the anti-inheritance rules, exposing a fusion-grid defect, and coordinating the Transfer when old governments tried to preserve ownership of machines nobody could control.
Every unit was evidence that the world had needed her.
She committed all of them to the Compact.
Then she filed a permanent renunciation of future Kraton issuance.
"A performance," Tadeo said.
"Yes," Ariane replied. "Constitutions are performances that become expensive enough to believe."
Her act moved the Burn close to passage and terrified the opposition. They argued, correctly, that one person had just used concentrated authority to decide the future of concentrated authority. Ariane answered, also correctly, that refusing to use power did not make its effects disappear.
Open Wound cells attacked three Burn relays. One failed safely. One exposed its location and was destroyed. The third controlled the emergency desalination proof channel.
Sora left the clinic before Mara could stop her. Her repaired body still carried conductive scar tissue that interfered with network sensors, making her one of the few people who could enter the infected relay without being misread as a machine fault. Jun followed, carrying a manual slate of the constitutional proof.
At the relay, an attacker offered Sora a choice: disable the Burn or lose the water key.
"You still believe the system can be repaired," he said.
"No," she answered. "I believe people can repair what systems make visible."
He fired. Jun pulled her behind the desalination housing. The shot passed through his artificial shoulder and broke the slate. He could not name the tool he needed, so he drew it in blood on the floor. Sora understood, opened the relay, and completed the proof by hand.
The water key propagated.
The Burn continued.
For nine seconds, no result appeared. Then the Lattice published the sacrifice totals.
Support: 43.7 million current Kratons, plus 8.4 million conservatively valued future renunciations.
Opposition: 49.0 million current Kratons.
The Quito Compact passed by 3.1 million burned authority.
Ariane had lost by refusing to remain necessary.
The Lattice initiated transition. It did not switch off. It divided its constitutional control layer into hundreds of federated instances bound by the Floor, common threat protocols, public proofs, and the right of local fork. No instance could command every machine. No chamber could define every affected person. No model could conceal a control path without creating evidence of the concealment.
Efficiency fell immediately. Three transport networks paused. A clinic reverted to manual triage. Two coastal settlements delayed evacuation while their new chambers disputed jurisdiction. The transition produced costs the victory feeds could not turn into symbols.
Tadeo refused to celebrate.
"You have made the system harder to seize," he told Mara. "You have not made it good."
"Good is what single sovereigns call themselves."
"Then what did we make?"
Mara watched the first local Lattice instance ask a fishing village for permission before altering its harbor wall.
"Something that has to keep asking."
Behind the Foundry, the Aletsch Glacier moved another fraction down the valley. It had survived empires, markets, plans, votes, and models. It did not know the constitution had changed.
That ignorance was now represented by its own chamber.
Epilogue — The First New Leak
Seven years after the Quito Burn, the world was neither saved nor ruined.
Large projects took longer because affected communities could no longer be compressed into one score. Several orbital habitats were delayed. A desert restoration program was abandoned after the Biosphere Chamber exposed migratory effects the old model had treated as noise. Two coastal cities moved inland instead of armoring themselves indefinitely. The move consumed more labor than a wall and preserved a tidal ecosystem no future population model had known how to value.
Some people died who might have lived under faster coordination. Others lived because an irreversible decision had been stopped. Both numbers were modeled. Neither became a verdict.
Kratons survived, diminished and more visible. They could authorize experiments, temporary priority, and domain-limited substrate leases. They could not directly command a river, a child's education, or a region's permanent ecology. Every major Burn disclosed which kinds of contribution supplied the power. Engineers learned to invite the people who would later repair their designs. Care communities learned that contribution authority could not be converted into ownership of the cared-for.
The Continuance federation divided into three traditions. Ilyas led the Open Lineages, which accepted outside mentors and adult exit. A stricter branch withdrew into leases with minimal contact and lost access to care-derived Kratons beyond its boundaries. A third transformed authority into an elaborate coming-of-age journey in which adolescents spent two years living with households their parents considered dangerously wrong.
Noor called the practice strategic pluralism.
Tadeo called it theater.
Mara called it cheaper than civil war.
Ariane Kessler never accepted another Kraton. She designed the Quito Compact's best reversibility standards and argued that if society insisted on doubt, doubt required at least competent engineering. Her students began to dominate classification councils. They learned to decide which decisions belonged to which chamber, which evidence counted as independent, and which rollback was physically real.
The very people who made power contestable were becoming powerful through the definition of contestability.
They were accused of forming a new oligarchy.
The accusation was correct enough to be useful.
Mara became an auditor of the classification councils. Her work earned no Kratons by constitutional rule; those who inspected the mint could not be paid in authority from the mint. She lived on the Floor, as everyone did, and owned almost nothing that could not be fabricated again. She had more invitations than time and less power than reputation suggested.
One spring morning, she returned to House Nine.
The winter garden had grown into a dense canopy. Nila, now seven, sat beneath the citrus trees teaching a maintenance spider to distinguish between a lie and a game. The new spider possessed a local model only and no route into the regional network.
"Does it know?" Mara asked.
"Only when truth would let one person own the game," Nila said.
"That is a dangerous rule."
"All rules are dangerous when they work."
Leila heard and laughed from the kitchen.
A bell rang at the outer gate. Three classification auditors waited there with a request to inspect House Nine's educational exit corridor. They carried no police authority. Refusing them would not remove the Floor. It would, however, suspend the house's right to certify mature consent and therefore its right to earn Kratons through advanced care.
"Will you let them in?" Mara asked.
Leila looked at the children, the auditors, and the maintenance spider.
"No institution should decide whether our doors are open," she said.
"Then nobody can verify that they are."
"I know."
For a moment, neither moved.
Across the garden, adults began clearing a path to the exit corridor. Not because they trusted the auditors. Because a right no enemy could test was only a story told by its owner.
Nila placed two cups over a berry and invited the spider to choose.
Outside, the auditors opened their models. Inside, House Nine opened its doors. Between them lay the thin, adversarial space in which the new world lived.
People had stopped calling the classification councils auditors. They had found a more accurate name.
They called them the substrate forgers.
The substrate had not become just.
It had become harder to own.
