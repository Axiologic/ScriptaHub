A right needs protection
Rights Are Not Real asks why a recognized entitlement can fail to protect someone.
The provocative title separates moral claims, legal recognition, and institutions that make protection effective.
The book first gives political power its strongest realistic hearing.
It then challenges the mistake of treating successful coercion as moral justification.
Readers follow that tension toward protections strong enough to restrain their own defenders.

Three different claims
A moral claim can remain meaningful even when a legal system refuses to recognize it.
Legal recognition can exist while practical enforcement remains weak or inaccessible.
Effective protection depends on coordinated institutions and people able to make violation costly.
The book separates these layers because their familiar combination can hide the work sustaining them.
Its title challenges complacency about protection, not the possibility of judging existing power morally.

The hard thesis turns back
The argument first examines organization, coercion, institutional capture, and the costs of sustained vigilance.
It then confronts the slide from explaining power to declaring its outcomes legitimate.
A durable institution needs information and criticism that unrestricted authority can destroy.
The proposed counterpower protects people while limiting those entrusted with their defense.
This movement makes the book more demanding than a simple celebration of political realism.

Read what makes morality effective
Begin with the three meanings of a right and preserve their differences throughout the argument.
Read the final correction alongside the earlier account of organized power.
The book asks how protection can survive conflict without becoming property of its defenders.
Its depth lies in connecting moral judgment with the practical architecture of durable freedom.
Read it to see which institutions turn an entitlement into something a person can actually use.
