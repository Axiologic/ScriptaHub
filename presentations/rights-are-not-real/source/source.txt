Prologue — The Sentence Nobody Likes
The gap between judgment and protection
There is a sentence civilized people learn to reject before they examine it: might is right. It sounds childish, brutal, and historically illiterate. It seems to confuse victory with virtue and coercion with legitimacy. It evokes the conqueror who writes the law after winning the war, the patriarch who calls obedience tradition, the oligarch who buys a judge, the criminal organization that becomes the real government of a neighborhood, and the state that preserves the vocabulary of constitutionalism while steadily emptying it of meaning.
The civilized reply is better: rights are universal. A person does not cease to deserve bodily integrity because the surrounding community approves of violence. A minority does not lose its moral claim to protection because the majority dislikes it. A dissident is not transformed into a criminal in any morally interesting sense merely because a legislature has voted to criminalize dissent. Slavery does not become acceptable when it is customary. Rape does not become less violent because a culture has normalized a status relation in which consent is ignored. Torture does not become humane because it is administered by a recognized state.
But the moral superiority of that reply can conceal a political problem. A moral proposition is not a police force. A declaration is not a court. A constitution is not an independent judiciary. A judgment is not an enforcement mechanism. A vote is not an organization. A protest is not a coalition. An outrage is not a veto. The fact that something ought not to happen does not contain within itself the means by which it is prevented from happening.
Imagine a woman who has obtained a restraining order against a politically connected man. On paper, she has a legal right to protection. Now imagine that the local police delay every call, prosecutors quietly refuse to pursue violations, and the court repeatedly postpones hearings. Nothing in the statute has changed. The legal text still says what it said yesterday. Yet the effective meaning of the right has changed dramatically. The difference between the official document and her actual safety is not philosophical. It is institutional. Somewhere between the legal sentence and the street, power has intervened.
The same structure appears at every scale. An ethnic minority may possess constitutional equality while local employers, police, schools, and property owners coordinate informally to exclude it. Workers may enjoy the right to organize while losing their jobs whenever they attempt to use it. A newspaper may be legally free while every major advertiser understands which stories produce commercial punishment. A country may possess formal sovereignty while financial, military, technological, or energy dependence sharply limits the decisions its government can make. None of these examples proves that rights are unreal in the moral sense. They show that rights exist in layers, and that political rhetoric often blurs those layers because the blur is comforting.
The first purpose of this book is to remove that comfort.
The phrase “rights are not real” is intentionally provocative, but it does not mean that moral claims are meaningless or that cruelty becomes legitimate when it succeeds. It means that rights do not enter political reality merely because they can be stated. They become durable when they are embedded in institutions, habits, organizations, budgets, professional norms, technologies, expectations, and countervailing centers of power that make violation difficult, costly, reversible, or punishable. A right may be morally universal while remaining politically fragile. The two propositions are not contradictory.
Modern societies are especially vulnerable to forgetting this distinction because success hides its own machinery. When courts generally work, citizens experience due process as if it were a natural property of citizenship. When elections are routinely competitive, alternation in power feels normal. When a journalist can investigate a minister without disappearing, press freedom seems like an attribute of the social atmosphere. When a bank cannot simply confiscate a depositor’s money, property rights seem almost physical. The generations that inherit functioning institutions tend to perceive the outcome while forgetting the historical struggle, organizational labor, professional discipline, money, and sometimes blood that created the outcome.
This forgetfulness produces a dangerous category error. We begin to speak about institutions that depend on maintained balances of power as though they were moral constants of the universe. Then, when those balances change, we are surprised that the words remain while the reality thins out.
A right can die without being repealed. It can be priced beyond ordinary use. It can be delayed until remedy is meaningless. It can become dependent on social status. It can be interpreted so narrowly that only exceptional cases qualify. It can remain available in theory while those who invoke it are exposed to retaliation that the state does not prevent. It can be transformed from a general expectation into a privilege exercised mainly by people rich enough to sustain years of litigation. Political decay often proceeds through this loss of density long before any constitutional funeral is announced.
This is the gap the book will inhabit: the space between what should be true and what organized human systems are capable of making true.
The experiment of taking power seriously
For most of the book, the argument will take a deliberately hard position. It will ask what follows if political reality belongs, in the first instance, to actors who can organize, coordinate, finance, persuade, recruit, punish, reward, endure, and sometimes coerce. It will ask whether elites are irrational to capture institutions when capture is available and rivals are likely to attempt the same. It will ask whether a population that refuses the burdens of organization can reasonably expect to retain indefinitely the protections created by more organized ancestors. It will ask whether
“let cultures evolve naturally” has any coherent meaning once we notice that culture is already saturated with unequal authority.
The point is not theatrical cynicism. The useful realist question is rarely
“who is evil?”
It is
“what incentives and capacities make this outcome stable?”
A corrupt judge may be venal, but a system in which judges can be purchased has an institutional architecture that matters more than the psychology of any single judge. A violent patriarch may be cruel, but his power is amplified or constrained by inheritance rules, property ownership, policing, kinship networks, labor markets, education, exit options, and the expectations of neighbors. An authoritarian politician may desire domination, but desire becomes regime only when it acquires administrators, financiers, media allies, legal instruments, security organizations, and a sufficiently passive or divided opposition.
Power is therefore broader than violence. It includes the ability to define defaults, control information, create dependencies, set agendas, shape reputations, decide who can coordinate with whom, determine which disputes reach a court, and make certain futures appear too costly to attempt. The most effective power often becomes less visible precisely because it has been converted into routine. A police baton is obvious. A labor market in which one employer dominates an entire region may require no baton. A censor is obvious. A media environment in which ownership concentration makes certain investigations commercially suicidal may produce similar silence with less drama. A dictator’s order is obvious. A community norm that makes dissent socially ruinous can be just as decisive in ordinary life.
The book will also take seriously an argument that liberal moral language often avoids: from the perspective of an elite, institutional capture can be rational. If control of prosecutors, regulators, tax authorities, media, procurement systems, and courts protects wealth and reduces political risk, why would an ambitious faction voluntarily leave those instruments neutral? If rivals are expected to capture them when they gain power, restraint may appear strategically naive. The same logic can operate inside families, corporations, parties, religious institutions, professional guilds, and criminal networks. Power invites preemption because actors fear being the only ones who obey rules that others are willing to bend.
This line of reasoning will be pushed almost to the point where “might is right” begins to sound less like an adolescent slogan and more like an unpleasant compression of recurrent history. That is intentional. A contrarian argument is useful only when it is allowed to become strong enough to expose the assumptions on which ordinary reassurance depends. If the argument is softened every time it becomes uncomfortable, the reader learns nothing except the author’s preferred morality.
Yet the book is metarational rather than merely cynical. It does not assume that the hardest-sounding explanation is automatically the deepest. In the final
chapter, the argument will turn on itself. Not because cruelty suddenly becomes
distasteful, but because a serious theory of power eventually discovers that domination has internal failure modes. An elite can privatize justice and discover that contracts become less trustworthy for elites as well. A ruler can silence criticism and lose the information needed to govern. A faction can destroy independent institutions and discover that it has also destroyed the mechanisms that would protect it when power changes hands. A master can create obedience so complete that nobody nearby can safely report bad news. A victorious movement can reproduce the coercive structure it once opposed.
The final reversal will therefore not say that power is unimportant. It will say almost the opposite: power is too important to be understood only as the capacity to compel. Durable power depends on information, legitimacy, coordination, trust, predictable succession, credible commitments, distributed competence, and limits that prevent short-term predation from consuming the system that makes power possible. Rights can then be understood not as magical sentences floating above politics, but as technologies for structuring power so that no actor can cheaply convert temporary advantage into permanent predation.
That conclusion has to be earned. We will not begin there.
First, we must understand why history repeatedly rewards people who remember what comfortable societies prefer to forget: moral language without organization is fragile; institutions without defenders are temporary; and those who refuse to think about power do not thereby escape it. They merely increase the probability that power will be exercised by someone who has thought about it more carefully.
Chapter 1 — Rights Without Teeth
1.1 Three meanings of a right
The easiest way to misunderstand rights is to assume that the word names a single thing. In ordinary speech it does useful moral work precisely because it compresses several different claims. We say that a person “has a right” and move quickly from moral entitlement to legal recognition to practical protection. In well-functioning systems the compression is often harmless because the layers reinforce one another. In failing systems the distinction becomes decisive.
The first layer is moral. A person ought not to be tortured, enslaved, raped, arbitrarily imprisoned, robbed, or silenced merely because a stronger actor finds it convenient. This claim can remain meaningful even in a society whose institutions deny it. An abolitionist can say that an enslaved person has a moral right to freedom even when every local statute recognizes slavery. A dissident can possess a moral claim to speak even while the state prosecutes the speech. Moral language can therefore stand against existing power rather than merely describe it.
The second layer is legal. A recognized system declares an entitlement, prohibition, procedure, or remedy. This is a major political achievement because law can convert a moral aspiration into an instruction for institutions. Yet legal recognition is not self-executing. A statute can prohibit discrimination while officials ignore complaints. A constitution can guarantee due process while courts remain politically dependent. A treaty can condemn torture while security agencies operate in secrecy. Law changes the field, but the existence of a legal text does not guarantee the behavior of the organizations that must carry it out.
The third layer is effective. The right is supported by actors and mechanisms capable of making violation difficult, expensive, reversible, or punishable. Courts have jurisdiction and independence. Police enforce judgments. Journalists can investigate retaliation. Lawyers can represent unpopular clients. Civil society organizations can aggregate dispersed victims into visible constituencies. Elections can remove officials. Budgets exist to fund the institutions named in the law. Rival centers of power can block abuses. Citizens expect that using a right will not expose them to unbearable private punishment.
The distinction becomes clear in mundane situations. A tenant may legally possess protection against an unlawful eviction. If the landlord can nevertheless change the locks, cut utilities, intimidate witnesses, and delay proceedings for a year, the tenant’s effective right is weaker than the statute suggests. A worker may possess freedom of association while understanding that every organizer is quietly blacklisted across the local industry. A small firm may possess formal access to procurement while knowing that contracts circulate within a politically connected network. The legal vocabulary remains intact; the practical distribution of power tells a different story.

The moral layer can be real in the sense that moral reasons exist regardless of enforcement. The political claim of this book is narrower and harsher: a right that cannot survive contact with hostile power is not yet a completed political achievement. It is a demand whose institutionalization remains unfinished.
The distinction should not be used to mock victims for lacking power. Quite the opposite. Confusing moral entitlement with effective protection is dangerous chiefly for the vulnerable. The privileged can often bridge institutional gaps privately. They can hire lawyers, move jurisdictions, purchase security, attract publicity, or call people who return calls. Those without resources depend more heavily on general institutions. When those institutions weaken, the same written right becomes stratified by wealth and status.
This is why the language of possession can be misleading. “I have rights” sounds like “I have hands,” as if the object were attached to the individual. Politically, many rights are relational achievements. I can speak because others are prevented from punishing me arbitrarily for speech. I can own property because registries, courts, police, banks, and social expectations recognize claims and resolve disputes. I can vote meaningfully because ballots are counted, opposition can organize, and losing parties expect to survive until another election. The right resides partly in me and partly in a system of coordinated restraint around me.
The category error becomes most dangerous when successful systems encourage citizens to forget the second half of that sentence. A right is experienced individually but produced collectively. It can therefore erode collectively while the individual continues to speak about it as private property.
1.2 How conflict becomes institution
Many constitutional histories are narrated as if abstract reason gradually taught societies better principles. Reason mattered, but rights more often emerged from conflict, bargaining, organization, and exhaustion. The history of liberty is full of actors who accepted rules not because they became saints, but because no faction could safely obtain everything it wanted.
Magna Carta is useful precisely because its later symbolic meaning is much broader than its immediate political origin. The agreement forced on King John in 1215 was not a universal charter of modern human rights. It reflected a struggle between a monarch and powerful barons, embedded in a feudal order that excluded most people from political equality. Yet the episode illustrates a recurring mechanism: actors with enough power to resist convert conflict into procedure. A concession made to a narrow group can become a precedent, a legal vocabulary, an institutional memory, and eventually a resource for later groups whose claims were not originally contemplated.
The same pattern appears in labor history. The eight-hour day did not emerge because industrial owners collectively discovered an elegant theory of leisure. Workers organized, struck, financed associations, endured dismissals and violence, built political parties, negotiated, and created enough disruption that accommodation became less costly than indefinite conflict. The eventual legal rule concealed the struggle that had made the rule plausible. Once normalized, later generations could experience the limit on working hours as ordinary employment law rather than as the frozen outcome of political power.
Civil-rights history in the United States offers another instructive example. The Supreme Court’s 1954 decision in Brown v. Board of Education was legally important, but a judgment alone could not desegregate schools. When Arkansas authorities resisted the integration of Little Rock Central High School in 1957, federal power was required to enforce the legal order. The episode is morally uncomfortable for anyone who prefers rights to appear independent of force: constitutional equality became materially meaningful in that moment because one coercive apparatus constrained another. The troops did not create the moral right to equal protection. They helped create conditions under which the legal right could be exercised against organized resistance.
This is the recurring architecture. Social movements articulate claims. Organizations make those claims durable. Political pressure creates bargaining power. Law codifies settlements. Institutions routinize them. Professional norms lower enforcement costs. Citizens internalize expectations. Eventually the right appears natural because the conflicts that created it have disappeared from daily view.

The figure should not be read as a mechanical recipe. Historical change frequently moves backward, skips stages, or begins from legal innovation rather than movement pressure. Its purpose is to show that effective protection normally requires a chain rather than a sentence. A moral claim must find channels through which it can acquire recognition, institutional capacity, enforcement, remedy, and resilience. If any link is missing, the right becomes more vulnerable to actors willing to exploit the gap.
The important word is resilience. A functioning right is not merely enforced when everyone agrees with it. It survives when politically influential people dislike the result. Judicial independence matters most when a judge rules against a government that controls the budget. Press freedom matters most when publication harms an advertiser or official. property rights matter most when the claimant is weak and the opponent powerful. Elections matter most when incumbents lose. The test of the institution is therefore adversarial.
This perspective also clarifies why constitutional language can be both noble and misleading. Constitutions are partly moral texts, but they are also compressed histories of conflict. Separation of powers is not only a philosophical idea; it is a design for preventing one center of authority from cheaply converting temporary dominance into total control. Bicameralism, judicial review, federalism, professional civil services, independent central banks, free media, and protected associations can all be interpreted as methods for increasing the number of actors whose cooperation is required before power can be concentrated.
The polite vocabulary calls this checks and balances. The harder vocabulary calls it organized mistrust.
That mistrust is not a defect. It is an admission that political actors remain political after taking office. Good constitutions do not rely exclusively on good people. They attempt to make predation costly even when good people are absent. They transform the insight that power seeks advantage into an architecture in which advantage encounters friction.
The miracle of a mature legal order is therefore not that coercion disappears. It is that coercion is made procedural, contestable, and predictable enough that most disputes can be resolved without private violence. The state may still enforce a judgment, collect a tax, arrest an offender, or remove someone from property, but those acts occur inside a web of authorizations and counter-authorizations that ideally make power less personal.
This achievement is expensive. It requires trained professionals, institutional budgets, records, appeal procedures, public trust, political opposition, and the expectation that today’s loser can survive to contest tomorrow. Whenever those supporting structures are treated as decorative bureaucracy, rights begin to lose the machinery that converts moral language into ordinary life.
1.3 How rights decay without disappearing
The most dramatic image of lost freedom is repeal: a parliament abolishes a right, a dictator suspends a constitution, soldiers close a newspaper. Such events occur, but many rights die more quietly. They are not abolished; they are hollowed out.
Consider the sequence through which a legal guarantee can become practically irrelevant. First, enforcement becomes selective. The right remains reliable against weak violators but uncertain against connected ones. Then procedures become slower. A remedy available after five years may be legally real and practically useless. Costs rise as hearings multiply, administrative requirements expand, or specialized expertise becomes necessary. Exceptions proliferate. Officials invoke emergency, security, confidentiality, public order, technical necessity, or resource limitations. None of these categories is inherently illegitimate, but together they can transform a general rule into an obstacle course.
Citizens adapt before the law formally changes. A journalist learns which owner cannot be investigated. A civil servant understands which procurement file should not be examined too closely. A worker notices who is promoted after joining a union and who is not. A small entrepreneur learns that litigation against a politically connected competitor is commercially suicidal. A victim observes that people who complain become isolated. Each adaptation is individually rational. Collectively, they reduce the number of conflicts that reach visible institutions, which allows officials to claim that the system functions because formal complaints remain low.
This is one reason institutional decay is hard to measure. The disappearance of claims can be misread as the disappearance of violations. In reality, people may simply stop using remedies they no longer expect to work.
Rights also become unequal before they disappear. Wealth can substitute for weak public institutions. The wealthy can purchase private arbitration, legal teams, insurance, physical security, international mobility, reputation management, or access to decision-makers. The poor cannot. The same legal order then contains two effective regimes: formal equality in the text and stratified enforceability in practice. The right survives most visibly among those least dependent on it.
This dynamic complicates common political arguments about whether a country “has” rule of law, free speech, property rights, or due process. Such qualities are better treated as distributions than switches. Who can safely invoke the rule? Against whom? At what cost? How quickly? With what probability of retaliation? Does enforcement change with political connection, wealth, geography, ethnicity, profession, or institutional affiliation? These questions reveal density rather than mere existence.
A mature theory of rights should therefore think in terms of maintenance. Institutions must be funded. Judges must be appointed through processes that preserve sufficient independence. Lawyers must be willing to take unpopular cases. Journalists must possess business models that do not make investigation impossible. Opposition parties must be able to organize. Citizens must retain enough economic and social independence to challenge powerful actors without immediate ruin. None of this is glamorous, which is precisely why it is vulnerable to neglect.
The hard realist’s first thesis can now be stated more precisely than the slogan with which we began. Rights are not illusions, but neither are they self-executing properties of moral truth. An effective right is a stabilized relation of power in which enough institutions and actors can impose costs on violation that ordinary people are justified in expecting protection.
This formulation avoids two opposite errors. The first is romantic idealism: if a right is morally universal, one speaks as if protection already exists. The second is crude cynicism: if the strong can violate a right, one concludes that the right was meaningless. Both erase important distinctions. The victim of an unjust system may possess a valid moral claim even when protection is absent. Yet the political task is not completed by naming that claim. The task is to build the capacity that makes the claim matter when challenged.
That is why societies cannot simultaneously demand robust rights and treat every supporting form of power as morally contaminating. Courts require authority. Regulators require investigative capacity. Workers require organization. Minorities require allies and institutions. Democracies require means to defend procedures against actors who regard procedures as temporary obstacles. The relevant question is not whether power can be removed from rights. It cannot. The question is whether power can be arranged so that it protects the space in which people remain capable of contesting power itself.
The civilization that forgets this does not transcend coercion. It merely abandons the field to actors who remember that rules survive only when somebody can make violating them expensive.
Chapter 2 — There Is No Culture Outside Power
2.1 The harmonious village seen from far away
“Let cultures evolve naturally” is an attractive sentence. It promises humility after centuries in which empires, missionaries, administrators, development agencies, revolutionary movements, corporations, and foreign governments repeatedly entered other people’s lives with the confidence that they knew what progress required. The sentence seems to offer an ethical correction: stop treating communities as raw material for external projects; allow people to preserve their own meanings, practices, institutions, and pace of change.
The intuition deserves respect. External intervention can destroy forms of knowledge that outsiders do not understand. It can replace flexible local arrangements with rigid bureaucratic categories, create dependency, reward compliant elites, misread symbols, or turn cultural difference into a problem that must be solved. History contains more than enough evidence that outsiders armed with universal theories can produce damage while sincerely believing they are doing good.
But the phrase “evolve naturally” contains a hidden fiction. Cultures do not float outside power until an outsider contaminates them. Families allocate authority. Elders sanction behavior. Property systems distribute dependence. Religious institutions define legitimate and illegitimate identities. Marriage rules allocate reproductive and economic options. Markets reward some forms of life and punish others. Criminal organizations create parallel orders. Schools privilege some languages and histories. Employers shape what dissent costs. States recognize certain leaders as representatives and ignore others. Every culture is already an ecology of permissions, constraints, bargaining positions, and inherited asymmetries.
From far away, a village may look harmonious because conflict is invisible to the visitor. Harmony may be genuine. It may also be the surface produced when the people with the least power have learned that public disagreement is dangerous or futile. A communal decision announced by respected elders can reflect a deeply shared consensus, or it can reflect the preferences of the people who possess land, weapons, status, education, religious authority, or control over marriage and employment. The outsider cannot infer legitimacy merely from order.
Nor can the outsider infer oppression merely from unfamiliarity. This is the other side of the problem. A practice that appears restrictive from outside may be embedded in obligations, solidarities, and forms of security that participants value. Individuals can rationally prefer institutions that a foreign observer would not choose for herself. The danger lies in treating either local continuity or external criticism as self-validating.
The hard question is not whether a culture should be respected. It is who is being respected when the sentence is spoken.
Suppose a community permits early arranged marriages. The official explanation may emphasize family continuity, economic security, religious meaning, and protection from social isolation. Some participants may sincerely endorse those reasons. Others may comply because refusing would mean losing housing, inheritance, kinship, employment, or physical safety. An external campaign that simply bans the practice can produce backlash, concealment, or worse outcomes. Yet an external posture that treats the practice as an expression of “the culture” may silently identify the culture with those actors who already possess the authority to define it.
The same ambiguity appears in less exotic settings. A corporate culture may praise total commitment while employees who refuse eighty-hour weeks become unemployable in the firm. A university may celebrate intellectual tradition while junior scholars learn which questions threaten careers. A wealthy neighborhood may defend “community character” through zoning rules that exclude poorer families. A professional guild may call an entry barrier a quality standard while incumbents enjoy the scarcity it creates. Culture is not something that happens only in tribes or distant societies. It is the layer of norms through which power becomes ordinary enough to stop looking like power.
This is why romanticizing cultural stability can be as misleading as romanticizing external reform. Tradition is partly memory, partly identity, partly accumulated practical knowledge, partly coordination, partly imitation, and partly the sediment left by old conflicts whose losers no longer appear in the official story. Some customs survive because they work. Some survive because they make domination cheap. Often the same custom does both for different people.
A serious account must therefore resist the temptation to ask whether a culture is
“good” or “bad” in the abstract.
The relevant questions are more concrete. Who can interpret the norm? Who can contest it? Who can leave? Who bears the cost of dissent? Who controls property? Who speaks publicly? Who is represented when outsiders negotiate with
“community leaders”?
Who is absent because the conditions for speaking have already filtered them out?
These questions do not eliminate cultural autonomy. They make the concept more demanding.
2.2 Culture as low-cost power
Power is often imagined as the opposite of culture. Power commands; culture persuades. Power uses police; culture uses norms. Power coerces from outside; culture forms identity from within. The distinction is useful for some purposes, but it hides why durable orders invest so heavily in shaping norms. Culture is one of the most efficient forms of power because it can reduce the need for visible enforcement.
A person who repays a debt because
“people like us keep our word” requires no bailiff.
A soldier who refuses an illegal order because professional identity makes obedience shameful requires no external supervisor. A merchant who avoids fraud because reputation within a community is valuable requires less policing. A citizen who believes losing an election is normal democratic life does not need to be physically compelled to accept the result. Norms can civilize power by moving restraint inside the actor.
The same mechanism can entrench domination. A daughter who experiences self-sacrifice as the natural duty of her sex may enforce the norm against herself. A lower-status group may internalize deference so deeply that demands for equality feel immoral. An employee may interpret exhaustion as proof of commitment and exploitation as opportunity. A community may convert the interests of its dominant faction into sacred tradition. In these cases power becomes cheaper because those who bear its costs participate in reproducing it.
This does not mean that internalized norms are false consciousness. People are not empty vessels into which ruling groups pour beliefs. They interpret, negotiate, resist, combine, and transform what they inherit. The point is structural: the strongest forms of authority do not have to win every dispute anew. They create a background in which some disputes never arise, some alternatives are literally difficult to imagine, and some challenges feel like betrayals of self rather than political choices.

The table is deliberately symmetrical. The same cultural mechanisms that can stabilize oppression can also stabilize freedom. A professional norm of judicial independence constrains politicians. A culture of academic criticism makes intellectual authority contestable. A family norm that treats daughters and sons as equally entitled to education changes bargaining power long before any court becomes involved. A union culture of solidarity can reduce the employer’s ability to isolate individuals. Culture is not morally predetermined. It is a medium through which values become socially cheap to reproduce.
This helps explain why cultural conflicts are often more intense than disputes over explicit rules. The prize is not merely what people are permitted to do. The prize is what comes to feel normal. If a behavior is enforced only by law, opponents can hope to change the law. If the behavior is embedded in identity, language, honor, disgust, professional status, and family expectations, the enforcement network is distributed across thousands of ordinary interactions.
The most effective ruler therefore does not want subjects who awaken every morning, calculate the cost of resistance, and reluctantly obey. He wants obedience to become common sense. The most effective emancipation movement seeks the mirror image: not merely a new statute, but a world in which the old hierarchy begins to feel embarrassing, illegitimate, or absurd to people who once considered it natural.
Consider the transformation of smoking norms in many wealthy societies during the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries. Law mattered: taxation, advertising restrictions, age limits, and bans in public spaces changed incentives. Medical evidence mattered. But a decisive shift occurred when smoking in enclosed public places moved from an ordinary social act to one widely experienced as imposing on others. The law altered culture and culture reduced the enforcement burden of law. Similar feedback loops occur in seat-belt use, drunk driving, workplace harassment, littering, corruption, and many other domains. Institutional and cultural power are not rivals; they repeatedly construct one another.
This matters for the darker examples because it prevents a simplistic response. If a practice involving violence or severe domination is culturally embedded, changing the statute may be necessary but insufficient. Police may share the norm. Judges may interpret exceptions through it. Families may punish complainants. Victims may face economic ruin if they seek help. External condemnation can even strengthen the practice by allowing local elites to reframe it as a symbol of resistance to outsiders.
The relevant unit of analysis is therefore not “the norm” but the entire ecology that makes the norm reproduce itself. Who benefits materially? Who gains status? Which institutions ratify the practice? What exit routes exist? What alternative identities are available? Can dissenters find one another? Can they earn independent income? Are there spaces in which private disagreement can become collective action? Cultural change becomes intelligible when treated as a shift in these relations rather than as a battle between enlightened ideas and backward beliefs.
This is also where the phrase “respect the culture” becomes politically dangerous. Outsiders often need a representative to negotiate with. Power conveniently supplies one. Elders, clergy, chiefs, business associations, government ministries, party officials, or recognized civil-society organizations can speak in clear sentences and sign documents. The marginalized often produce ambiguity: whispered disagreement, migration, strategic compliance, anonymous testimony, silence, or contradictory behavior. Bureaucratic systems reward legibility, and powerful actors are more legible because they already possess the institutions that make their preferences public.
Power has microphones. Weakness often has fragments.
A serious respect for culture must therefore include respect for internal disagreement. Otherwise cultural autonomy becomes a diplomatic name for the authority of whoever already controls the local microphone.
2.3 Endogenous adaptation without romanticism
A more defensible alternative to either aggressive external reform or romantic noninterference is endogenous adaptation: change whose selection, pacing, interpretation, and institutionalization are substantially controlled by the people who must live inside it. The phrase is useful because it treats people as agents rather than recipients of modernization. Yet it only becomes meaningful when we ask what conditions allow “the people” to exercise control.
If dissenters can be beaten, excommunicated, fired, dispossessed, or forcibly married, then collective control is a fiction. If women cannot own property, their formal participation in a village meeting may say little about bargaining power. If a hereditary minority cannot move, educate its children, or access courts, the statement that “the community chose” an institution hides the distribution of choice. If a worker can technically leave a dominant employer but would lose health care, housing, and the only job available within a hundred kilometers, exit exists legally while remaining expensive enough to discipline behavior.
Endogenous adaptation therefore requires more than absence of foreign command. It requires internal capacities for voice, exit, association, information, and contestation. Paradoxically, outsiders can sometimes strengthen these capacities without dictating the substantive outcome. Scholarships change bargaining power. Secure communications allow dispersed dissenters to find one another. Independent legal aid gives weak actors access to formal institutions. Roads and migration routes expand exit. New employers reduce local monopsony. Access to information reveals alternatives. Each intervention changes the local equilibrium even if the outsider never specifies what the community should ultimately choose.
There is no neutral position here. Funding a girls’ school changes power. Refusing to fund it leaves existing power unchanged. Recognizing one set of community leaders rather than another changes power. Refusing all contact privileges whoever already controls local institutions. Opening trade changes power. Closing trade changes power. Even anthropological attention can alter status by deciding whose account of the culture becomes authoritative abroad.
The fantasy is not intervention. The fantasy is a world in which nonintervention produces no distributional effect.
This becomes morally acute in extreme cases. Suppose a group has the capacity to enslave a weaker neighboring population and treats the practice as legitimate. Suppose sexual violence is normalized within a status relation that denies meaningful consent. Suppose a hereditary underclass can be exploited with impunity. Suppose a religious institution systematically protects abusers because victims lack social standing. What is the moral significance of an outsider’s judgment if the outsider has no capacity, jurisdiction, coalition, or willingness to alter the victim’s situation?
The outsider may be entirely correct. Moral judgment does not become invalid merely because it is powerless. But the victim does not need the observer’s metaphysical permission to be wronged. The victim needs a change in effective capability. This distinction can make sophisticated moral debate appear obscene when it becomes a substitute for action. Observers refine their language about cultural sensitivity while the local actor with weapons, money, status, or institutional access continues behaving exactly as before.
The hard realist is therefore suspicious of two forms of vanity. The imperial vanity assumes that possessing power grants understanding. The spectator’s vanity assumes that possessing the correct judgment counts as having protected anyone. Both allow the observer to center himself.
The practical alternative is more demanding. External actors should ask whether their actions expand the capacity of people inside a culture to deliberate and contest without prescribing every outcome of that deliberation. This can justify restraint when intervention would destroy local agency. It can also justify support when existing local authority has made agency impossible. The standard is not “do nothing” or “make them like us.” It is whether the intervention redistributes the capability to shape institutions toward those who must bear the consequences.
That standard remains imperfect because power reappears at every level. Who funds the local organization? Who certifies that it represents anyone? Which dissenters are visible to donors? Which values are encoded in eligibility criteria? At some point every procedure rests on judgments that cannot be procedurally purified. Metarational thinking does not eliminate that problem. It refuses to hide it.
The second contrarian thesis can therefore be stated plainly. There is no culture outside power. To preserve a culture is to preserve some distribution of authority, status, resources, memory, and permissible dissent. To reform a culture is to alter that distribution. To refuse involvement is to leave existing local powers relatively stronger than potential challengers. None of these choices is innocent.
The defensible objective is not a culture untouched by power. Such a culture has never existed. It is a culture in which enough forms of counterpower exist that no faction can easily convert tradition into permanent ownership of other people’s lives.
Chapter 3 — The Innocence of Nonintervention
3.1 Doing nothing has beneficiaries
Nonintervention sounds morally clean because it describes an absence of visible action. No troops are sent, no regulation is imposed, no institution is reorganized, no external authority tells anyone what to do. The observer appears to keep his hands clean. Yet an absence of intervention is still part of the environment in which other actors calculate what they can do.
The logic is easiest to see in small settings. If one child repeatedly hits another while the parent declares neutrality, the parent’s nonintervention benefits the stronger child. If a landlord controls the only housing in a remote settlement and the state refuses to enforce basic tenant protections, the refusal strengthens the landlord’s bargaining position. If a monopoly employer can dismiss organizers and the government treats labor relations as purely private, the policy of abstention is not neutral between employer and employee. It changes the price of resistance.
The same reasoning scales upward. A government that refuses to enforce competition law may call the result a free market, but incumbents experience the absence of enforcement as an asset. A platform that declines to moderate coordinated harassment may describe itself as neutral, while organized harassers benefit from rules that impose more costs on targets than aggressors. A state that refuses to protect a minority from local violence may insist that it is avoiding favoritism, while the locally dominant faction receives the practical freedom to coerce.
This does not prove that intervention is always desirable. It proves something narrower and more important: nonintervention should be evaluated by its consequences rather than by its visual innocence. Doing nothing is a distributional decision when the background distribution of power is unequal.
Political morality often has trouble with this because visible acts attract responsibility more readily than background conditions. A government that intervenes in a crisis can be blamed for every mistake caused by the intervention. A government that does nothing can describe subsequent harm as the product of ancient conflict, market forces, social complexity, or unfortunate local choices. The causal chain is less visible, and moral accounting follows visibility.
Sophisticated actors learn to exploit this asymmetry. Power is easier to defend when it is embedded in defaults. A legislature does not need to ban an opposition newspaper if government advertising and licensing rules can quietly make the newspaper insolvent. A university does not need to prohibit a line of research if hiring and promotion incentives make it career suicide. A company does not need to order employees not to organize if scheduling practices, subcontracting, and individual performance systems make organization too costly. A family does not need to physically confine an adult child if dependence on housing, inheritance, social belonging, and immigration status makes exit ruinous.
Invisible power benefits from being mistaken for the natural environment.
This is why serious analysis begins by asking not
“who acted?” but “what options did the institutional arrangement make cheap or expensive?”
A policy of abstention can be wise when intervention would generate worse harms. It can also be cowardice disguised as humility. The distinction cannot be made by looking at whether an actor’s hands remain visibly clean.
The pattern becomes clearer when we examine how law defines a baseline. Contract law, property law, bankruptcy law, labor law, and family law are sometimes described as neutral frameworks within which private actors bargain. But the framework itself assigns bargaining positions. If dismissal is legally easy, an employer enters negotiation with one set of options; if dismissal triggers substantial obligations, the worker enters with another. If household property is legally controlled by one spouse, private family bargaining begins from that fact. If creditors can seize essential assets immediately, debt negotiation occurs under a different distribution of fear than if bankruptcy rules provide a protected minimum.
There is therefore no policy position called
“leave power alone.”
Government always defines some background of enforceable claims, even when that background is inherited rather than consciously chosen. The decision not to change it is still a decision to reproduce it for another period. This does not make every inequality an injustice or every intervention desirable. It simply prevents political argument from giving the status quo a fictitious zero value.
Once we see defaults this way, nonintervention becomes a substantive policy that must defend itself like any other. It may be justified by ignorance, institutional humility, subsidiarity, respect for autonomy, or the risk of unintended consequences. Those are serious reasons. But “we did nothing” is no longer an argument by itself. The relevant question is what doing nothing allowed other actors to continue doing.
3.2 Rescue, sovereignty, and dirty hands
The obvious objection is powerful: outsiders repeatedly make things worse. History contains interventions justified by religion, civilization, security, modernization, development, anti-communism, democracy, counterterrorism, humanitarianism, and human rights that produced exploitation, destroyed institutions, empowered collaborators, intensified conflict, or created dependencies that lasted generations. Any doctrine that treats external capacity as moral license is dangerous.
The hard realist should concede this completely. Power does not become knowledgeable merely by becoming powerful. Military superiority does not provide understanding of local political economies. Wealth does not reveal which institutions are legitimate. Technical expertise can optimize the wrong objective. Foreign administrators often mistake the groups easiest to negotiate with for the groups most representative of society. Interventions generate second-order effects because local actors adapt strategically: elites rebrand themselves to attract support, opponents frame conflicts in the vocabulary donors understand, and institutions evolve to capture external resources.
Yet failure of rescue does not sanctify abandonment. The relevant choice is rarely intervention versus purity. It is one intervention with uncertain consequences versus another background policy, including abstention, whose consequences also fall on real people.

International sovereignty makes this tension especially visible. The modern state system depends on a powerful convention: recognized governments possess authority within territorial boundaries, and other states should not casually use force to redesign their internal arrangements. This convention reduces the frequency with which stronger states can treat weaker states as administrative projects. It protects political independence and makes coexistence possible among societies with different values.
But sovereignty also raises the question of what precisely is sovereign. Is the state sovereign over the people, or is the state the institutional form through which a population exercises collective agency? If the first proposition is taken literally, nearly any external criticism can be dismissed as interference. If the second is taken seriously, a government cannot automatically transform severe abuse into a morally private matter merely by controlling the border.
The dilemma cannot be solved by vocabulary alone. Consider episodes in which international actors knew mass killing was occurring but were unwilling to bear the risks of intervention. The failure to act is later described as a moral failure precisely because nonintervention did not create neutrality for the victims. It left the locally stronger killers with fewer obstacles. Yet other interventions undertaken with humanitarian or democratic language have produced prolonged instability and civilian harm. The record refuses to provide a clean doctrine.
What it can provide is a stricter method of accounting. Count predictable harms on both sides. Ask who gains power and who loses it. Examine whether the proposed protector can be removed if it becomes predatory. Ask whether local actors retain meaningful ownership of the institutions created. Study exit options, information problems, incentives for collaboration, and the danger that temporary emergency authority becomes permanent. Compare intervention not to an imaginary zero, but to the likely trajectory of the system if nothing changes.
The table exists to discipline intuition, not to generate an automatic answer. “Intervene” and “do not intervene” are not moral categories. Each can protect agency or destroy it, depending on design and context. A limited intervention that opens humanitarian corridors, supports local legal capacity, or protects independent communication may redistribute power differently from occupation or externally imposed regime change. Similarly, nonintervention can reflect prudent respect for local autonomy or indifference to predictable domination.
The deeper issue is dirty hands. Political action often requires choosing under uncertainty when every available option implicates the chooser in some harm. The desire to remain morally unstained is understandable, but it can become a form of irresponsibility if abstention is treated as though it had no causal footprint. Leaders who make no decision still govern the default.
This insight does not abolish moral limits on intervention. It strengthens them by replacing self-image with consequence. The question is not whether an actor can tell a morally flattering story about restraint. The question is whether restraint leaves vulnerable people with more or fewer practical options than feasible alternatives.
The institutional design of rescue matters as much as the motive. Temporary protection that strengthens local courts, independent media, medical systems, or secure channels of exit can leave behind more agency than an intervention organized around permanent external administration. Conversely, even technically competent aid can hollow out domestic institutions if the best local professionals leave public service to work for better-paid external organizations. The rescuer can unintentionally become the most powerful labor market, agenda setter, and source of legitimacy in the very society it claims to empower.
This is why humility must be operational rather than rhetorical. An intervention is more humble when it contains mechanisms that allow local actors to reject, redirect, and eventually terminate it. It is less humble when the external actor remains judge of whether local people are ready to govern themselves. Exit rules, accountability, local fiscal capacity, and the distribution of knowledge after the intervention ends matter more than declarations of respect.
The realist insight is uncomfortable but useful: rescuers need constraints for the same reason rulers do. Good intentions cannot substitute for counterpower. A humanitarian or developmental organization that cannot be challenged by the people whose lives it reorganizes is still an institution exercising power, even if its staff are unusually benevolent.
3.3 Who may judge, and who must pay?
The phrase “who are we to judge?” can express genuine humility. Outsiders often misunderstand contexts they enter, apply categories selectively, and judge other societies more harshly than their own. The history of moral judgment is full of hypocrisy. Empires condemned local cruelties while practicing their own. Governments invoke human rights against geopolitical rivals and ignore similar abuses by allies. Social classes moralize the behavior of people whose material constraints they have never experienced.
But the phrase can also become an abdication. If no outsider may judge any practice whose participants describe it as traditional, moral criticism ends exactly where local power has succeeded in monopolizing interpretation. The slaveholder can define slavery as social order. The abusive patriarch can define control as family duty. The dominant caste can define hierarchy as sacred. The criminal organization can define extortion as protection. The state can define repression as security. Power has always possessed vocabularies in which its own behavior appears necessary.
Moral standing and political capacity must therefore be separated. A powerless observer may have good reasons to judge an act wrong. His lack of power does not make the judgment false. But the political significance of the judgment depends on what follows from it. The victim remains inside the system that can act. The observer remains inside a system that can comment.
This distinction reveals an uncomfortable asymmetry of sacrifice. People in safe institutions can demand justice at relatively low personal cost. Those expected to create justice may have to risk careers, wealth, safety, freedom, or life. A foreign audience applauds dissidents but does not share prison. Consumers praise labor rights while continuing to reward supply chains built around weak bargaining power. Citizens condemn corruption while avoiding the organizations, taxes, watchdog institutions, and electoral work required to constrain it. Moral applause and political burden are frequently separated.
The hard realist asks a question that polite discourse dislikes: what obligations accompany the demand that somebody else take risks on behalf of our values? If we insist that a vulnerable group resist domination, what resources are we willing to provide? If we believe an institution should remain independent, are we willing to fund it when it produces decisions we dislike? If we demand that journalists expose corruption, will we subscribe to the publications that employ them, defend them when they are sued, and tolerate investigations of our own side? If we believe a foreign atrocity must be stopped, what costs are we prepared to bear rather than merely assign to anonymous soldiers or taxpayers?
This is not an argument that only people willing to die may speak. Democratic morality would become grotesque if every opinion required personal martyrdom. It is an argument against treating cost as someone else’s problem. Rights become political when coalitions distribute the cost of protecting them widely enough that individual defenders are not destroyed one by one.
The asymmetry also helps explain why organized minorities can dominate dispersed majorities. A small group with intense interests may rationally invest time and money in changing a rule that produces large benefits per member. Millions of citizens who each bear a small cost may rationally remain inattentive. The result can be a policy opposed by most people in the abstract but defended effectively by the few who have organized around it. Political systems are not simple counting machines for preferences. They are markets in organized attention, resources, expertise, and persistence.
The third contrarian thesis follows. Nonintervention is not the absence of power. It is a decision about which existing powers will operate without additional constraint. Moral judgment is not protection. It becomes politically consequential only when connected to actors willing and able to carry costs. Sovereignty is not metaphysical ownership of suffering by whoever controls a territory. It is a useful political convention whose legitimacy depends in part on whether it protects the agency of people rather than merely the authority of rulers.
There is no position from which one can avoid all responsibility by refusing to choose. The choice to abstain remains a choice inside a field already structured by power.
A mature politics therefore separates three questions that public debate often collapses. Is the practice wrong? Is an external actor entitled to condemn it? Is that actor competent and justified in attempting to change it? The answer can be yes, yes, and no. Moral clarity about a practice does not automatically provide a responsible intervention strategy. Conversely, uncertainty about intervention does not require pretending the practice is morally undecidable.
This separation is valuable because it allows humility without paralysis. We can condemn slavery while admitting that a particular military intervention would be catastrophic. We can recognize severe exploitation while preferring measures that expand exit, information, and local organization rather than impose an external institutional template. We can judge an abuse while refusing to make the victim the experimental subject of our need to act dramatically.
The burden of power is precisely this refusal of easy symmetry. Actors with capacity must answer not only for what they do but also for foreseeable harms they permit when alternative action was feasible. Actors without capacity should not confuse eloquence with protection. Between them lies politics: the construction of coalitions capable of bearing costs without transforming protection into a new ownership claim over those protected.
Chapter 4 — The State Is Organized Coercion
4.1 The coercion inside ordinary order
Modern states are so familiar that their coercive foundation disappears into routine. Taxes arrive as forms and electronic transfers. Property appears as entries in registries. Court judgments are printed on paper. Regulations are written in administrative language. Elections take place according to schedules. Because most citizens encounter these processes peacefully, it is easy to describe the state primarily as a provider of services, a democratic forum, or an expression of collective identity.
All of those descriptions can be true. They are incomplete.
A tax is ultimately backed by sanctions. A property title is meaningful because trespassers can eventually be removed. A court judgment matters because the losing party cannot simply ignore it indefinitely without consequence. Environmental rules constrain factories because inspectors, fines, license suspensions, and ultimately coercive enforcement exist behind them. The state is distinctive not because it uses violence constantly, but because it organizes authoritative coercion so successfully that violence is rarely necessary.
The classic phrase “monopoly on the legitimate use of physical force” should not be read too literally. No state has a perfect monopoly. Families, firms, gangs, private security companies, social movements, militias, and foreign actors possess varying capacities to coerce. The state’s special position is that it claims the authority to decide which uses of coercion are legitimate, to resolve disputes among competing claims, and to mobilize enough organized force that its decisions usually become the final reference point.
This structure is morally ambiguous by design. Without organized coercion, many rights would be exposed to private power. A restraining order means little if nobody can compel the abuser to stay away. A contract means less if the stronger party can simply ignore it. Environmental protections are requests if polluters face no sanctions. The abolition of slavery required more than a moral declaration because slaveholders possessed property claims, local institutions, and armed power. Public coercion can therefore be the mechanism through which private coercion is constrained.
At the same time, the state can become the largest predator in the system. The organization that can arrest kidnappers can also imprison dissidents. The tax authority that funds public goods can be used to punish enemies. The intelligence service that detects foreign threats can surveil political opponents. The licensing system that protects safety can exclude competitors. The police force that protects a minority can enforce discrimination against it. The same concentration of capacity that makes rights enforceable makes systematic abuse possible.
The mature political question is therefore not whether coercion can be removed from the state. A state without the capacity to enforce general rules is not necessarily freer; it may simply create a vacuum filled by private coercion. The question is how coercive capacity is authorized, distributed, supervised, appealed, and limited.
This is why due process matters. It slows power down. It forces the state to translate suspicion into reasons, reasons into evidence, evidence into procedures, and procedures into decisions that other actors can review. From the perspective of an impatient ruler, this is inefficiency. From the perspective of anyone who might someday be wrongly accused, the inefficiency is the protection.
A mature legal order is thus a technology for making coercion boring. It turns personal retaliation into standardized procedure, replaces feuds with filings, and forces powerful actors to wait for forms they would rather bypass. The apparent dullness of administration is one of civilization’s most underappreciated achievements.
This reframing also helps with a common libertarian intuition: if coercion is dangerous, perhaps the solution is simply to reduce the state until individuals transact voluntarily. Voluntary exchange is enormously valuable, but it depends on prior decisions about ownership, contract, liability, fraud, inheritance, corporate form, and the enforceability of agreements. A market is not the absence of political order. It is a particular political order in which many decisions are decentralized after a public framework has specified the claims participants may enforce.
The same is true of family autonomy, religious autonomy, and local self-government. Decentralization can protect freedom by keeping distant authorities from controlling intimate life. It can also concentrate power locally where exit is costly. Central law can be oppressive, but it can also provide the weak with an appeal against a local majority. The relevant comparison is therefore not state coercion versus no coercion. It is one allocation of coercive authority versus another.
This is the scandal hidden in ordinary government: civilization works not by abolishing force but by making the authority to use force conditional, legible, and contestable. When those conditions weaken, coercion becomes personal again. The difference between an official and a warlord is not that one compels and the other does not. It is that the official is supposed to operate inside an institutional chain that others can inspect and challenge.
4.2 Why people obey, and when they stop
If the state rested only on fear, governance would be catastrophically expensive. Every tax collection would require a threat. Every regulation would need an inspector at every door. Every election loss would produce a security crisis. Durable states instead rely on a mixture of force, legitimacy, habit, coordination, dependence, and belief.
Most people pay ordinary taxes not because an officer stands beside them but because they expect others to pay, believe the state is entitled to collect, fear eventual penalties, value public goods, and have organized their lives around compliance. Drivers stop at red lights at empty intersections because law has become habit and because they expect the system to function when everyone follows the rule. Businesses honor court judgments because future access to the legal system matters and because defiance carries reputational and financial costs. Legitimacy lowers the price of enforcement.
This helps explain why governments care about narratives. A regime that can portray its commands as lawful, traditional, democratic, religiously required, scientifically necessary, economically inevitable, or nationally protective needs less visible coercion than one perceived as nakedly predatory. The battle for legitimacy is not cosmetic. It is a struggle over enforcement costs.
Yet legitimacy alone is not enough. People also obey because coordination is valuable. Even citizens who dislike a particular government may prefer a common legal framework to competing private authorities. A merchant wants contracts interpreted predictably. A commuter wants one set of traffic rules. A family wants property records that others recognize. The state can remain stable because many actors benefit from the existence of a focal authority even while disagreeing over how it is used.
This creates a paradox. The state is strongest when citizens voluntarily comply, but voluntary compliance is partly produced by the credible background possibility of enforcement. If everyone knew that taxes were entirely optional, norms might erode quickly. If everyone expected the police to ignore court orders, private enforcement would expand. Coercion and legitimacy support one another until abuse causes them to pull apart.
The threshold at which obedience fails is rarely obvious. A government can become less legitimate while remaining fully functional for years because habit and dependence persist. Citizens may dislike corruption but still require licenses, pensions, schools, passports, courts, and employment. Firms may distrust officials but still need access to procurement or permits. Bureaucracies can continue operating through professional routine even when political leadership loses credibility.
This inertia is useful because it prevents every political disagreement from becoming a regime crisis. It is also dangerous because it allows institutional degradation to proceed gradually. By the time citizens conclude that ordinary compliance sustains a predatory order, the networks capable of coordinating resistance may already have weakened.
Political stability is therefore not evidence that power is legitimate. It can reflect genuine consent, coordination benefits, fear, lack of alternatives, or some mixture. The same visible peace can be produced by very different underlying relations.
The hard realist refuses to infer too much from obedience. He asks instead what would happen if the state demanded something costly from a powerful group. Would the group comply? Could it evade? Could it buy an exception? Could it mobilize enough economic or social disruption to force revision? Law reveals its true distribution when it encounters actors who can resist.
The mixture of motives behind obedience also explains why sudden regime collapse often surprises observers. For years, millions of people may comply while privately disliking the order. Each person sees visible compliance by others and infers that resistance would be isolated. If a shock reveals that many others were also complying strategically, expectations can change much faster than underlying preferences. What looked like deep legitimacy can evaporate because part of the stability was coordination around a belief about other people’s behavior.
The reverse can happen in democracies. Citizens may complain constantly about institutions while continuing to defend them when a serious challenge appears. Everyday criticism is not necessarily evidence that legitimacy has vanished. One test is whether people accept adverse decisions when the procedure is credible. Another is whether officials can lose power without expecting personal destruction. A system in which losers leave office and later return to ordinary life has achieved something more important than high approval ratings.
Obedience should therefore be read dynamically. Governments need enough legitimacy for voluntary compliance, enough coercive capacity to handle defection, and enough procedural restraint that coercion does not destroy legitimacy faster than it restores order. The proportions vary by circumstance, but no stable regime escapes the triangle.
4.3 The state as prize and the privatization of justice
Once the state is understood as an organized concentration of coercive and administrative capacity, a disturbing implication follows. Control of the state is an extraordinarily valuable asset. It can alter taxes, contracts, licenses, investigations, public procurement, education, land use, banking rules, media access, policing priorities, and the distribution of public employment. Political competition is therefore never only a contest over ideas. It is also a contest over machinery capable of changing the fortunes of groups.
This does not mean every politician seeks office for private gain. It means the institutional prize is valuable enough to attract actors who do.
The most consequential corruption is not a cash envelope exchanged for a permit. Petty bribery is visible and often socially destructive, but state capture operates at a deeper level. A business network may influence the rules by which competitors are licensed. Political leaders may ensure that prosecutors avoid certain investigations. Judges may remain formally independent while appointment, promotion, budget, or disciplinary systems create predictable incentives. Media owners may exchange favorable coverage for access to public contracts. Procurement can channel state resources toward loyal firms that then finance the political network. The border between public and private power becomes porous.

Public power normally constrains private power: courts enforce contracts, police restrain violence, regulators limit fraud, and elections allow citizens to remove officials. Under capture, these arrows reverse. Private networks gain privileged access to public coercion, and public institutions become instruments through which private advantage is enforced against rivals.
The result is a privatization of justice even when courthouses remain open. The connected actor can threaten investigation, delay cases, obtain selective inspections, influence procurement, or exploit regulatory complexity that burdens competitors more than allies. The state still appears public because formal offices remain intact. Its decisions increasingly behave like assets within a patronage network.
Why would elites attempt this? Because the state is too powerful to ignore. If one faction expects rivals to weaponize institutions when they gain office, leaving those institutions neutral can appear strategically suicidal. Control today becomes insurance against control tomorrow. Political appointments become defensive.
Media influence becomes defensive. Friendly prosecutors become defensive. Soon every faction describes its own capture as necessary to prevent worse capture by enemies.
This logic can generate a race in which each actor becomes individually rational and the system collectively irrational. Institutions lose credibility, so every group invests more in private protection. Because public justice is unreliable, firms cultivate political relationships. Because firms cultivate political relationships, public justice becomes more unreliable. Citizens stop expecting neutral rules and vote for patrons who can protect “our people.” The distinction between corruption and governance dissolves.
The fourth contrarian thesis is therefore harsher than the usual moral condemnation of corruption. A state is not merely an arena in which power competes. It is one of the most valuable objects of competition because it can convert political victory into legal, economic, and coercive advantage. Actors who forget this may lose institutions to actors who do not.
But the argument has not yet shown that capture is wise. It has shown why it can be rational from inside the game. The next chapter will allow the elite to make its strongest case before we ask what happens when everyone follows the same logic.
Captured systems also create a peculiar hierarchy among elites. Outsiders often imagine “the elite” as a unified bloc. In practice, wealthy and influential groups compete fiercely. Some depend on open markets, predictable courts, and international investment; others profit from licenses, monopolies, public contracts, or privileged access to natural resources. State capture can therefore be a struggle among elites with different business models rather than a simple war between a unified ruling class and everyone else.
This difference matters because reform coalitions sometimes emerge from actors who are powerful enough to defend impersonal institutions for self-interested reasons. A firm that expects to compete through innovation may prefer neutral procurement to patronage. A financial sector integrated into international markets may value judicial credibility. Regional governments may resist central capture to preserve their own autonomy. Power can constrain power without requiring anyone to become altruistic.
The practical lesson is important for the later reversal of the book. If the state is a prize, the solution cannot be to hope that nobody wants it. The more plausible strategy is to divide the prize, narrow what any victory delivers, and create actors with resources to resist total capture. Constitutional design is therefore less a sermon against ambition than a method for making ambitions collide productively.
Chapter 5 — Why Corruption Is Rational
5.1 The elite's case for capture
Corruption is usually described in the moral vocabulary of individual vice. Officials are greedy, businesspeople are unscrupulous, voters are cynical, judges are compromised. These descriptions may be accurate, but they are analytically weak when they leave the strategic structure untouched. A better question is why rational actors repeatedly invest in corrupting institutions even when they understand that corruption damages the society in which they live.
Imagine an elite family that owns major businesses and has accumulated wealth over several generations. It operates in a country where elections are competitive but institutions are young, party systems unstable, and property disputes frequently politicized. A reform movement rises on a platform of redistribution and anti-corruption. Its leaders promise independent prosecutors and tougher regulation. From the reformers’ perspective, this is a project to restore impartial law. From the family’s perspective, it may look like a threat whose eventual boundaries are impossible to know.
The family has choices. It can trust that courts will remain neutral even if political rhetoric becomes hostile. It can accept that prosecutors selected by future opponents will distinguish legitimate investigation from political retaliation. It can allow media outlets it does not control to shape public narratives about its wealth. Or it can act preemptively: finance friendly parties, acquire media, cultivate judges, place allies in regulators, diversify assets abroad, and build relationships inside the security apparatus. Each step can be narrated as prudence.
This is the elite’s strongest argument: institutions are valuable precisely because they can be weaponized. If rivals are likely to capture them, unilateral restraint is not virtue but exposure.
The logic is not limited to wealthy families. Political parties pursue it when they alter electoral rules while in office because they fear what opponents would do with the same authority. Bureaucratic factions protect budgets and appointments because losing administrative control may mean losing relevance for a generation. Trade unions seek privileged access to labor ministries. corporations cultivate regulators. Media organizations defend licensing rules that protect incumbents. Professional associations lobby to determine who may enter an occupation. Religious organizations bargain for authority over education or family law. Each group can describe its position as necessary protection against actors with incompatible interests.
The strategic core is familiar from security dilemmas. A measure taken defensively by one actor appears offensive to another. A company finances a politician because competitors are financing politicians. The competitor interprets the donation as evidence that political access is necessary. An opposition party sees the governing party placing loyalists in independent institutions and concludes that it must do the same when it returns to office. Judges see politics becoming more partisan and seek patrons who can protect their careers. The expectation of capture produces the behavior that makes capture increasingly rational.
This is why moral exhortation alone rarely solves systemic corruption. Telling actors to “respect institutions” asks them to bear a risk that the institutional environment may not reward. If the first faction to behave impartially loses everything to the faction that does not, impartiality becomes difficult to sustain as an equilibrium.
The hard realist is tempted to conclude that the elite is simply behaving intelligently. If political institutions are instruments, control them. If the law can be used against you, shape the law. If regulators can destroy your firm, influence regulators. If public opinion can threaten wealth, influence public opinion. If courts decide property, cultivate courts. The logic appears especially compelling when everyone else is already playing the same game.
This chapter will not rescue the reader from that conclusion immediately. The point is to see why corruption can be internally rational before declaring it externally destructive.
This perspective also explains why elites often support the language of the rule of law selectively. General predictability is useful for commerce, inheritance, and ordinary transactions. What the strategic elite wants is not necessarily anarchy. It wants a mostly functional legal order with exceptional access at moments of high consequence. Total corruption would destroy too much value. Selective privilege preserves the benefits of order while reducing personal exposure.
That combination is politically durable because many citizens experience genuine legality in ordinary life. Their apartment sale is registered, traffic fines are processed, routine lawsuits are decided, and public services function. Claims of capture can therefore sound exaggerated until a case touches a protected network. The system is neither rule of law nor lawlessness. It is stratified legality.
Stratified legality is more stable than crude tyranny because it gives most people reasons to defend the system most of the time. The privileged exception hides inside the normal rule. This is why diagnosing capture requires attention to variance: whether outcomes change systematically when high-status actors become involved, not merely whether institutions work at all.
5.2 Preemptive capture and corruption as insurance
Once institutions become politically contestable assets, capture begins to resemble insurance. A firm does not need a favorable judge in every case. It needs confidence that a catastrophic case will not be decided by an adversary. A political leader does not need every newspaper to praise him. He needs enough influence that a scandal cannot become existential before he can respond. A wealthy network does not need to evade every tax. It needs the ability to prevent tax enforcement from becoming a weapon uniquely directed at it.
This is why the most valuable corruption is often not the transaction that produces immediate profit. It is the relationship that reduces uncertainty. Access to a minister may matter more than a particular subsidy. A friendly prosecutor may matter even if no prosecution is currently expected. A media outlet may operate at a loss because its political value exceeds its commercial value. A network of appointments across regulatory bodies can function as an early-warning system. The captured institution becomes part shield, part sensor, part bargaining chip.
From inside the network, these arrangements may not feel corrupt. Participants use words such as trust, reliability, loyalty, access, continuity, national interest, strategic partnership, or institutional stability. Moral language adapts to strategic necessity. People rarely experience themselves as villains. They experience themselves as people doing what must be done in an environment they did not choose.
The logic becomes stronger when law is complex and discretion unavoidable. No regulatory system can specify every case. Prosecutors decide which files deserve priority. inspectors decide which violations require immediate action. Tax authorities interpret ambiguous transactions. Banks assess risk. procurement committees compare offers that are never perfectly commensurable. Wherever discretion exists, relationships can affect outcomes without requiring an explicit illegal order.
A sophisticated capture strategy therefore aims not to abolish rules but to control interpretation. The institution continues to function. Most routine cases may be handled professionally. The network intervenes only when stakes become important. This selective use preserves public legitimacy better than blatant corruption because ordinary citizens continue to encounter normal procedure most of the time.

Corruption as insurance also explains why wealth tends to seek political diversification. A rational elite does not want all protection from one party, one ministry, or one ideology. It funds multiple relationships, hires former officials from different factions, places family members across sectors, invests abroad, and maintains channels with whoever might govern next. To the public this looks cynical. To the actor it is portfolio management applied to political risk.
The analogy should not be pushed into mathematical theater. No elite calculates a precise expected value for purchasing a judiciary. Decisions are made under uncertainty, with imperfect information, emotion, identity, and historical memory. Yet the underlying intuition remains: the cost of maintaining influence may be small compared with the possible loss if a hostile state acquires unconstrained power over one’s assets or freedom.
This creates a perverse lesson for citizens. If powerful actors are building private protection against public institutions, everyone else has an incentive to seek patrons as well. A small entrepreneur joins the ruling party because permits arrive faster. A civil servant seeks a political sponsor because merit rules are unreliable. A family votes for a local boss because he can obtain hospital access or a job. Patronage becomes rational adaptation to the unreliability produced by patronage.
The system can therefore be corrupt without requiring a population of unusually corrupt people. Ordinary actors learn that impersonal rules are less reliable than relationships. Once that belief becomes accurate, refusing to cultivate relationships can be costly. Corruption reproduces itself as social knowledge.
The insurance logic becomes particularly strong during transitions. When regimes change, constitutions are rewritten, or new technologies disrupt established industries, nobody knows which claims will remain protected. Actors with resources invest in influence not only to obtain rents but to reduce the variance of the future. Transitional uncertainty can therefore produce exactly the capture that later prevents institutions from consolidating.
This creates a sequencing problem for reformers. If they attack every incumbent interest simultaneously, they may convince powerful groups that survival requires defeating the reform itself. If they accommodate every incumbent, capture remains intact. Successful reform often depends on giving enough credible protection to potential losers that some can afford to accept neutral rules. Amnesty, compensation, gradual transition, protected opposition rights, or credible limits on retrospective punishment can be morally frustrating, yet they may reduce the incentive for preemptive sabotage.
The point is not that elites deserve special indulgence. It is that institutions become stable when powerful actors discover they can lose particular contests without losing everything. The same principle that protects minorities from majorities can protect former majorities from revenge. Restraint becomes sustainable when it is reciprocal across time.
5.3 When tactical corruption becomes regime logic
The strongest defense of institutional capture is local and strategic: I must protect myself because others may abuse the system. The weakness appears when many actors adopt the same rule for long enough. What is rational for one faction can transform the environment in ways that make everyone less secure.
Begin with justice. A business group buys influence over courts to protect itself from predatory officials. Competitors respond by seeking their own influence. Judges learn that career advancement depends on patrons. Litigants begin to interpret every decision politically. Because neutral adjudication becomes less credible, firms invest more in relationships and less in contracts. Foreign partners demand higher risk premiums or external arbitration. Domestic entrepreneurs without political connections avoid sectors where disputes are likely. The group that captured justice for security has helped create a society in which security depends even more heavily on capture.
The same dynamic affects administration. If senior positions are distributed primarily through loyalty, professional competence becomes less valuable as a career strategy. Skilled officials leave or adapt. Information reaching leaders becomes filtered because subordinates learn that politically inconvenient facts are dangerous. Procurement favors loyal firms, which weakens competitive pressure to perform. Poor performance then requires more political intervention, because ordinary administrative mechanisms no longer work reliably.
The captured state gradually loses the distinction between governing capacity and factional capacity. It may remain extremely powerful against isolated citizens while becoming weak at tasks that require honest information and coordinated expertise. It can punish a critic but fail to maintain infrastructure. It can direct contracts but not ensure construction quality. It can control television yet misunderstand public anger until it becomes explosive. It can compel formal obedience while losing the trust required for voluntary cooperation.
This is the first glimpse of a paradox that will dominate the final chapter: power can consume the conditions that make power useful.
For now, however, the realist can still reply that elites may prefer a weaker country they control to a stronger country in which they are exposed. That preference can be rational. A ruler may willingly reduce national wealth if doing so prevents an independent business class from becoming a political rival. A party may prefer a less competent bureaucracy staffed by loyalists because competent independent officials could resist illegal orders. A monopolist may prefer slower innovation if innovation would create competitors. Political actors do not necessarily maximize social welfare. They maximize objectives shaped by survival, status, wealth, ideology, and fear.
This is why corruption persists even when everyone knows it is costly. The social loss is dispersed. The protective benefit of capture is concentrated. Reform asks actors to surrender private insurance in exchange for a public system whose neutrality will be tested only later. If trust is low, the bargain is difficult.
A serious anti-corruption strategy therefore cannot consist only of punishment. It must change the expectation that neutral institutions are dangerous to anyone not currently controlling them. That requires credible rules of succession, property protection that survives political turnover, independent enforcement, transparent procedures, and enough countervailing power that no victorious faction can easily expropriate the losers. In other words, fighting corruption eventually requires the same thing that rights require: institutions capable of making restraint rational.
But the hard case remains. Suppose reform has not yet succeeded. Suppose rivals really will use institutions against you. Suppose the public is fragmented, courts are weak, and politics is winner-take-all. Is it rational for an elite to behave morally and lose?
The answer may be no.
That is what makes the problem serious.
The fifth contrarian thesis is not that corruption is good. It is that under certain institutional conditions corruption is an individually rational response to political insecurity. Condemning the response without changing the conditions is intellectually shallow. Yet rationality at the level of the actor does not guarantee survival at the level of the system. The distinction will matter later, when the argument turns against itself.
The transition from tactical to systemic corruption can also be recognized by what competent people learn to optimize. In a healthy bureaucracy, advancement depends imperfectly but substantially on solving institutional problems. In a captured one, advancement depends increasingly on anticipating the needs of patrons. The most intelligent officials do not disappear; they redirect intelligence toward survival inside the network. Administrative sophistication can therefore coexist with declining public capacity.
This helps explain why captured states can perform spectacular projects while failing at routine governance. Politically salient tasks receive concentrated resources and direct supervision. Ordinary systems such as maintenance, local courts, procurement quality, or public-health records receive less attention because they do not generate immediate political rewards. The regime looks strong in televised moments and weak in distributed functions.
Systemic corruption should thus be understood as an alternative operating system, not merely a collection of illegal transactions. It has recruitment rules, information channels, sanctions, career paths, and norms of loyalty. Reform that removes a few corrupt individuals without replacing those structures will reproduce similar behavior under new names.
Chapter 6 — The Unorganized Have Appeals, Not Rights
6.1 Outrage is not organization
A society can contain millions of people who dislike an outcome and still fail to change it. This fact is endlessly rediscovered because democratic language encourages a simple picture: citizens possess preferences, elections count preferences, and institutions translate the majority will into policy. In reality, political systems process organized preferences much more efficiently than unorganized ones.
Outrage is psychologically intense but structurally weak. A scandal can produce millions of angry comments, a week of protests, and a dramatic fall in trust. If anger does not become membership, funding, expertise, leadership, legal strategy, electoral work, monitoring, or durable institutions, the system can wait for attention to move elsewhere. The powerful often possess the opposite temporal structure. They do not need to become outraged because they already employ people whose job is to remain interested after the public becomes bored.
Consider labor rights. The historical achievement of collective bargaining did not arise from workers independently feeling exploited. Exploitation can produce resignation as easily as organization. What changed bargaining power was the construction of unions, strike funds, meeting places, newspapers, legal strategies, political parties, mutual-aid societies, and cultures of solidarity strong enough to prevent employers from isolating workers one by one. The right to organize became effective because organization already existed strongly enough to demand legal recognition.
The same is true of civil-rights movements. Famous speeches become historical symbols because they articulate moral claims with extraordinary force, but speeches did not integrate schools, register voters, finance litigation, protect organizers, coordinate boycotts, or negotiate legislation by themselves. Churches, local associations, legal organizations, student groups, donors, journalists, lawyers, and national political coalitions turned moral clarity into persistent pressure. The moral claim was essential. The organization gave it duration.
This distinction becomes easy to forget once institutions mature. A worker born into a society with workplace-safety law may experience safety as a service provided by the state. A citizen inheriting universal suffrage may experience voting as an individual possession. A consumer protected by competition law may never meet the lawyers and regulators maintaining the framework. The social labor disappears behind the right.
The hard realist therefore says something that sounds cruel: an unorganized group often has appeals rather than enforceable power. It can ask institutions controlled by others to behave benevolently. Sometimes they do. A decent society should cultivate exactly such responsiveness. But if the group has no capacity to impose political, economic, legal, reputational, or electoral costs when ignored, its protection depends more heavily on the virtue of actors outside the group.
That dependence is especially dangerous for minorities whose interests are easy to sacrifice. Majority support can be broad but shallow. A small organized opponent may care enough to dominate the policy process. This is not a defect unique to democracy. It is a general feature of collective action.

Authoritarian systems may tolerate private dissatisfaction while restricting independent unions, associations, media, universities, religious networks, and foreign funding. The ruler understands something important: isolated anger is manageable; coordinated anger can become an alternative center of power.
Digital media intensify the confusion between expression and power. It has never been easier to display anger, discover that others share it, or make a scandal visible. These capacities matter. They can accelerate organization and expose abuses once hidden. But platforms also reward rapid emotional cycles, making it easy to mistake synchronized attention for durable capacity. A million reposts can vanish before a committee has finished drafting a rule.
The organizations on the other side of a dispute often operate on a different clock. A trade association maintains staff for years. A ministry retains files across news cycles. A corporation hires counsel who follow a regulation from early consultation to final implementation. Durable power lives in calendars that outlast outrage.
Movements succeed when they learn to bridge the clocks: using moments of attention to recruit members, collect resources, build expertise, file cases, elect representatives, or create institutions that remain after the hashtag disappears. The transition from crowd to organization is difficult precisely because organization requires compromises that expressive politics can avoid. Somebody must keep records, allocate money, decide strategy, and accept accountability. Power begins where spontaneity becomes structure.
6.2 The asymmetry of organized minorities
Political power is not proportional to population size. A small group can dominate a large one when its members have intense interests, strong coordination, information, money, institutional access, and low internal transaction costs. A majority can lose repeatedly because each individual has little incentive to invest in a contest whose benefit would be shared with millions of others.
This asymmetry explains outcomes that otherwise look like democratic mysteries. A narrow industry may obtain a favorable regulation that costs each citizen only a small amount. The industry has reason to hire experts, lobby officials, finance campaigns, and monitor technical rulemaking. The ordinary citizen would spend far more time understanding the issue than the individual cost justifies. Rational inattention aggregates into political weakness.
The same mechanism can be benign. A small civil-liberties organization may protect rights that most citizens value weakly until they personally need them. An environmental group may monitor a technical permitting process the general public cannot follow. A professional association can provide expertise that improves policy. Organization is a technology; its moral value depends on the purpose for which it is used.
But organized minorities have a structural advantage over diffuse majorities because coordination itself is power. This advantage becomes stronger in complex states where decisive decisions are made far from elections: administrative rules, procurement standards, licensing criteria, zoning plans, technical committees, judicial appointments, and budget details. The public cannot monitor everything. Actors whose livelihoods depend on a narrow issue can.
The democratic fantasy is that legitimacy automatically corrects this imbalance. If ninety percent of citizens would oppose a policy if fully informed, surely the system will eventually reflect that preference. Sometimes it does. But there is no automatic mechanism. Somebody must make the issue visible, translate it into understandable stakes, build a coalition, maintain attention, and convert broad sympathy into institutional pressure.
This is why the question
“does a group deserve protection?” and the question “can a group sustain protection?” must be kept separate.
Moral entitlement does not depend on organizational competence. A persecuted minority does not become less deserving because it is fragmented, poor, traumatized, or afraid. To suggest otherwise would convert vulnerability into moral guilt.
Politically, however, dependence on external protection remains risky. A group unable to organize may survive because a constitution, professional judiciary, majority norm, foreign ally, church, trade union, or humanitarian institution protects it. Those arrangements can be admirable. Yet if every defender disappears simultaneously, the moral claim alone does not stop an aggressor.
The hard lesson is therefore not
“victims should have fought harder.”
That sentence is often both cruel and false. The lesson is that a decent order should build organizational capacity before crisis, especially for groups that would be weakest during crisis. Freedom of association, access to education, independent media, legal aid, transparent funding, and protected political participation are not decorative liberal values. They are infrastructures through which vulnerability can be converted into counterpower without requiring violence.
The strongest societies do not force every person to become a warrior. They create systems in which people can pool small amounts of effort into institutions capable of defending them collectively. That is what organized freedom looks like when it becomes ordinary.
The advantage of organized minorities is amplified by expertise. Modern policy often turns on technical details incomprehensible to nonspecialists. A single phrase in a tax rule, telecommunications standard, drug-reimbursement formula, zoning code, or procurement specification can redistribute millions. Those most affected have both the incentive and resources to understand the details. The public often arrives only after the distribution has been encoded in technical language.
Transparency helps but does not solve the problem automatically. Publishing ten thousand pages can be a form of opacity if only professionals have time to read them. Democratic counterpower therefore needs intermediary institutions able to translate complexity: investigative journalism, public-interest law, independent research institutes, consumer organizations, unions, professional whistleblowers, and opposition staffs. Without intermediaries, formal openness can coexist with practical invisibility.
This is another reason a healthy democracy contains many organizations that appear unelected. Their legitimacy does not come from replacing elections but from making information and coordination available between elections. The alternative is not pure popular rule. It is often a system in which only concentrated private interests can afford continuous expertise.
6.3 The cost of self-government
Self-government has costs that prosperous democracies often hide. Elections must be administered. Courts must be funded. Laws must be read, challenged, and revised. Citizens must spend time understanding public questions that do not produce immediate personal benefit. Journalists must investigate subjects that powerful people prefer to keep obscure. Associations must collect dues. Opposition parties must recruit candidates. Professionals must sometimes defend institutional rules against the faction they personally support.
These activities consume money, time, attention, reputation, and occasionally safety. When they work, the resulting society feels effortless precisely because the maintenance labor is distributed across many actors. When citizens begin to treat that labor as someone else’s responsibility, the institutions can persist for a while on inherited capital. Then gaps appear.
The uncomfortable question is whether a group can reasonably expect indefinitely to retain protections it is unwilling to help sustain. The moral answer is easy: people do not lose basic rights because they are politically lazy. A child, a disabled person, an exhausted worker, or an ordinary citizen with no taste for activism does not need to earn the right not to be abused. Rights worthy of the name protect people precisely when they cannot protect themselves.
The political answer is different. Institutions do not reproduce themselves through moral entitlement. Somebody must bear the costs. If nearly everyone refuses, those costs concentrate among a shrinking minority of judges, activists, journalists, civil servants, donors, soldiers, voters, and volunteers. Eventually the defenders burn out, become professionalized and socially isolated, or discover that more disciplined groups can capture the same institutions.
This is the distinction between deserving protection and sustaining a protective order. The first is a moral principle. The second is a collective-action problem.
Risk makes the problem sharper. Rights often survive because somebody is willing to use them before using them is perfectly safe. The first employee who challenges an abusive employer may face retaliation. The first official who exposes corruption may lose a career. The first newspaper that publishes a censored fact may be sued. The first citizens who organize against an emerging authoritarian movement may be mocked for overreacting. If everyone waits until resistance is costless, resistance begins only after the system has already made it expensive.
This is not an invitation to romantic martyrdom. Political systems that depend constantly on heroic sacrifice are badly designed. The objective is to distribute risk so widely that ordinary courage is enough. Whistleblower protections, union funds, legal defense organizations, insurance, anonymous reporting, tenure, judicial independence, opposition parties, diversified media ownership, and international networks all reduce the personal cost of saying no.
The cruelty of capability is that history does not guarantee protection to the morally deserving. Small, disciplined, and ruthless organizations have repeatedly defeated larger populations that were divided, surprised, or unwilling to coordinate. A society can correctly believe in justice and still lose control of the institutions through which justice is made effective.
The sixth contrarian thesis is therefore a warning rather than a moral verdict. The unorganized may possess valid rights, but their ability to make those rights durable depends on organizations somewhere in the system. If citizens want freedom without the burdens of permanent mobilization, they must build institutions that store past mobilization as routine capacity. Courts, constitutions, professional norms, associations, and decentralized centers of power are precisely such storage devices.
The goal is not to live forever in struggle. It is to remember that peace is an achievement of organized arrangements, not evidence that organization is no longer necessary.
The burden can be reduced through design. Automatic voter registration, transparent administrative data, simplified procedures, publicly funded information, collective legal actions, ombuds institutions, and default protections can turn constant individual vigilance into occasional participation. Good institutions make freedom cheaper to maintain by storing expertise and spreading risk.
But no design eliminates the need for citizens to care at all. Every watchdog can be captured. Every professional norm can decay. Every organization develops its own interests. Periodic public attention is necessary to renew mandates and replace guardians who have become owners. The problem is similar to maintaining a complex technical system: automation reduces routine labor, but somebody must still notice when the automation itself is failing.
Self-government is therefore neither permanent mobilization nor passive consumption. It is a division of political labor in which citizens delegate extensively while retaining enough organized capacity to revoke, challenge, and redesign the delegation. A population that delegates everything permanently has not eliminated politics. It has selected new rulers.
Chapter 7 — Vigilance Has a Budget
7.1 Liberty is a maintained system
The word vigilance evokes a citizen staring suspiciously at the government, searching for signs of tyranny. That image is too narrow and often unhealthy. Democratic vigilance is less a mood than a maintenance system. It includes accountants who notice unusual procurement, judges who insist on procedure, journalists who compare official statements with records, researchers who test claims, opposition parties that monitor appointments, civil servants who preserve archives, professional associations that defend standards, and citizens who occasionally devote attention to matters that would be easier to ignore.
Liberty depends on mundane capacities. A freedom-of-information law without staff to process requests becomes symbolic. An anti-corruption agency without investigators becomes a logo. A court with a five-year backlog can transform formal rights into delayed favors. An election commission without technical competence can create distrust even when nobody intends fraud. A newspaper that cannot pay reporters can remain legally free while becoming incapable of investigation.
This is why vigilance has a budget. Institutions that constrain power require money, expertise, infrastructure, and continuity. The cost is visible; the catastrophe prevented by the spending is not. Successful prevention therefore contains a political trap. When an institution works for decades, citizens begin to ask why it is necessary.
The same logic operates in engineering. A bridge that does not collapse appears to require less maintenance than engineers claim. A cybersecurity team that prevents incidents can look expensive because executives see salaries rather than the attack that failed. Public institutions face the same problem. The auditor who detects nothing may be wasting money, or may be the reason people avoid detectable fraud. Causal success often resembles absence.
This makes protective institutions easy targets during periods of fiscal or political pressure. Their budgets can be cut without immediate visible consequences. Staff retire and are not replaced. Expertise migrates. Oversight becomes procedural rather than substantive. Years later, when failure becomes obvious, rebuilding capacity is slower than destroying it was.
Vigilance is also distributed across institutions that may irritate one another. Independent media can be unfair. Courts can be slow. regulators can overreach. opposition parties can obstruct. universities can become ideological. civil-society groups can exaggerate. The fact that a check on power is imperfect does not make concentrated power safer. A plural order accepts some friction because friction is
part of the mechanism that prevents any one actor from defining reality without
challenge.
The hard realist therefore evaluates proposals for efficiency with suspicion. Streamlining can be valuable, but every removed veto, shortened review, consolidated agency, emergency procedure, or centralized database changes the cost of future abuse. Efficiency is not politically neutral. A system optimized for a benevolent administrator may be dangerously efficient for a predatory one.
The correct question is not whether oversight slows action. It does. The question is which delays are the price of making irreversible mistakes harder.
Maintenance also has a temporal asymmetry. Cutting institutional capacity can be fast; rebuilding it is slow. Experienced investigators cannot be hired overnight. Judicial culture is not restored by passing a statute. Trust in statistics returns only after repeated demonstrations of independence. A newspaper closed during a period of political pressure may not reappear when pressure ends because readers, advertisers, and staff have dispersed.
This asymmetry makes early defense rational even when the probability of capture is uncertain. Waiting for definitive proof can mean waiting until the capacity needed to respond has already been weakened. The problem resembles fire protection: it would be absurd to build a fire brigade only after flames are visible in every district.
Yet maintenance must remain proportional. Institutions can defend their budgets by inventing permanent emergencies. Security agencies can invoke threats to avoid scrutiny. Every bureaucracy can claim that cuts would produce catastrophe. Vigilance therefore applies to the defenders of vigilance as well. The budget of liberty is justified not by sacred status but by transparent evidence that institutions continue performing functions that citizens can evaluate.
Maintenance is also a question of institutional ecology. An oversight body may be formally independent yet ineffective if prosecutors ignore its referrals, courts delay its cases, or media cannot explain its findings to the public. Capacity is relational. The strength of one guardian depends on the surrounding network of institutions willing to recognize and act on its work.
This is why isolated institutional reform often disappoints. Creating a new agency is easier than creating the expectations, career incentives, budgets, data access, and judicial cooperation through which the agency becomes consequential. Liberty is maintained by systems of systems.
7.2 Attention, dependency, and the material base of dissent
Vigilance is often discussed as if citizens merely need to pay more attention. Attention matters, but people cannot continuously monitor every institution. Modern states are too complex, and ordinary lives contain work, family, health, and private obligations. A sustainable system therefore converts diffuse concern into specialized monitoring. Journalists monitor ministries. regulators monitor industries. auditors monitor accounts. opposition legislators monitor executives. unions monitor employers. professional bodies monitor peers. Citizens monitor the monitors intermittently.
This layered structure works only if the monitoring institutions possess enough independence to act on what they see. Economic dependence can quietly destroy that independence.
A local newspaper whose revenue depends almost entirely on municipal advertising may technically remain free while becoming cautious about investigating the mayor. A university laboratory dependent on one corporate sponsor may avoid questions that threaten the relationship. An employee with a mortgage, children, and no alternative employer within commuting distance may possess freedom of speech but not much freedom to antagonize management. A nonprofit dependent on a single donor may learn the donor’s preferences without receiving any explicit command.
Dependence is not identical to coercion, but it alters the price of dissent. Political freedom therefore has a material dimension that formal law alone cannot capture. People with savings, portable skills, multiple employers, independent professional status, social networks, or access to public benefits can absorb retaliation better than people whose income, housing, health care, and social identity depend on one institution.
This is why company towns have long been politically significant. When the same firm controls employment, housing, stores, local officials, and social status, a nominally private employment relationship acquires quasi-governmental power. The worker can “leave,” but exit may require leaving the entire community. Similar concentrations can emerge in digital platforms, universities, military towns, migrant-labor systems, or highly specialized industries even when legal structures differ.

This is the logic of counterpower. The citizen does not become powerful enough to defeat every institution personally. Instead, society creates overlapping structures that prevent any single relationship from becoming total. The worker can appeal to a regulator, union, court, journalist, or competing employer. The journalist can rely on multiple revenue sources. The firm can challenge an administrative decision. The opposition can investigate through parliament. The judge can rely on tenure and professional norms.
When these alternatives disappear simultaneously, formal freedom becomes thin. A person who must obey the same network to keep a job, obtain credit, access media, receive public contracts, and resolve legal disputes may still possess rights on paper. Effective exit is disappearing.
Vigilance therefore requires a budget not only for watchdogs but for social structures that make disagreement survivable. A democracy whose citizens are economically terrified can remain electorally competitive while becoming politically timid. Fear narrows the set of opinions people are willing to act upon.
Economic pluralism is thus politically significant for reasons broader than efficiency. A society with many independent employers, funding sources, media organizations, universities, banks, and associations gives individuals more places to go after conflict. Competition creates exit options. Exit does not replace voice, but it changes the cost of voice because people are less completely owned by any single relationship.
This is why concentration can be politically dangerous even when consumer prices remain low. A dominant platform may provide excellent services while controlling access to audiences, livelihoods, or information. A dominant employer may pay well while making regional dissent risky. A dominant donor can professionalize a field while narrowing intellectual diversity. The problem is not simply monopoly profit. It is the conversion of economic dependence into political deference.
Conversely, decentralization has costs. Fragmented systems can duplicate effort, protect local abuses, and reduce capacity for large-scale coordination. The objective is not maximal fragmentation but enough independent nodes that disagreement remains survivable. Counterpower needs redundancy.
The material base of dissent also includes time. People working multiple insecure jobs have less capacity to attend meetings, study legislation, volunteer for campaigns, or pursue complaints. Political equality can therefore coexist with radical inequality in the disposable time required to exercise citizenship. Wealth buys not only influence but attention.
Institutions that lower participation costs are consequently forms of counterpower. Paid civic leave, accessible voting, collective representation, public-interest litigation, and simple complaint mechanisms do not make citizens more virtuous. They make the exercise of rights less dependent on private reserves of money and time.
7.3 Between complacency and paranoia
The argument for vigilance can easily become pathological. Once every institution is viewed as a potential instrument of domination, every compromise looks like betrayal, every administrative mistake like conspiracy, and every opponent like an aspiring tyrant. A society permanently mobilized against imagined capture can destroy trust faster than actual corruption would.
This creates a difficult calibration problem. Complacency allows genuine concentrations of power to grow unnoticed. Paranoia interprets normal disagreement as evidence of hidden control. Both weaken institutions. The complacent citizen withdraws from maintenance; the paranoid citizen delegitimizes every mechanism that produces an unwelcome outcome.
The distinction depends partly on evidence and partly on institutional behavior. Healthy vigilance asks whether decisions are reviewable, records available, conflicts of interest disclosed, rules applied consistently, leadership replaceable, and critics able to organize. Paranoia begins with the conclusion that all visible procedures are masks and treats disconfirming evidence as proof of deeper deception. Because no institution can prove an absolute absence of hidden power, the paranoid theory becomes unfalsifiable.
This matters because authoritarian movements frequently borrow the language of vigilance. They claim that courts, media, universities, bureaucracies, elections, and professional bodies are all captured by a single enemy. The proposed solution is then to concentrate power in a leader who promises to cleanse the system. The argument begins with justified suspicion of institutions and ends by removing the pluralism that made suspicion politically safe.
The opposite failure is the generation that inherits peace. People born after major institutional conflicts often experience checks and balances as inefficient remnants of old anxieties. Why should judges block popular policy? Why should opposition parties receive procedural rights? Why should governments tolerate hostile media? Why maintain institutions designed to restrain abuses nobody remembers personally?
Peace becomes evidence that defense is unnecessary.
This is one of civilization’s recurrent tragedies. The success of an institution erases the memory of the problem that created it. Vaccination reduces disease until people fear vaccines more than disease. Flood defenses prevent floods until maintenance appears wasteful. Strategic deterrence can make major conflict seem implausible. Constitutional constraints make political violence rare enough that constraints look excessive.
The seventh contrarian thesis is therefore not
“trust nobody.”
It is more precise: liberty survives when distrust is institutionalized in limited, evidence-sensitive, reciprocal forms. Audit the government, but audit the auditor. Give regulators power, but make their decisions reviewable. Protect journalists, but allow criticism of journalism. Fund intelligence agencies, but subject surveillance to law. Preserve opposition, but require the opposition to accept electoral defeat. The objective is not universal suspicion. It is to prevent any actor from becoming the sole judge of its own conduct.
A free society should feel less dramatic than the struggle that created it. That is the point. But its calm should be produced by maintained counterpowers, not by the belief that power has finally become irrelevant.
A useful culture of vigilance therefore values falsifiability. Claims of capture should generate predictions that can be tested: decisions systematically favor connected actors; oversight budgets decline after investigations; appointment patterns change; retaliation follows criticism; exceptions cluster around a network. Evidence may remain incomplete, but the theory must be capable of being wrong.
This standard protects vigilance from becoming conspiracy. It also protects institutions from the opposite trick, in which every concern is dismissed because no single document says
“we are capturing the state.”
Real capture is rarely recorded that conveniently. Patterns matter. The discipline is to infer from patterns without making the inference immune to revision.
Healthy vigilance is emotionally less satisfying than both trust and paranoia. It asks citizens to maintain institutions they sometimes dislike, investigate allies as well as enemies, and tolerate uncertainty after serious warning signs appear. This is precisely why it must become a norm rather than depend on moments of fear.
Institutions can help with calibration by publishing indicators that make power legible over time: case backlogs, ownership concentration, procurement competition, appointment patterns, access to information, enforcement disparities, and retaliation complaints. No single indicator proves capture. Longitudinal evidence makes it harder for both complacency and paranoia to rely entirely on impression.
A culture capable of revising its own suspicions is stronger than one that alternates between blind trust and total distrust. The objective is not confidence in rulers. It is confidence that rulers can be checked without destroying the system.
Chapter 8 — Freedom Forgets What Built It
8.1 The moral inheritance problem
Political orders are inherited unevenly. One generation may experience censorship, violence, unemployment, racial exclusion, arbitrary administration, war, or dictatorship directly. It builds institutions in response. The next generation encounters the institutions rather than the original danger. The third may encounter mainly the inconveniences of the institutions. What began as a defense against a remembered threat becomes a set of procedures whose purpose is increasingly abstract.
This is the moral inheritance problem. People inherit protections without inheriting the emotional knowledge of what made those protections necessary.
The phenomenon is not evidence that younger generations are frivolous. It is a predictable consequence of successful institutions. A person who has never lived under arbitrary arrest cannot be expected to feel due process as viscerally as someone who has. A citizen who has never seen an election canceled may treat procedural complaints as technicalities. A worker who has never seen industrial accidents routinely hidden may experience safety regulation mainly as paperwork. Success changes the experiential evidence available to later citizens.
This creates a strange politics in which the protections built by the strong can come to look like the preferences of the weak. Due process protects the accused, so it appears to favor criminals over decisive government. Free speech protects unpopular speakers, so it appears to privilege offensive minorities over social cohesion. asylum procedures protect people with uncertain claims, so they appear inefficient beside summary exclusion. Judicial review protects losers from majorities, so it can seem anti-democratic. The visible beneficiary is often the person currently resisting power; the invisible beneficiary is everyone who might one day need the same restraint.
The aesthetics of power amplify the misunderstanding. Decisive action looks competent. A leader who “gets things done” appears strong. Institutions that ask for reasons, hearings, evidence, appeals, consultations, and conflict-of-interest rules look hesitant. The symbolic contrast is unfair because constraint is supposed to be slower than command. Yet political cultures repeatedly romanticize the speed of concentrated power.
Inherited freedom can therefore produce impatience with the very friction that preserved it. The citizen asks why institutions do not simply implement the obvious good. The answer is that history contains many actors who considered their preferred good obvious. Procedures exist because certainty about ends does not justify unlimited means.
There is also a social inheritance problem. Public virtues can decay when the incentives that produced them disappear. A professional bureaucracy built after a corruption scandal may preserve standards for decades. New entrants then encounter the standards as arbitrary rituals rather than as answers to known failures. If enough of the rituals are relaxed, the old opportunities for patronage reappear, but the historical memory that would recognize the pattern may be gone.
This is why institutional memory matters. Archives, education, commemorations, historical scholarship, professional training, and even boring case law store information about failure. They remind later generations that rules are not random obstacles. Some are fossils of past catastrophes.
The hard realist gives this insight a sharper edge. Freedom is not only inherited morality. It is inherited power. Later generations receive courts others made independent, associations others financed, voting rights others fought to expand, and norms others made socially expensive to violate. If those powers are not renewed, inheritance becomes consumption.
A civilization can spend institutional capital just as a family can spend financial capital. The early years may feel prosperous because inherited assets remain. By the time the loss becomes visible, rebuilding may require costs the society has forgotten how to bear.
Education about institutional inheritance often fails because it teaches reverence rather than causation. Students learn that constitutions are important, courts independent, and elections sacred. The language can become civic catechism. A stronger education would ask what problem each institution was built to solve, which actors resisted it, how it failed in past cases, and what incentives might undermine it again.
Understanding causation makes institutions easier to criticize intelligently. A citizen who knows why judicial independence matters can still argue that judges are unaccountable. A citizen who understands why emergency powers are dangerous can still support temporary emergency measures under defined conditions. Institutional memory should not produce worship. It should preserve the design problem.
This matters because inherited systems must evolve. Defending every old rule simply because it once constrained power can fossilize injustice. Some safeguards protect obsolete elites. Some professional norms exclude new groups. The task is to distinguish a load-bearing constraint from historical clutter. That distinction requires understanding the failure mode the constraint addresses rather than merely repeating tradition.
8.2 Tolerance without surrender
Pluralistic societies depend on tolerance, but tolerance is easily confused with passivity. To tolerate a belief is not to agree with it. To tolerate an organization is not to grant it unlimited freedom to destroy the conditions of tolerance. A society that cannot distinguish coexistence from surrender eventually forces every disagreement into one of two bad categories: suppress the opponent or capitulate to him.
The problem becomes acute when groups possess incompatible visions of social order. A liberal system may protect religious communities that reject liberal moral assumptions internally. A secular society may tolerate movements that seek stronger religious law. A market democracy may allow parties that advocate nationalization. A socialist movement may organize legally within a capitalist economy. The whole point of pluralism is that people need not share final values in order to share procedures.
But procedures are not self-defending. A faction can use freedoms instrumentally while intending to abolish them once power is obtained. Democracies have faced this problem repeatedly: how much organizational freedom should be extended to movements that reject alternation in power, equal citizenship, or the right of opponents to organize?

This does not mean every social conflict should be juridified or securitized. Overdefending pluralism can create the very authoritarianism it seeks to prevent. The purpose of the architecture is to make ordinary disagreement safe enough that extraordinary coercion remains exceptional.
The paradox is that a tolerant order must preserve the capacity to be intolerant of attempts to abolish tolerance while remaining deeply suspicious of anyone who claims that such an attempt is underway. This is not elegant. Mature politics rarely is.
There is no purely procedural answer because any line can be abused by incumbents to suppress legitimate opposition. Ban anti-democratic parties too broadly and government defines dissent as extremism. Tolerate every strategy until violence begins and a disciplined movement may capture institutions before the legal threshold is crossed. Liberalism cannot escape judgment by pretending procedure alone decides.
The hard realist proposes a principle of reciprocal danger. A plural order should remain tolerant enough that opponents can seek change, but strong enough that no faction can cheaply transform temporary victory into permanent exclusion. This implies protecting the opposition, decentralizing power, preserving independent courts, maintaining professional security services, and making constitutional change difficult enough that losing one election does not mean losing the political future.
Tolerance then becomes a confidence produced by counterpower. One can allow opponents to speak because speech does not give them ownership of the police. One can permit radical parties because courts and constitutional rules remain independent. One can accept an election loss because another election is expected. Pluralism is easiest when no single contest is existential.
This is why winner-take-all systems create incentives for intolerance even among actors who prefer moderation. If losing office means losing property, prosecution, media access, or physical safety, political competition becomes a struggle for survival. Each faction has reason to prevent the other from winning. Constitutionalism reduces the stakes of losing by protecting a minimum domain that the winner cannot easily invade.
The apparent softness of tolerance therefore rests on hard architecture. A society can afford to be generous because power is fragmented. When fragmentation disappears, tolerance becomes a plea addressed to the victor.
Reciprocity also clarifies a common misunderstanding about liberal neutrality. A liberal state cannot be neutral toward whether political competition continues to exist. It may remain neutral among religions, lifestyles, and many economic doctrines, but it must privilege procedures that preserve the possibility of future disagreement. Otherwise neutrality abolishes itself.
The difficult cases lie near the boundary. A movement may use harsh rhetoric without possessing a realistic plan to end democracy. Another may speak moderately while systematically capturing election administration. Focusing only on words can miss institutional behavior; focusing only on secret intentions invites paranoia. The best evidence is what actors do with partial power. Do they protect rules that constrain them? Do they accept adverse judgments? Do they permit rivals to organize where they govern?
Tolerance becomes sustainable when it is based less on trust in intentions and more on observable commitment to reciprocal constraints. A radical program can remain part of democratic competition if its proponents accept that opponents retain the right to reverse it through lawful means.
8.3 When peace erases the memory of defense
The relationship between peace and preparedness is structurally unstable. If defenses work, the threat may remain invisible. If the threat remains invisible long enough, defenses look unnecessary. If defenses are dismantled, the threat can return under conditions in which rebuilding is expensive.
The pattern appears in military security, public health, cybersecurity, banking regulation, disaster preparedness, and democratic institutions. Each domain contains expensive capacities whose best outcome is that nothing happens. The absence of catastrophe is then politically reinterpreted as evidence that the capacity was excessive.
Freedom is subject to the same cycle. A stable democracy produces generations for whom coups, party bans, censorship, electoral fraud, and political imprisonment feel distant. That distance is a triumph. It can also lower sensitivity to early warning signs because citizens compare every new abuse with the worst historical case. If the current government is not a dictatorship, concern appears hysterical. If elections still occur, democracy is assumed healthy. If some independent media survive, press freedom is declared intact.
Institutional decline benefits from this binary thinking. Rights rarely move directly from full protection to abolition. They become less equal, more expensive, more selective, and more dependent on loyalty. The early stages look too small to justify alarm. By the time the pattern is undeniable, actors who might have opposed it have adapted individually to the new incentives.
This is the politics of normalization. Each concession is tolerable in isolation. A prosecutor is replaced. An oversight body loses budget. A broadcaster changes ownership. A procurement rule acquires an exception. A court appointment procedure is “streamlined.” A civil servant learns that one investigation damages careers. None alone proves systemic capture. Together they can change the equilibrium.
The hard realist’s eighth thesis is that liberty often fails through forgetting rather than explicit rejection. People do not vote against freedom in the abstract. They trade pieces of its infrastructure for efficiency, security, partisan advantage, lower costs, emotional satisfaction, or simply relief from political attention. The transaction seems reasonable because the lost capacity was not being visibly used.
There is no requirement that citizens live in permanent fear. Indeed, fear is itself a political resource easily exploited by would-be authoritarians. The objective is memory without panic: institutions that preserve knowledge of failure even when ordinary life remains calm.
This can be achieved structurally. Sunset clauses force emergency powers to be renewed rather than become permanent. Independent audits create routine skepticism. Federalism and local autonomy preserve alternative centers of competence. Professional associations defend standards across electoral cycles. Civic education can explain not merely constitutional ideals but the conflicts and failures that made particular safeguards necessary.
A mature society should know why its freedoms are inconvenient.
The strongest argument for vigilance is not that enemies are everywhere. It is that human incentives have not fundamentally changed simply because current institutions are successful. Ambitious actors still prefer favorable rules. Organizations still protect themselves. majorities still become impatient with minorities. officials still dislike scrutiny. citizens still prefer benefits whose costs are borne by someone else. The architecture of freedom was built around these ordinary facts, not around the assumption that everyone would become virtuous.
Peace is therefore not the end of political defense. It is what successful defense feels like from inside.
The memory problem also affects external security. Long periods without major war can make military capacity look archaic; repeated conflict can make military institutions dominate civilian life. The challenge is to preserve credible defense without allowing the defender to become an autonomous political power. Democracies developed civilian control precisely because security capacity is necessary and dangerous at the same time.
The same pattern repeats at smaller scales. Cybersecurity teams need privileged access that could itself become a surveillance risk. Public-health authorities may require emergency powers that must later expire. Financial regulators need discretion that can be captured by industry. Every protective function creates a new principal-agent problem.
This recursive structure is not an argument against protection. It is the adult form of the argument for it. We need guards, and we need rules for the guards. We need institutions that remember threats, and institutions that prevent remembered threats from becoming permanent excuses for concentrated power.
Ritual can sometimes preserve memory when personal experience disappears. Constitutional ceremonies, professional oaths, remembrance days, and public inquiries can seem symbolic, yet symbols help transmit institutional caution across generations. Their value depends on whether they connect memory to present mechanisms rather than merely sanctify the past.
A society that remembers only heroes learns the wrong lesson. It should also remember procedures, funding arrangements, failed warnings, institutional compromises, and the ordinary people who kept systems functioning. The history of freedom is less cinematic when told accurately, but more useful.
Chapter 9 — The Adult Theory of History
9.1 Might writes the first draft
History contains too many defeated moral causes to permit the comforting belief that justice automatically prevails. Peoples have been conquered, languages erased, minorities expelled, reforms crushed, dissidents imprisoned, and institutions destroyed by actors whose principal qualification was superior organization and force. Moral worth does not confer immunity from defeat.
This is the empirical core of the realist position. Might often writes the first draft of political reality.
Borders are frequently the sediment of wars. Property systems have roots in conquest, enclosure, colonization, revolution, and legal transformation. State languages become standard through schools, bureaucracies, and sometimes suppression of alternatives. Religious majorities reflect conversion, migration, demographic change, and past political advantage. Social classes inherit accumulated victories and exclusions. Even peaceful institutions often stand on historical settlements whose coercive origins later become invisible.
The point is not that everything in history reduces to violence. Trade, invention, disease, ecology, technology, demography, ideas, and chance matter enormously. The point is that when incompatible groups contest the same scarce authority, outcomes depend partly on capability. A movement with brilliant ideas but no organization can be defeated by a mediocre movement with discipline, money, and logistics.
The saint needs logistics.
Moral movements succeed when conscience is translated into capacity. Abolitionism required publications, political organizations, religious networks, fundraising, litigation, electoral alliances, and in some places war. Anti-colonial movements required clandestine organization, diplomacy, strikes, armed struggle or the credible threat of disruption, international support, and administrative competence. Women’s suffrage required societies, petitions, campaigning, civil disobedience, lobbying, and sustained organization across decades. Ideas changed people, but organization made the changed people politically consequential.
This is why later historical memory can mislead. The speech survives; the meeting minutes disappear. The famous leader survives; the local organizers are forgotten. The principle survives; the fundraising system does not. History becomes a story in which moral insight changed the world because the infrastructure that made insight effective is less memorable.
The realist correction is not to demean ideals. It is to restore the missing machinery.
Ideas are themselves forms of power when they alter legitimacy, identity, and coordination. A moral narrative can make soldiers refuse orders, consumers boycott firms, citizens accept taxation, or allies bear costs. But an idea becomes historically powerful through transmission networks, institutions, incentives, symbols, and organizations. The slogan that “ideas change the world” is true only after we ask who teaches them, publishes them, funds them, embodies them, and protects the people who act on them.
Might writes the first draft because organized capability determines what can be imposed. It does not necessarily write the final draft because power that cannot acquire legitimacy becomes expensive to maintain.
The phrase “first draft” is important because conquest itself rarely settles meaning. Victors must teach, administer, tax, classify, marry, trade, and govern. Populations reinterpret imposed institutions. Languages mix. legal categories are adapted to local practice. Economic incentives transform the conqueror as well as the conquered. The political order produced by force becomes a field of negotiation almost immediately.
This is one reason empires that appear omnipotent in military history can look surprisingly dependent in administrative history. They rely on local elites, tax intermediaries, translators, merchants, religious authorities, and existing institutions. Power expands through collaboration as much as direct control. The capacity to conquer and the capacity to govern are different achievements.
The realist should therefore avoid a romance of force as carefully as the idealist avoids a romance of principle. Violence can decide who gets the first opportunity to design the order. It does not eliminate the need for organization, information, and legitimacy afterward.
Technology repeatedly changes what kinds of might matter. Fortifications altered medieval warfare; printing changed the scale of religious and political mobilization; railways transformed state logistics; radio created new possibilities for mass persuasion; digital networks lowered the cost of both surveillance and decentralized coordination. A group can lose power not because its moral claim changed but because the infrastructure through which it organized became obsolete.
This technological dimension strengthens rather than weakens the realist argument. Capabilities are historical. A strategy adequate for yesterday’s environment may fail without any change in values. Political autonomy therefore requires continual adaptation in the means by which a group communicates, finances itself, protects information, and coordinates action.
9.2 Coercion, coordination, and legitimacy
The hardest version of political realism becomes crude when it treats power as a single substance measured mainly by weapons. A more useful theory distinguishes at least three currencies: coercion, coordination, and legitimacy.
Coercion is the capacity to impose costs directly. States arrest, armies destroy, employers fire, banks withdraw credit, platforms remove access, families disinherit, and communities ostracize. Coercion changes behavior by altering the price of refusal.
Coordination is the capacity to make many actors move together. A union that can organize a strike may possess little physical force but significant coordination power. A political party can mobilize voters, donors, lawyers, and local officials. A religious network can move information and resources rapidly. A corporation coordinates thousands of employees and suppliers through contracts and management systems. Coordination converts dispersed capability into collective effect.
Legitimacy is the capacity to make an order appear rightful, normal, or at least preferable to available alternatives. It lowers enforcement costs because people comply voluntarily, defend institutions, and interpret challenges as illegitimate. Legitimacy can arise from democracy, tradition, religion, nationalism, competence, fairness, prosperity, law, or habit. It can be manipulated, but it cannot be reduced entirely to propaganda because sustained legitimacy requires enough lived experience to make the narrative plausible.

The adult theory of power therefore asks not simply

A guerrilla movement may outlast a stronger army because it can survive cheaply and draw legitimacy from local grievances. A government may win every battle while losing the political conflict. A corporation may dominate a market until dependence on one technology becomes a vulnerability. A social movement may capture public sympathy but fail to translate it into durable law.
This multidimensional view already contains the seed of the book’s final reversal. An elite that maximizes coercive control by destroying independent institutions may reduce coordination and legitimacy. A ruler who captures every information channel may make it harder to know what is happening. Power can trade one currency for another at terrible exchange rates.
For now, however, the realist can still insist on a harder point: legitimacy without the capacity to resist coercion can be crushed. Coordination without resources can exhaust itself. Moral authority without organization can become memorial culture. Durable orders need enough of all three.
A fourth resource could be added to the table: knowledge. It is separated here not because knowledge is unimportant, but because knowledge often increases the value of the other currencies. Coercion directed by bad information becomes wasteful. Coordination around a false model produces collective failure. Legitimacy built on claims repeatedly contradicted by experience decays.
This is why intelligence services, statistics agencies, accounting systems, scientific institutions, and free inquiry have political importance beyond their immediate functions. They help power perceive the world it is attempting to influence. A regime can centralize information gathering while still corrupting information if analysts learn which conclusions are career-safe.
Power is thus partly epistemic. Actors capable of learning faster can defeat actors with greater initial resources. Institutions that permit correction may outlast institutions that maximize immediate obedience. The final chapter will turn this from a secondary observation into a central objection to unconstrained domination.
The currencies also operate at different speeds. Coercion can change behavior immediately. Coordination usually takes longer to build but can scale quickly once networks exist. Legitimacy accumulates slowly and can collapse suddenly after an event that reveals hypocrisy or incompetence. Strategic actors who optimize only for the next month may therefore spend legitimacy as though it were free because the cost arrives later.
A durable order manages a portfolio across time. It uses coercion sparingly enough that legitimacy is not exhausted, invests in coordination capacity before crisis, and protects information channels that reveal when the balance is deteriorating. This is a more demanding form of realism than simple dominance.
9.3 There is no final victory
The attraction of “might is right” lies partly in its apparent finality. Power decides. The victor rules. The argument ends.
History rarely ends.
Every victory changes the environment in which the victor must continue operating. Conquest creates territories that must be governed. Monopoly attracts competitors and regulators. Political capture creates rivals who now believe capture is necessary for survival. Repression generates information problems. Expropriation teaches wealth to flee. A revolution that abolishes old institutions inherits the administrative tasks those institutions performed. The successful actor becomes responsible for maintaining the system that success has altered.
This is why elites require legitimacy even when they command force. Permanent coercion is expensive. A ruling group prefers taxpayers who pay without soldiers at the door, workers who work without constant surveillance, administrators who solve problems without waiting for commands, and citizens who treat the regime’s continuation as normal. Victory creates a demand for consent.
The reverse is also true. Moral movements that gain office discover that legitimacy is not enough. They must collect taxes, appoint competent administrators, resolve disputes, maintain infrastructure, negotiate with hostile groups, and sometimes use coercion. Movements can fail after winning because governing requires capabilities different from protesting.
There is no moral exemption from administration.
This is the point at which the slogan “might is right” becomes unstable. If it means that successful coercion determines what people must endure in the short run, history repeatedly confirms it. If it means that success creates moral justification, the inference does not follow. If it means that the strongest actor can safely ignore all constraints indefinitely, history offers abundant counterexamples. If it means that rights require supporting power, the claim is difficult to deny.
The adult theory therefore keeps the insight and discards the metaphysics. Might often determines which moral claims acquire institutional reality first. It does not determine which claims are morally valid. More importantly, might is not a static possession. It must be reproduced across time through institutions, knowledge, loyalty, resources, and adaptation.
The realist’s final position before the reversal can now be stated with maximum force. A group that wants its autonomy, culture, property, institutions, or moral vision to survive must possess capabilities compatible with survival. It cannot outsource every burden of defense while assuming the world will respect
abstract entitlement. Elites who fail to secure their position may be replaced by
elites who do. Populations that allow independent institutions to decay may discover that formal rights cannot reconstruct those institutions during crisis. Communities that refuse to organize can be dominated by smaller groups that do.
Right without might is aspiration.
Might without right is predation.
Political order is the unstable attempt to bind them together.
For nine chapters we have emphasized the first sentence because comfortable moral cultures tend to forget it. The next chapter will demonstrate the opposite temptation: a civilized language so careful, balanced, and inclusive that it becomes capable of describing domination without ever naming who dominates.
The absence of final victory also changes how we should think about deterrence. A group does not need enough power to dominate everyone. It may need only enough capacity to make domination costly. Small states survive beside larger ones through alliances, geography, economic integration, international norms, and the expected cost of aggression. Minorities survive politically through constitutional protections, coalition value, organization, external allies, and the reputational price of persecution.
Counterpower is often asymmetrical rather than equal. A whistleblower cannot defeat a ministry but can make concealment risky. A newspaper cannot command a corporation but can raise reputational costs. A court cannot administer the executive but can invalidate an act. These limited capacities matter because political outcomes are shaped by marginal costs as well as total control.
This makes the realist picture less fatalistic. The weak do not need to become the strong in every dimension. They need strategic capacities that prevent stronger actors from treating them as costless objects.
Political success should therefore be evaluated by what it makes possible after the victory. Does a reform create institutions that can survive leaders who disagree with its founders? Does an elite settlement permit new entrants? Does a revolution establish procedures for peaceful succession? Does a military victory create a governable peace? These questions distinguish conquest from institution-building.
The strongest victory may be the one that makes repeated victory unnecessary. When former rivals accept a procedure for sharing or alternating power, they convert resources previously spent on existential defense into ordinary competition. Peace is not the absence of power; it is power organized so that fewer conflicts need to be settled by maximum force.
Chapter 10 — The Civilized Voice
10.1 The seduction of balanced language
There is another way to speak about power. It is smoother, safer, and institutionally attractive. Instead of saying that one group dominates another, we say that stakeholders have different perspectives. Instead of saying that an institution has been captured, we say that trust has declined. Instead of saying that a powerful actor is exploiting a dependency, we say that incentives are misaligned. Instead of asking who can impose costs on whom, we ask how dialogue can improve mutual understanding.
This vocabulary is not foolish. It is often the language required to keep negotiation possible. Diplomats cannot begin every meeting by identifying the most predatory participant. Mediators cannot reduce conflicts to moral indictments if the purpose is to produce an agreement. Researchers hedge because evidence is incomplete. Administrators use neutral categories because institutions must serve citizens who disagree about values. Civilized language lowers emotional temperature.
The danger appears when the method of communication becomes a theory of reality.
Imagine a town in which one employer controls most jobs, finances local political campaigns, owns the principal newspaper, and has close ties to the bank. Workers complain that safety violations are ignored because local officials fear economic retaliation. A perfectly balanced report may state that employees seek stronger enforcement, management emphasizes competitiveness, officials face resource constraints, and community leaders value social stability. Every sentence can be accurate. The report can still conceal the central fact: the parties do not possess equal bargaining power.
The grammar of equal weight is attractive because it avoids accusation. Each side receives a paragraph. Every value gets acknowledged. Conflicts become trade-offs among legitimate concerns. The format is useful when power is sufficiently balanced that participants can actually negotiate. When power is radically asymmetric, symmetry of language can become distortion.
A person with a gun and a person asking not to be shot do not merely hold divergent preferences about safety.
The civilized voice dislikes such sentences because they close interpretive space. Sometimes that caution is wise. Political rhetoric routinely exaggerates asymmetry to justify aggression. Every party claims victimhood. Every government describes opponents as threats. A vocabulary that slows moral escalation can prevent conflict.
Yet excessive balance can also become an anesthetic. By insisting that every description remain mutually acceptable, the language gives powerful actors an informal veto over accurate characterization. Exploitation disappears because exploiters reject the word. Capture becomes
“perceived capture.”
Corruption becomes
“governance challenges.”
Intimidation becomes
“polarization.”
A legal fiction becomes a
“gap between policy and implementation.”
The language remains civilized while the underlying relationship remains unchanged.
The style is especially common in organizations that must preserve broad coalitions. International institutions, universities, foundations, large corporations, and public agencies often depend on actors with incompatible interests. Their language evolves to avoid formulations that would cause one constituency to exit. Euphemism can therefore be an organizational survival strategy.
This does not make the language empty. Terms such as
“stakeholder,”
“challenge,” and “misalignment” can create common ground for people who would reject one another’s moral narratives. The cost is that the vocabulary tends to privilege problems that can be described without assigning responsibility. Structural asymmetry becomes easier to discuss than deliberate exploitation; historical injustice easier than a living beneficiary;
“barriers” easier than actors who maintain barriers.
The reader should therefore listen for grammatical agency. Who is the subject of the sentence? If
“trust declined,” what events made people distrust?
If
“mistakes were made,” who made them?
If
“communities were displaced,” by whom?
Passive grammar is not always deception, but repeated disappearance of agents can reveal an institution trying to discuss power without offending power.
One diagnostic technique is to rewrite a smooth paragraph in causal language without changing the facts.
“Community tensions increased after restructuring” becomes “the restructuring eliminated jobs concentrated in one community, and protests followed.”
The second sentence may still be incomplete, but it exposes actors and sequence. The exercise reveals how much political information is stored in verbs.
Institutions should not abandon diplomatic language, but analysts inside them need spaces where causal descriptions can remain sharper than public communiqués. Otherwise the organization gradually loses the ability to think thoughts it cannot comfortably publish.
10.2 Process as a substitute for judgment
Institutions under moral pressure often respond by creating process. A commission is established. Stakeholders are mapped. Consultations are held. A framework is drafted. Indicators are selected. Another round of consultation validates the framework. The process can be valuable because complex problems genuinely require information, legitimacy, and participation.
Process becomes pathological when it substitutes for judgment.
Suppose an organization receives credible evidence that a senior figure repeatedly abuses subordinates. A careful procedure is necessary because accusations can be false and due process protects everyone. But procedure can also be manipulated to disperse responsibility. Each official waits for another review. Confidentiality prevents patterns from becoming visible. Narrow evidentiary standards treat every incident separately. The accused remains powerful enough to influence careers. Years later, the organization can truthfully say that it followed process while failing to protect people.
This is the moral convenience of procedure: responsibility is transferred from decision-makers to the existence of steps. If the steps were followed, nobody needs to own the outcome.
The same temptation appears in public policy. Consultation can become a way to postpone conflicts whose facts are already clear. Impact assessments can become rituals that document rather than alter a predetermined decision. Ethics committees can create reputational cover for technologies whose business incentives remain untouched. International forums can repeatedly “express concern” because participants cannot agree on sanctions. The presence of procedure creates the feeling that something is being done.
The civilized voice then develops an infinite regress of sensitivity. Before condemning a practice, we must understand every context. Before acting, we must consult every affected group. Before deciding, we must consider every unintended consequence. Each requirement is defensible individually. Together they can produce paralysis in situations where delay benefits the actor already exercising power.
This does not mean speed is morally superior. Rapid moral certainty has produced atrocities. The point is asymmetry: uncertainty is not distributed evenly. The person currently being harmed experiences every month of careful deliberation as another month inside the existing order. The powerful actor often benefits from the burden of proof because the status quo is its preferred outcome.
A smooth institution can therefore become a machine for turning urgent conflicts into administrative time.
Figure 4. How smooth language can dissolve agency.
The figure is intentionally simple. It shows how a concrete asymmetry can pass through layers of neutralization until the final institutional description becomes difficult to disagree with and equally difficult to act upon. The transformation is not necessarily dishonest. Each stage can improve precision. The danger is cumulative loss of agency: by the time everyone accepts the description, nobody is clearly responsible for changing the situation.
The civilized voice is especially seductive to educated people because it rewards complexity. Strong moral language can look primitive beside nuanced qualification. But nuance is not the same as accuracy. Sometimes the most accurate sentence is asymmetrical.
A person with power can impose costs that another person cannot impose in return.
No amount of conceptual sophistication makes the relation balanced.
Process also has a class dimension. People with lawyers, time, language skills, and institutional familiarity can survive long procedures better than people living close to economic or physical crisis. A hearing scheduled six months later is inconvenient for a corporation and potentially catastrophic for a tenant facing eviction. The same formally equal process imposes unequal practical costs.
This is why procedural justice must care about accessibility and time. Due process is not maximized by making every decision infinitely elaborate. Good procedure matches the stakes, protects the vulnerable from arbitrary action, and reaches decisions while decisions still matter. Delay can violate justice as surely as haste.
The smooth institution often measures procedural completeness because it is easier to audit than substantive courage. Forms were filed, meetings held, policies adopted. Whether the procedure altered the underlying power relation is harder to quantify and more politically dangerous to ask. The result can be immaculate governance documentation surrounding unchanged domination.
The cure is not fewer procedures but procedures with decision points. A consultation should specify what can actually change. An ethics review should identify who can stop the project. A complaint system should define timelines and remedies. A commission should have a mandate connected to authority rather than merely to publication. Process becomes meaningful when participants can trace how participation alters outcomes.
This also makes failure visible. If a process ends without action, the institution must say whether evidence was insufficient, legal authority absent, costs judged too high, or powerful interests prevailed. Explicit reasons are politically uncomfortable. That discomfort is part of accountability.
10.3 Why the civilized voice still matters
Having spent most of this chapter attacking smooth language, we must defend it. The civilized voice exists for reasons deeper than cowardice. Societies need forms of speech that allow people who dislike one another to remain inside the same institutions. Negotiation requires a vocabulary in which compromise does not feel like moral surrender. Courts require disciplined language precisely because public anger is unreliable. Science requires uncertainty markers because confident stories can outrun evidence. Diplomacy requires face-saving because humiliated actors can choose war over technically better agreements.
The problem is therefore not civility. It is civility detached from power analysis.
A mediator should use language that preserves negotiation while privately understanding the bargaining positions. A regulator can consult stakeholders while recognizing that an industry with hundreds of lawyers has a different capacity to influence a rule than dispersed consumers. A court can protect due process while accounting for intimidation and unequal access to evidence. A researcher can describe uncertainty without pretending that every hypothesis is equally plausible.
The mature version of civilized language is not neutral in the sense of refusing judgment. It is disciplined in how judgment is expressed and tested.
This distinction matters because the alternative to smooth language can be a politics in which every disagreement becomes existential. If every conflict is narrated as predator versus victim, then compromise becomes collaboration with evil. Political actors lose the ability to retreat without humiliation. Citizens sort themselves into moral camps whose internal cohesion depends on treating the other side as dangerous. Under those conditions, the realist logic of preemptive capture intensifies. Each faction seeks more power because peaceful alternation no longer feels safe.
Civilized language can therefore be a technology of de-escalation. It can lower the stakes enough that actors accept procedures rather than seek total victory. Its weakness becomes a strength when the alternative is spiraling retaliation.
The chapter’s role in this book is deliberately uncomfortable. After nine chapters insisting that power must be named, the civilized voice reminds us that accurate diagnosis and sustainable coexistence are different tasks. A society cannot be governed entirely in the language of exposure. People need room to change position without confessing total moral defeat. Institutions need continuity even when narratives conflict.
The danger lies in confusing the diplomatic surface with the underlying mechanics. A negotiation table can create equality of speaking time without equality of exit options. A stakeholder process can offer every participant a microphone while one participant controls whether the project proceeds. A public consultation can record opposition while the legal decision has already been allocated elsewhere. Civility is valuable only if analysts remain capable of seeing through the civility when necessary.
The penultimate thesis is therefore deliberately moderate. Smooth language is not the enemy. It becomes dangerous when it erases asymmetry, delays action indefinitely, or allows powerful actors to convert demands for nuance into protection from judgment. Used well, it can preserve the shared arena in which power struggles remain nonviolent. Used badly, it can become the lullaby through which a society describes the loss of its own capacity to resist.
We are now ready for the reversal. The first nine chapters argued that rights need power. The tenth showed how easily moral language can conceal power. The final chapter will ask whether the realist, after learning to see power everywhere, has made an error just as serious: mistaking the ability to dominate for the deepest form of power.
There is a further reason to preserve the civilized voice: human beings are poor at living permanently inside moral emergency. Constant exposure to accusatory language produces fatigue, defensive identity, and eventually indifference. Institutions need ordinary registers in which conflicts can be processed without requiring participants to experience every dispute as a referendum on their worth.
The art is to maintain two languages at once. The public language of procedure keeps the arena open. The analytical language of power ensures that procedure is not mistaken for equality. Mature institutions can say, in effect, “we will treat everyone with procedural dignity while still investigating whether one party has the capacity to impose costs the other cannot.” Civility and realism need not be enemies.
This dual capacity may be one of the hardest institutional achievements in the entire book. Systems often choose between moralized conflict and depoliticized administration. A durable free order requires enough moral energy to recognize domination and enough procedural restraint to prevent recognition from becoming license for unlimited retaliation.
Chapter 11 — Power Against Itself
11.1 The trap inside the hard thesis
The hard thesis has earned its force. Rights without institutions are fragile. Culture contains power. Nonintervention has beneficiaries. The state concentrates coercion. Institutional capture can be rational. Organized minorities can dominate dispersed majorities. Liberty requires maintenance. Historical victories depend on capacity. Anyone who ignores these facts invites exploitation by actors who do not.
Now the trap closes.
The first error is subtle: because power determines which norms can be enforced, we begin to treat power as though it determined which norms are justified. The argument slides from description to evaluation. A group can enslave another, therefore slavery is politically real. That is true. The group can maintain slavery for generations, therefore slavery is legitimate. Nothing in the first proposition entails the second.
The distinction may seem obvious, yet much political realism depends on blurring it. Successful systems acquire a retrospective aura of necessity. Institutions that survived are described as adapted to reality. Groups that lost are described as unrealistic. The fact of victory becomes evidence of wisdom. But survival selects for survival under particular conditions, not for justice, truth, or even long-term robustness.
A criminal organization can be effective. A dictatorship can be stable. A monopoly can be profitable. A slave system can persist. These outcomes demonstrate capability. They do not generate moral authority.
The second error is more damaging to the realist’s own theory. Power is multidimensional. An actor can increase one form of power by consuming another. A ruler who eliminates all opposition may increase freedom of action while reducing information quality. An elite that captures courts may win individual disputes while making contracts less credible across the economy. A corporation that destroys competition may increase market share while reducing the innovation pressure that kept it competent. A military that terrifies civilians may gain short-term control while destroying the legitimacy needed for intelligence and cooperation.
The crude realist sees the immediate victory and calls it power. The metarational realist asks what capabilities the victory has silently destroyed.
This is where “might is right” begins to fail even as a theory of might.
Power that cannot tolerate counterpower may be weaker than power that can.
The third error follows from the first two: assuming that because power is unavoidable, every constraint on power is hypocrisy. This is a favorite move of sophisticated cynicism. Courts use coercion, so judicial restraint is merely another power strategy. Human-rights organizations seek influence, so their moral language is only a weapon. Protesters build organizations, so emancipation is just one faction competing for dominance.
There is truth in each observation and error in the conclusion. The fact that moral institutions operate through power does not erase the distinction between power organized to remain contestable and power organized to eliminate contestation. A court that can be appealed differs politically from a secret police order even though both ultimately rely on state force. A union that can lose a vote differs from a party that imprisons rivals even though both seek influence.
Cynicism becomes shallow when it notices that everyone has interests and stops analysis there. The important question is what institutional forms interests take. Some strategies preserve a field in which rivals remain actors; others attempt to convert rivals into subjects. That difference is not moral decoration. It changes information, innovation, succession, and the cost of error.
A fourth error is temporal. Hard realism often takes the current distribution of capability as the baseline of rationality. Yet strategies alter the future distribution. Terror may suppress opposition now while creating more committed enemies. Exclusion may protect an incumbent profession while reducing the talent entering it. Censorship may stabilize a narrative while degrading the system’s ability to detect error. The rationality of domination depends on the time horizon used to evaluate it.
Short horizons naturally favor extraction. If a ruler expects to lose office next month, stealing today may be rational. If an owner expects to sell a depleted asset tomorrow, maintenance looks wasteful. Institutions exist partly to lengthen horizons by making actors expect future rounds. Stable succession, property rules, pensions, professional careers, and repeated elections encourage behavior whose payoff arrives later.
The deepest critique of crude power politics is therefore not that it is too rational. It is often rational over a horizon made artificially short by insecurity. Durable institutions change the horizon and thereby change what rational behavior means.
11.2 The information problem and the corruption paradox
Every hierarchy has an information problem. The people at the top depend on subordinates to report conditions they cannot observe directly. The more dangerous it becomes to deliver bad news, the more optimistic the information becomes. A leader can therefore increase obedience while decreasing knowledge.
The pattern is familiar in authoritarian politics, corporations, military organizations, and families. A manager punishes employees who challenge unrealistic deadlines. Soon every project is reported as on schedule until failure becomes undeniable. A political leader publicly humiliates officials who report economic problems. Statistics improve faster than the economy. A military commander treats retreat as cowardice, so local officers conceal losses. A patriarch interprets disagreement as disloyalty, so family members perform harmony while privately planning exit.
The ruler experiences silence as evidence of control.
Silence may be evidence that the reporting system has died.
This creates a fundamental trade-off. To receive useful information, the powerful actor must make some forms of disagreement safe. But making disagreement safe creates spaces in which counterpower can organize. The dictator wants truthful subordinates who never challenge authority, yet truthfulness itself requires permission to contradict authority. The executive wants employees who identify strategic errors, yet error detection can threaten the status hierarchy that made the executive powerful.
The corruption paradox is similar. Capture initially protects the elite. Over time, universal knowledge that institutions are captured changes everyone’s behavior. Contracts require political insurance. Talented people invest in connections rather than competence. Entrepreneurs avoid sectors dominated by patrons. officials hide information because evidence can be weaponized. Rivals move assets abroad. citizens evade taxes they believe will be stolen. The captured institution loses the general trust that made it valuable.
Figure 5. The capture-to-fragility feedback chain.
The figure shows a feedback chain with no mathematical claim attached to it. Capture reduces independent challenge. Reduced challenge worsens information and increases selective extraction. Extraction lowers legitimacy and voluntary cooperation. Lower cooperation raises the cost of control. Higher control costs encourage more capture and coercion. What began as a strategy for security can produce systemic fragility.
This is why the strongest actor may benefit from rules that constrain it. An independent court can occasionally rule against a government, which is precisely why investors believe contracts will survive a change in government. A free press can embarrass leaders, which is precisely why it can reveal problems before they become catastrophic. A professional civil service can resist political orders, which is precisely why it can preserve administrative competence across electoral turnover.
Rights begin to look different from this angle. They are not merely protections given by the strong to the weak out of benevolence. They can be commitment technologies through which powerful actors make their own future behavior more predictable. A ruler who cannot credibly promise not to confiscate successful businesses may receive less investment. By accepting property constraints enforceable by independent courts, the ruler gives up some immediate freedom and gains a richer tax base. A political party that cannot credibly promise not to imprison opponents may face desperate resistance. By protecting opposition rights, it lowers the stakes of losing and makes peaceful competition more plausible.
The constraint creates capacity.
This is the part crude realists miss because they treat every limitation as loss. In repeated social systems, the ability to bind oneself can be a form of power. The actor who can make a credible promise can coordinate futures unavailable to an actor who remains free to defect at any moment.
The information problem is not cured simply by technology. A ruler can possess unprecedented surveillance and still misunderstand society if the categories used to interpret data are politically protected. Dashboards can report precisely what officials have chosen to measure while omitting phenomena nobody is permitted to name. Artificial intelligence can amplify the problem if models are optimized to satisfy the assumptions of the organization commissioning them.
Epistemic counterpower therefore requires more than data collection. It requires independent actors able to generate inconvenient interpretations: opposition researchers, rival firms, auditors, scientists, journalists, courts, local governments, whistleblowers, and citizens. Their redundancy looks inefficient because multiple institutions investigate overlapping realities. The overlap is the point. Monopolies of interpretation are fragile even when technically sophisticated.
This provides a nonmoral reason to defend pluralism. Competing centers of inquiry create error-correction channels. A system that wants to learn needs some people whose careers do not depend on confirming the current leadership’s worldview.
11.3 When the slave becomes the master
Oppressed groups learn the language of power because they must. They organize, build solidarity, acquire resources, control institutions, and challenge the legitimacy of the dominant order. Success can produce emancipation. It can also reproduce the structure of domination with new occupants.
This is the oldest warning in revolutionary politics. The slave becomes the master, not necessarily because liberation was false, but because the organizational instruments required to defeat domination can survive after the original enemy is gone. Secrecy, discipline, centralization, moral certainty, emergency authority, and loyalty tests may be effective during struggle. Once victory arrives, the movement discovers that it possesses a machinery well suited to suppressing dissent.
The new rulers can justify repression with impeccable historical memory. Opponents resemble the former oppressor. Criticism threatens the fragile gains of liberation. Independent institutions may shelter reaction. Compromise can be framed as betrayal of victims. The stronger the moral narrative, the easier it becomes to treat counterpower as illegitimate.
This is where victimhood can become political capital without becoming false. A group may have been genuinely oppressed and still become capable of oppression. Historical injury explains fear; it does not grant permanent moral immunity to present power. The metarational view must be able to hold both statements simultaneously.
The hidden power of the weak also becomes visible here. Weak actors are not powerful only when they acquire weapons or offices. They can withdraw cooperation, deny legitimacy, reveal information, form networks, migrate, sabotage, refuse, shame, litigate, vote, strike, or make domination expensive in ways difficult to predict from formal hierarchy. A regime that treats the weak as inert resources frequently underestimates these capacities until resistance becomes organized.
This means universal rights can be interpreted as anti-predation infrastructure. They establish boundaries that do not depend entirely on which group currently holds the moral high ground. Freedom of speech protects today’s reformer and tomorrow’s critic of the reformer. Due process protects the revolutionary before victory and the former ruler after defeat. Property rules protect dissidents from confiscation and can also protect entrenched wealth.
Association rights protect minorities and organizations that majorities dislike. No right guarantees only morally attractive users.

That neutrality is frustrating. It is also the point.
If rights applied only to actors whom the current majority judged virtuous, they would be rewards rather than constraints. Their deepest political function is to reduce the ability of temporary power to define which people remain entitled to future power.
None of these institutions is pure. Free speech can spread lies. association can empower extremists. Property rights can entrench inequality. Elections can reward demagogues. Due process can allow guilty people to escape punishment. The realist sees these costs clearly.
The metarational question is whether the costs are partly the price of keeping counterpower alive.
A system that eliminates every dangerous use of freedom may eliminate the freedom’s protective function. The dissident is dangerous to the ruler because dissent must be capable of becoming consequential. A union is meaningful because it can disrupt production. An opposition party matters because it can remove the government. A court is independent because it can issue judgments the executive hates. A press is free because it can publish information powerful people would prefer suppressed.
The right to remain harmless is not much of a right.
The danger is greatest when a victorious movement believes history has permanently certified its virtue. Past suffering can make present restraint feel like betrayal. Yet the moral achievement of liberation is precisely the creation of a world in which no group needs to dominate in order to be safe. If safety requires permanent supremacy, liberation has remained structurally incomplete.
This is why constitutional moments after conflict are so important. The temptation is to encode the victor’s moral narrative as permanent state identity. A more durable design asks what protections the victor would want if it lost the next election, became a demographic minority, or watched a rival control the institutions it is now constructing. Designing from behind that uncertainty can convert memory of oppression into limits on future oppression rather than into a license for reversal.
The test of an emancipatory movement is not only how it treats former enemies at the moment of victory. It is whether it preserves institutions through which future generations can criticize the movement’s own legacy.
11.4 Counterpower as the architecture of durable freedom
We can now return to the problem of culture. Earlier we argued that “endogenous adaptation” is more defensible than either external domination or romantic noninterference, but we also admitted that local power can monopolize the meaning of “endogenous.” The final correction is to define endogenous change not by the absence of external influence, but by the presence of distributed internal agency.
A culture is more genuinely self-governing when people inside it can disagree, associate, experiment, leave, return, publish, reinterpret tradition, challenge leaders, and form alternative institutions without facing disproportionate coercion. This does not require every culture to converge on one social model. It requires that “the culture” remain something people participate in making rather than an asset owned by its current interpreters.
External actors can support or damage that capacity. The relevant test is not whether they influence the culture; influence is inevitable. The test is whether their influence expands durable local counterpower or substitutes external control for local control. A scholarship that creates independent capacity differs from a bureaucracy that dictates outcomes. Legal protection for dissent differs from choosing which dissenting faction must win. The line is imperfect, but it is more informative than the fantasy of untouched cultural evolution.
The same logic applies to states. Durable freedom is not the absence of strong institutions. It requires institutions strong enough to enforce general rules and fragmented enough that no one institution can become the final judge of its own legitimacy. A weak state may leave citizens exposed to gangs, oligarchs, monopolies, militias, or local patriarchies. An unconstrained strong state may become the predator itself. The objective is capable government surrounded by capable countergovernment: courts, legislatures, media, local authorities, associations, markets with real competition, professional bodies, and citizens with enough material independence to say no.
This is not a geometric balance in which every actor has equal power. Equal power is impossible and often undesirable. A police officer should possess powers an ordinary citizen does not. A surgeon should have authority in an operating room. A central bank should perform tasks that cannot be crowdsourced. The problem is whether specialized authority can be reviewed, replaced, appealed, and prevented from expanding indefinitely beyond its function.
Rights are one language for encoding those limits.
At the beginning of the book, we said a right without teeth is a moral demand waiting for an institution. We can now add the reverse: teeth without rights eventually bite the body that feeds them. Power that destroys every rival becomes epistemically blind, economically extractive, politically brittle, and personally dangerous even for members of the ruling group. When law becomes a weapon, tomorrow’s ruler inherits the weapon. When property depends on favor, today’s favorite is only one succession away from vulnerability. When dissent is treason, policy errors become difficult to correct before reality corrects them more violently.
The strongest reason for the strong to accept limits is therefore not charity. It is uncertainty about the future.
Nobody remains permanently strong. Leaders age. Coalitions split. technologies change. fortunes reverse. Children inherit enemies they did not choose. Economic shocks create new classes. wars reorder alliances. A system designed on the assumption that current power will be permanent is a system designed by people who have misunderstood time.
This is where universal rights acquire their deepest realist justification. Universality is a commitment made under uncertainty about who will be weak tomorrow. It says that some protections should survive changes in identity, faction, status, and political fortune. The principle is morally ambitious, but it is also strategically conservative: it reduces the incentive for every political contest to become existential.
The final reversal can therefore be stated without sentimentalism.
Might does not create right. Right without organized might is fragile. Might without institutionalized right is self-corroding. Durable political power requires counterpower because systems need information, credible commitments, legitimate succession, and the capacity to correct mistakes before mistakes become catastrophes.
Rights are not real as metaphysical shields that descend automatically when injustice appears. They are very real as social technologies when people build, fund, defend, and reproduce the institutions that make them effective.
The phrase “might is right” captured one half of political adulthood: morality that cannot survive contact with hostile power remains vulnerable. The phrase fails when it forgets the other half: power that cannot survive contact with criticism, limits, succession, and independent truth becomes vulnerable too.
A mature society does not choose between morality and power. It builds power that can be morally contested without collapsing and morality that can acquire enough power to matter.
That is a less elegant sentence.
It is also closer to how freedom actually survives.
Epilogue — Making Morality Exist
At the end of a book this contrarian, it would be easy to retreat into reassurance. We could say that power matters, of course, but justice matters more, and leave the reader with the moral hierarchy restored. That would miss the argument.
Justice does not protect people by occupying a higher conceptual shelf. It protects people when moral claims are converted into arrangements capable of surviving conflict.
A child protected from abuse does not need a theory of universal rights at the moment of protection. She needs adults who will act, institutions that will believe evidence, procedures that cannot be quietly purchased by the abuser, somewhere safe to go, and a social order that does not make survival depend on returning to the person who holds power over her. The universal principle matters because it tells those institutions whom they are obligated to protect. The institutions matter because they make the principle material.
A minority does not remain free because the majority has permanently become benevolent. It remains freer when law, organization, social norms, economic independence, and political alliances make persecution costly. A business does not rely on property rights because nobody powerful will ever desire its assets. It relies on courts and institutions that make confiscation difficult even when confiscation is tempting. A journalist does not possess freedom because every official admires criticism. Freedom exists when the official’s dislike is insufficient to silence the journalist cheaply.
Morality becomes political through capacity.
But capacity becomes dangerous when it forgets why it was built. The police created to protect can intimidate. The revolutionary organization built to liberate can become a new ruling class. The court designed to constrain arbitrary government can become an insulated elite. The union created to balance employers can become an exclusionary cartel. The company that coordinated innovation can become a monopoly. Counterpower is not morally self-purifying merely because it began as resistance.
This is why there is no final institutional design. Every concentration of protective capacity creates a new object that must itself be constrained. Civilization is not the elimination of power but the recursive domestication of power: force constrained by law, law challenged by rights, rights supported by organizations, organizations exposed to competition and accountability, governments checked by governments, experts questioned by other experts, majorities limited by protections they cannot revoke at every moment of anger.
The recursion can look inefficient. It is inefficient.
Predation is often faster.
One person with a weapon can make a decision immediately. A court requires evidence, procedure, argument, and appeal. A dictator can order a bridge built tomorrow. A constitutional system may spend years debating budgets, land rights, environmental impacts, procurement, and local opposition. Sometimes the dictator’s bridge will indeed appear faster.
The relevant comparison is not only the first bridge.
It is the society that still knows how to correct the tenth mistake.
The central lesson of the book can therefore be compressed into three sentences.
A moral right can exist without political power, but it cannot reliably protect anyone without institutions that make violation costly. Power can create order without moral legitimacy, but unconstrained power tends to destroy information, trust, and counterpower until the order becomes brittle. A durable free society is the unstable construction in which enough power exists to defend rights and enough rights exist to prevent defenders from becoming owners.
There is no guarantee that such a construction will survive.
That is not a reason for despair.
It is the reason vigilance matters.
