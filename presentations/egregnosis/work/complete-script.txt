Before an idea becomes visible
Egregnosis asks why an available idea may remain invisible to an entire society.
The missing step can be shared recognition rather than another piece of evidence.
The book proposes a vocabulary for collective attention, expectations, prestige, and memory.
It follows those filters into AI systems that can widen or narrow what becomes thinkable.
Readers learn to examine how an idea becomes publicly possible without assuming rejection proves genius.

Knowing that others know
The opening account separates private belief from knowledge of what others will publicly do.
A possibility can remain individually understood while seeming collectively unavailable.
Visible support changes that situation by altering expectations about participation and enforcement.
The book uses this distinction to introduce a distributed architecture of collective perception.
It does not require an invisible mind hovering above the people involved.

The filter can learn
Prestige, institutional categories, memory, and identity then explain different kinds of selective visibility.
The AI chapters ask what happens when those inherited filters become fluent assistants.
An unusual idea may become clearer while losing the irregularity that made it informative.
The book also asks whether its own collective-brain analogy describes a testable mechanism.
Those objections matter because an attractive vocabulary can conceal the very filtering it claims to reveal.

Begin where reality became shared
Begin with the opening threshold story, then follow how visibility acquires institutional consequences.
The later AI discussion extends that question into the systems shaping everyday intellectual choices.
Read the objections alongside the proposal rather than treating the new term as established science.
The book offers distinctions between unavailable evidence and evidence that cannot yet become publicly actionable.
That difference gives readers a more precise way to investigate why an idea cannot travel.
