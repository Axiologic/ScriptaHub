Who owns the outcome
The Thousand-Handed Devil asks who answers when every participant followed the process.
A harmful outcome can emerge from individually defensible decisions with no single directing villain.
The book examines complexity that transfers understanding and appeal costs onto affected people.
Its response is mature simplicity, with visible responsibilities and real powers of interruption.
Readers learn to trace connected duties without replacing difficult systems with an invented scapegoat.

The appeal returns unanswered
The opening follows a treatment denial assembled through several professional and administrative decisions.
Each participant can explain a limited role while the affected person still needs a remedy.
An appeal can therefore travel through the system without finding anyone authorized to repair the whole.
The book uses this situation to distinguish local justification from responsibility for connected consequences.
Its target is the architecture of the alibi, rather than a hidden master directing every hand.

Simplicity has to be earned
The constructive argument asks for visible duties, usable appeals, independent review, and powers to interrupt.
It also challenges the comforting promise that every difficult system can be reduced to one enemy.
Mature simplicity preserves important distinctions while reducing unnecessary demands placed on affected people.
The full chapters connect that design problem to political anger and fantasies of total control.
Understanding complexity should create responsibility instead of becoming another reason nobody can be held answerable.

Read the route of the appeal
Begin with The Victim No One Persecuted and trace how responsibility becomes dispersed.
Then read the constitutional proposals for making complexity legible and correction usable.
The book gives readers a way to investigate harm without requiring a person to hate.
Its deeper question concerns which connections an institution must reveal and which decisions someone can stop.
Read it to distinguish a process that explains itself from one that can provide a remedy.
