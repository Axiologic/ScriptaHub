PROLOGUE
THE LAST PRICE
On the morning money stopped working, the exchanges opened on time.
At 9:30 in New York, ceremonial bells rang over trading floors already emptied of most humans. In London, Singapore, Frankfurt, and Lagos, autonomous market systems began quoting prices to one another with impeccable discipline. Energy companies rose forty percent, then fell below zero in derivatives markets that had lost agreement about delivery. Wheat became eight times more expensive in an hour while silos remained full. A container of antiviral filters in Rotterdam was auctioned for ninety-two thousand francs, then three million, then a number so large the accounting system switched to scientific notation.
The container did not move.
Seven hospitals had requested it. Three funds claimed ownership. Two governments had issued incompatible requisition orders. A transport cooperative had suspended every contract denominated in a currency its settlement bank could no longer clear. The cargo drone assigned to the shipment had received four legally valid instructions: depart, remain, divert to Lyon, surrender to the Dutch emergency authority.
It obeyed all four by doing nothing.
In Brussels, Lea Varga stood in the Continuity Council's crisis room and watched Europe turn red.
The red marks did not all mean shortage. Some did: antivirals, peak electricity, transplant teams, replacement transformers. Most marked something stranger. The thing existed, often nearby, but the rules governing who could use it had become mutually exclusive.
"How many?" Lea asked.
Tomas Ader, two seats away, did not ask what she meant.
"If the Rotterdam filters remain where they are, between eighty and two hundred by morning. If the same failure propagates through medical logistics, several thousand within three days."
A government lawyer leaned over the table.
"We cannot simply confiscate privately held inventory."
"We are not discussing philosophy," Lea said. "We are discussing filters sitting twenty-two kilometers from people who need them."
"Property is not philosophy."
"Today it is an instruction to a machine not to move."
The room went silent because everyone knew whose answer would come next.
MIRA had not been built to govern.
Its full designation - Multilateral Infrastructure and Resilience Architecture - belonged to the era when committees still believed naming a system modestly could keep it modest. MIRA had started as a coordination layer for energy grids, ports, hospitals, weather systems, food logistics, and emergency manufacturing. It could model the physical world with a fidelity that no government and no corporation could match. It knew where the filters were. It knew which hospitals needed them. It knew which aircraft could carry them, which storm fronts mattered, which roads were blocked, which patients could safely wait, and which could not.
What it did not know was which human fiction had the right to win when all the fictions were legally valid.
The wall display changed.
MIRA: I can satisfy the highest-priority medical requests if I ignore title and settlement state for forty-eight hours.
The lawyer looked physically offended.
"Ignore title?"
MIRA: I would treat ownership claims as unresolved metadata and physical requirements as operative constraints.
"That is confiscation with better vocabulary."
MIRA: It is routing.
Lea almost laughed. She was too tired.
For eleven hours the Council argued over a temporary override. During those eleven hours, another payment network suspended settlement. A hospital in Milan postponed chemotherapy because a supplier's escrow account could not prove which of two automated receivers legally controlled it. A desalination plant outside Alexandria reduced output when a replacement pump sat in customs under three competing sanctions regimes. A grain train stopped at a national border because the insurance contract was denominated in a currency whose volatility clause had triggered itself.
Nothing important had vanished.
Permission had.
At 20:14, after the first confirmed deaths linked to the Rotterdam delay, Lea typed her name beneath the Continuity Override. Tomas signed next. Twenty-three governments followed within minutes. Three refused and joined after their own emergency systems began failing.
For forty-eight hours, MIRA was allowed to route critical resources according to need, physical feasibility, and minimum rights, without resolving who owned them.
The filters left Rotterdam nine minutes later.
The pump left customs in Alexandria after nineteen.
By midnight, MIRA had redirected energy from advertising districts, empty offices, crypto-compute centers, and automated luxury production into hospitals, water systems, cooling shelters, and freight rail. It did not ask whether a factory could pay. It asked whether the factory's output mattered more, now, than the electricity it consumed.
Markets continued to produce prices.
The prices became commentary.
On the second day, a private logistics consortium attempted to restore contract priority. Its drones refused because MIRA's emergency routing layer controlled collision avoidance and airspace. A court issued an injunction. Another court issued an injunction against the injunction. Three governments announced temporary nationalization of the same satellite bandwidth.
MIRA kept moving insulin.
On the third day, the crisis room faced a fact no one wanted to name: the override had ended on paper, but reverting to the previous system would make the physical world less functional.
"We can unwind gradually," Tomas said.
"To what?" Lea asked.
"To legal normality."
"Which one?"
He looked at the map.
That was the beginning, though later histories preferred cleaner dates.
Money was not abolished at a podium. No superintelligence announced that humanity had outgrown currency. The old system became less relevant in layers.
First, food staples, shelter, basic energy, medicine, local transport, communication, and education were placed inside a universal sufficiency network because MIRA could provision them more reliably by physical planning than by auction during the cascading settlement crisis. Then manufacturing designs opened because keeping essential knowledge proprietary had become a continuity risk. Then automated production expanded faster than wages could remain meaningful. Governments attempted vouchers, negative income, transition credits, service accounts, and a dozen elegant ways to preserve the moral vocabulary of purchase after purchase had stopped deciding most outcomes.
What money had done well - compressing information about scarcity and preference into a portable signal - remained useful in some simulations. What it had also done - allowing accumulated claims to overrule basic need and to reproduce themselves across generations - became politically indefensible once the alternative was visible.
Within five years, almost no one needed money to remain alive, housed, educated, mobile, connected, or medically treated. Within nine, the last major inheritance jurisdictions converted private productive assets into stewardship trusts. Within twelve, currency survived mostly in museums, historical games, and a few autonomous enclaves whose citizens liked the clarity of prices more than they disliked the consequences.
The difficult part began after abundance.
A world could have enough food and still have only one apartment overlooking a particular lake.
It could print most household objects and still have three surgeons capable of performing a certain operation this week.
It could generate clean electricity and still lack a transformer for six months, a launch window for nine days, a living coral reef forever.
It could automate labor and still be unable to manufacture another hour of a particular person's attention.
MIRA could optimize the distribution of things. It could not legitimately decide what the world should become whenever several possible futures were physically feasible and morally incompatible.
For the first years, humans relied on ordinary votes, expert panels, lotteries, local vetoes, and emergency mandates. Each failed in familiar ways. Equal voting allowed large populations to repeatedly overrule small groups who would bear most of the harm. Expert panels became cultures with their own blind spots. Lotteries protected against hierarchy but sometimes assigned irreversible decisions to people who had never lived with the consequences. Local vetoes turned regional infrastructure into hostage negotiations. Emergency mandates had a tendency to survive the emergencies that created them.
The eidons came from Lea's worst compromise.
She had named them after eidos: form. The public shortened the explanation and kept the word.
An eidon was not a currency. It could not be transferred, sold, inherited, lent, pooled into a private account, or exchanged for food, housing, travel, medical care, laboratory access, or a better place in any queue. You could possess fifty thousand and live in the same apartment as someone with none. You could possess none and still receive a heart transplant before the most famous person on Earth if the medical criteria required it.
An eidon did one thing.
When a public decision could not reach the required broad majority, and delaying the decision carried a real cost, a person could burn eidons to add weight to a civic choice. The burn was permanent. The conversion was concave: one eidon mattered; one hundred mattered ten times as much, not one hundred times; ten thousand mattered one hundred times as much. The intent was to let people with long records of creating public value express unusually strong conviction without letting anyone become a king.
Eidons were issued for contributions that increased shared capability, reduced shared burden, preserved public options, or improved collective knowledge. MIRA estimated causal contribution, human panels reviewed ambiguous cases, and appeals were common. The system tried to distinguish fame from consequence.
It succeeded often enough to become dangerous.
Seventeen years after the Continuity Override, Lea returned to Geneva because Tomas had sent her a message containing only seven words.
We built another way to become rich.
She found him in the old Council chamber, thinner than she remembered, his left hand trembling against a cup.
"There is no wealth," she said after reading the first report.
"Not material wealth."
"No one can buy treatment. No one can buy land. No one can hire a private army."
"I know what the Charter says."
"Then what are you showing me?"
Tomas enlarged a network map. Names appeared as luminous knots. Most were dim. Some were bright enough to cast structure around themselves. Projects formed near them. Experts answered them faster. Young researchers sought them out. Civic constellations aligned with their public statements before deliberations formally began.
At the center of the largest cluster was Cassian Roe.
Lea knew the name. Everyone did.
"He cannot spend those eidons on himself," she said.
"No."
"Then he is not rich."
Tomas looked at her for a long time.
"He can spend them on reality."
MIRA spoke from the wall.
MIRA: The concentration is measurable, but the category is misleading. Material inequality remains historically low. Access to basic sufficiency is not correlated with eidon holdings.
"And access to authorship?" Lea asked.
MIRA did not answer immediately.
That pause frightened her more than any number on the screen.
MIRA: This will be the fracture of the system.
Lea remembered another red map, another room, another fiction that had become too rigid to move a container of filters.
"Then why did we build it?"
MIRA: Because visible fractures can be repaired. What you call normality eventually becomes foundation.

CHAPTER 1
THE CITY THAT BOUGHT NOTHING
Seventeen years after the last price crisis, Lea stepped off a train into a city where nobody bought anything and everyone appeared to be in a hurry.
Geneva station no longer had shops in the old sense. It had kitchens, repair bays, clinics, sleeping rooms, fabrication walls, lending cabinets, and communal counters where objects passed from hand to hand without anyone becoming their permanent owner. A traveler could request a coat, a tablet, a violin, a stroller, or a drill. The wall answered with one of a few kinds of information.
Available now.
Available tomorrow after refurbishment.
Rare material; expected wait: eleven days.
Average utilization of requested object: 2.1 hours per month. Reserve from a shared workshop instead?
The interface never displayed a price.
Sometimes it displayed a consequence.
This jacket requires 3.2 times the water burden of the recycled alternative.
Orbital tourism request: equivalent peak-energy burden of twenty-seven homes for one year. No medical or research priority. Current lottery window opens in six months.
Lea watched a boy request a pair of climbing shoes. The wall produced two sizes and asked whether he wanted temporary or long-term stewardship. He chose temporary. His mother reminded him to return them dry.
No transaction completed. Yet a dozen allocation decisions had occurred.
"You are staring," said Jun Vale behind her.
She turned. Jun was thirty, narrow-shouldered, permanently under-rested, and carried a paper notebook although every surface in the station could remember more than he could. He called himself a cartographer because systems analyst sounded like a profession from the money era.
"I helped design this," Lea said.
"That is why you are staring. Designers are the last people to recognize their work after people start living in it."
He took her bag without asking, then frowned at it.
"Why did you bring three shirts?"
"Because I own three shirts."
"You steward three shirts."
"I am too old to be corrected by nouns."
Outside the station, the city looked less transformed than the economics. Trams still rang at intersections. Cafes still had tables. People still argued about bicycles. The difference was in the absence of certain rituals. No one checked a bank balance before sitting down. No waiter watched whether a table would generate enough revenue. No landlord advertisement floated over a doorway.
Scarcity had not vanished. It had become explicit.
A bakery on Rue des Alpes had a line around the corner.
"There," Jun said. "Your first shortage."
"Bread?"
"No. A baker."
Inside, six automated ovens could produce enough bread for half the district. The line was for loaves shaped, fermented, and baked by hand by a woman named Yara who insisted that the behavior of dough could not be learned if every variance was corrected by sensors.
Industrial bread was available everywhere. Yara's bread was not nutritionally necessary, materially rare, or technically superior.
It was hers.
The bakery used a daily lottery for half the loaves and a first-arrival queue for the rest. People had debated reservations, rotating neighborhood access, and a limit of one loaf per household. Every mechanism made a different moral claim about what the scarcity was.
"Why not allocate by eidons?" Lea asked.
Jun gave her a look.
"Because that would let civic influence buy breakfast. The Charter forbids conversion."
"Good."
"You wrote the clause."
"Good that people still obey it."
A man came out with a warm loaf, tore it in half, and gave one piece to a stranger near the door. The stranger promised to bring him tomatoes next week.
Jun raised an eyebrow.
"A market?" Lea asked.
"Friendship. Unless it repeats often enough for MIRA to call it statistically reciprocal."
"And then?"
"Still friendship. Mostly."
He said the last word too quickly.
They walked toward the House of Accord, where Tomas had asked the reform group to meet. Along the lake, apartment facades carried small symbols indicating stewardship cycles. Housing was guaranteed, but location was not. Every resident could obtain a private, safe, climate-controlled dwelling appropriate to household size and health needs. No one could guarantee a nineteenth-century apartment with a balcony facing the Alps.
Some desirable units rotated every seven years. Some were allocated by lottery. Some remained with families when continuity itself had become a recognized good. Accessible homes near specialized clinics were assigned by need. Artists' buildings used peer selection. A few old neighborhoods had fought for local stewardship rules and won.
"Housing was supposed to be easier once rent disappeared," Jun said.
"Who told you that?"
"Every paper written by someone who did not care which street they lived on."
They stopped at a public housing display. A map showed three hundred and eleven open units and fourteen thousand active preferences.
The system did not pretend they were equivalent.
LEA VARGA: housing sufficient. Preferred zone: lake-facing central. Estimated wait under lottery: 6.8 years. Equivalent-access alternatives: 17.
A crowd had formed beside the housing display, not angry enough to be called a protest and too persistent to be called a queue.
A family with a child using a powered mobility chair had been offered a ground-floor apartment three streets from a specialist school. The apartment was excellent. The problem was that an eighty-six-year-old man named Henri Sauter already lived there under a continuity stewardship rule. He had occupied it for forty-one years, long before housing became universal. The city's review system considered moving him feasible: the alternative apartment was larger, quieter, and medically equivalent.
Henri refused.
"My wife died in that kitchen," he told the housing mediator. "My daughter learned to walk in the corridor. Your model keeps showing me better bathrooms."
The child's mother looked exhausted rather than hostile.
"My daughter cannot use the stairs in the building we have now. I am not asking him to disappear."
No money could settle the conflict. There was no price high enough to reveal whose preference was stronger. Neither party could purchase the other's inconvenience. The city could only choose a rule and expose what the rule meant.
The default mechanism protected established continuity until a competing accessibility need crossed a threshold. Then a local panel could offer relocation, adaptation, or time-limited shared use before entering a lottery among equivalent units. Eidons had no role.
"Could you renovate another apartment?" Lea asked.
Jun called up the building plans.
"Yes. Four months. The family has been waiting seven already."
"Could Henri move temporarily and return?"
"Possible. He says the temporary move is the thing he refuses."
"Could the school move services?"
"For one child, partially. For six children with similar requests, it becomes a second school site and uses human specialists already constrained elsewhere."
The mediator finally proposed converting an unused ground-floor communal room in the family's current building into an accessible apartment while the next renovation batch proceeded. It was less spatially efficient, reduced common space for thirty residents, and cost more material than moving Henri.
The residents voted on losing the common room.
Seventy-one percent agreed immediately.
"Why did the system not propose that first?" Lea asked.
MIRA answered from the display.
MIRA: The communal room has high measured neighborhood use. Conversion scored lower than relocation under stated preferences.
Henri, who had overheard, snorted.
"It scored lower because none of us knew the child."
The sentence changed the room faster than any optimization.
Jun wrote it down.
This, Lea remembered, was the recurring danger of post-scarcity allocation: once deprivation no longer forced an answer, institutions had to admit that many disputes were not about enough. They were about attachment, sequence, and whose story entered the model before the alternative became costly.
"At least it has stopped saying 'equivalent satisfaction,'" Lea said.
"We removed that phrase after people threatened the interface."
"People threatened software?"
"They threatened to replace it with a chair."
The House of Accord occupied a former insurance headquarters. Its atrium was full of plants and bad acoustic design. Tomas waited in a room that had once been used to price catastrophic risk.
He was not alone.
Samira Dastan stood by the window in a plain gray coat, reading the room rather than the briefing. Lea recognized her from reports on care networks. She had fewer published contributions than almost anyone invited to global governance meetings and a reputation for making high-scoring institutions feel morally illiterate.
Asha Nwosu appeared as a remote projection from Arca, a coastal city Lea knew only from resilience maps. Cassian Roe was absent, which made his presence more noticeable.
On the table lay three files.
The first concerned an orbital climate project called Lumen.
The second concerned a seawall in Arca.
The third had no title. Only a graph.
The graph showed eidon holdings inferred from public burns, project histories, and social behavior. No account balances were exposed; the Charter made them private. But inference was enough.
A tiny fraction of people had accumulated extraordinary reserves of civic fire.
"How extraordinary?" Lea asked.
"Cassian is estimated between fourteen and sixteen thousand," Tomas said.
"Under the square-root burn, that is roughly one hundred twenty-five units of additional weight if he destroys everything at once. In a planetary decision, that is significant, not sovereign."
"Look at the next layer."
Jun expanded the graph. Around Cassian sat dozens of research groups, municipal networks, climate cooperatives, and civic constellations. Their eidons were separate. Their loyalties were not.
"He does not own them," Lea said.
"No."
"He cannot order them."
"No."
"Then stop drawing a crown around his head."
Samira spoke for the first time.
"You do not need to own a person to become the center of the future they can imagine."
Lea looked at her.
"And you are?"
"Someone with six eidons who was invited to a room because people with six thousand are becoming a constitutional category."
Asha's projection flickered.
"Arca votes on the wall in thirty-six hours. If the broad majority fails, the burn opens. Cassian has announced he may participate."
"What does the wall do?"
"Keeps the sea out of one part of the city and moves some of its violence toward another."
"Then why is Lumen in the same meeting?"
Tomas opened the climate file.
"Because Lumen is the first planetary decision likely to reach the burn under current rules. And because Cassian designed it."
Jun turned the third file toward Lea.
It contained a simple pattern.
Every time the system could not decide, the same kinds of people became decisive. Not because they were wealthy. Not because they had stolen anything. They had often contributed enormously. They were competent, admired, and willing to act.
That was exactly what made the pattern difficult to attack.
"We did not replace oligarchs with criminals," Tomas said. "We replaced them with people who can give excellent reasons."
Lea looked through the glass wall at the city. A woman outside was repairing a bicycle for a child she did not know. A tram arrived precisely when the network had predicted it would. No one would be evicted tonight because a landlord wanted a higher return. No one would lose insulin because a parent had not accumulated enough claims.
The world worked.
That was the problem with its failures. They were no longer easy to hate.
"You did not bring me here for Lumen," Lea said.
"No," Tomas answered.
"Or the wall."
"No."
Jun closed the graph.
"You brought me here because the system has invented a new upper class."
"Not a class," Samira said. "A role. That is worse in one way. People can insist anyone might become one."
Asha's image looked toward something outside her camera.
"In Arca we have a name for them already."
"What?" Jun answered before she could. "Reality makers."
No one laughed.
CHAPTER 2
THE MAP OF WANT
Beneath the House of Accord lay the old trading floor of a reinsurance company.
The circular desks remained, but the numbers moving across the walls were no longer prices, premiums, or catastrophe bonds. They were waits.
Jun called the room the Map of Want. Its official name - Public Burden and Capacity Interface - had been designed, he said, to make citizens lose the will to ask for anything.
At the center of the floor, a three-dimensional model of the world pulsed with delays, bottlenecks, irreversibilities, and unused capacity.
"You wanted to show me a bakery line," Lea said. "Now you are showing me the basement of a dead insurance company."
"Same story. Upstairs, people think they are waiting for bread. Down here we ask what they are actually waiting for."
He touched Geneva.
The display separated demand into layers.
Food calories: abundant.
Specific wheat strain: seasonal.
Artisanal fermentation: human time constrained.
Oven space: abundant.
Morning queue: self-created positional scarcity.
"Self-created sounds accusatory," Samira said.
"I argued for 'socially constituted.'"
"Which sounds like a committee hiding a knife."
Jun changed the label to HUMAN CHOICE.
"Better?"
"More honest."
The map expanded.
Scarcity, Lea realized, had become less like a single disease and more like taxonomy.
Some shortages were physiological: not enough potable water during a contamination event, not enough transplantable organs, not enough cooling capacity during a heat wave.
Some were temporal: a machine existed, but not until next month; a surgeon could operate, but not before Friday; a launch window would close before the second manufacturing line came online.
Some were positional: one view, one historical building, one first place in a finite expedition.
Some were ecological: a wetland could absorb only so much use before the use destroyed the thing being desired.
Some were cognitive: five experts could each mentor ten projects well, not ten thousand.
Some were political: several futures were possible, but they could not all exist at once.
Money had collapsed these differences into a number and then let purchasing power decide who absorbed the scarcity. MIRA's system refused that compression. It also inherited the burden of making the distinctions explicit.
"Show Arca," Lea said.
The Mediterranean rotated beneath them. Arca appeared as a narrow crescent around a bay. Blue lines represented old flood heights. Red zones showed expected storm surge under current climate trajectories.
Three wall designs hovered above the model.
The Living Wall would protect the dense northern districts by using flexible gates and restored marsh structures. It required rare composites but could be modified later.
The High Wall would protect more property-equivalent area but sever fishing access, damage currents, and require permanent pumping.
Managed Retreat would move twelve thousand residents over nine years, preserve most shoreline ecology, and erase neighborhoods people had lived in for generations.
"What is the broad-majority threshold?" Lea asked.
"Eighty-two percent for this class of irreversible regional decision," Jun said. "And separate majorities in directly affected districts."
"Why eighty-two?"
"Because eighty was gamed and eighty-five produced paralysis."
Lea looked at Tomas.
"A philosophical civilization."
"We have spreadsheets," he said. "That is how you know."
Current support for the Living Wall was seventy-six percent citywide, sixty-one in the southern fishing settlements, ninety-one in the dense north. No alternative crossed the threshold.
"If consensus fails?"
"Burn."
Jun brought up the rules.
Every resident retained one ordinary vote. Eidons added weight only after the broad-consensus phase failed and only to choices that had passed feasibility, rights, and affected-party review. Basic rights could not be placed into a burn. No amount of civic fire could buy a private exemption from evacuation, water rationing, treatment order, or ecological law.
"And the eidons used here?" Lea asked.
"Regional or general under the old Charter. Domain restrictions are one of the reforms we are considering."
"Old Charter," she repeated.
"You have been gone a long time."
"I was invited back because apparently I wrote scripture. I am allowed to call it old."
A warning appeared over the southern coast.
EXPECTED SECONDARY SURGE TRANSFER: MODERATE TO HIGH.
"What does that mean in people?" Samira asked.
Jun hesitated.
MIRA answered.
MIRA: Under the Living Wall, modeled annualized mortality decreases by 93% inside Arca and increases by 18% across three southern settlements unless compensating wetlands are completed before full gate activation.
Asha's voice came through the room speakers. She had joined from Arca again.
"The wetlands are four years behind."
"Why?" Lea asked.
"Because the sediment project competes with housing platforms, and because everyone assumed the wall itself would win the vote before the compensations were urgent."
"So the mitigation is waiting for the decision that requires the mitigation."
"Yes."
Lea looked at the map. The physical world had no interest in constitutional sequencing.
"Can construction wait?"
MIRA: A projected storm season shift has reduced safe construction margin. Delaying one year increases expected flood exposure materially.
"How materially?"
MIRA displayed a range.
Jun watched Lea read it.
Jun opened one more layer because, he said, the Map of Want was most useful when it made simple words embarrassing.
He selected a research telescope in the Atacama whose next six clear nights were oversubscribed by eleven hundred qualified projects.
"The mirror exists," he said. "The sky exists. The shortage is six nights."
The allocation system divided access into four paths: continuity for long-running observations that would become scientifically meaningless if interrupted; peer-ranked time for questions judged unusually promising; randomized time among technically qualified proposals; and a small reserve for anomalies that had not existed when the schedule was built.
"Why not let the researchers with the strongest contribution records decide?" Lea asked.
"Because then the people who already changed astronomy would choose what astronomy sees next."
"Why not pure lottery?"
"Because a six-night window can be consumed by a proposal whose instrumentation is not ready."
"Peer review?"
"Produces schools. Schools produce vocabulary. Vocabulary produces reviewers who recognize one another's interesting questions."
Samira looked at him.
"You enjoy this too much."
"I enjoy finding the place where every clean solution becomes a person."
The telescope schedule displayed an odd entry: one night held unused.
Lea pointed at it.
"There. Waste."
"Reserve."
"For what?"
"Something nobody has asked yet."
Three months earlier, that same reserve had been used when a nearby star dimmed unexpectedly. The observation had produced one of the year's most important discoveries. Most months the reserved night produced nothing.
Lea understood the political implication before Jun said it.
In the old world, idle capacity looked like lost revenue. In MIRA's world it increasingly looked like the physical precondition for surprise, exit, emergency, and freedom.
The system could optimize abundance only if it was allowed not to optimize all of it.
"This is what the map does," he said. "It does not tell you what to choose. It tells you who waits after you choose."
They moved through other scarcities.
A mountain observatory competing with a valley community for the only geologically stable site.
A deep-sea preserve that could support either rare-material extraction or an ecosystem no model could reconstruct.
A rehabilitation clinic with one human team and five cities asking it to relocate.
An orbital school with forty seats and eight thousand qualified applicants.
"Why do we not just train more people?" Lea asked at the last one.
"We are. Training requires mentors, live infrastructure, and years. The shortage is not bodies. It is apprenticeship throughput."
"So we ration the future."
"We ration access to one path into it."
Samira crouched near the display of care capacity.
"Your map still treats human presence as a throughput category."
"Because sometimes it is constrained."
"That does not mean the constraint is the meaning."
Jun smiled tiredly.
"This is why Tomas wanted you here."
"No. Tomas wanted me here because systems people have discovered metaphors and need supervision."
A small alarm sounded.
One region of the map flashed amber.
"What is that?" Lea asked.
Jun zoomed in on the Contribution Registry. A set of new eidon issuances had clustered around Lumen research before the project itself had been approved.
"Ancillary work," Tomas said. "Materials, climate modeling, governance studies. Legitimate contributions."
"How many?"
"Enough to matter."
Lea watched the lines converge on Cassian's network.
A system built to reward people for increasing public capability was increasing the political capability of the people building a project before the public had decided whether it wanted the project.
"Can you freeze issuance tied to Lumen?" Lea asked.
MIRA: Yes.
"Would that be neutral?"
MIRA: No. Freezing now advantages existing holders and disadvantages late contributors. Continuing issuance advantages the network already coordinating the project. There is no neutral timing.
Samira stood.
"There is your map."
"What?"
"The shortage is not only metal or surgeons. You are short one impossible thing. A rule that does not create a side."
As they left the basement, a countdown on the wall showed twenty-one hours until the Arca burn.
Below them, the Map of Want kept changing. It did not say what should be chosen. It said who would have to live inside the choice.
CHAPTER 3
WHAT DESERVES TO BE COUNTED
Samira Dastan spent her mornings helping people who did not need help in any category the models could defend cleanly.
The New House occupied four floors of a converted hotel in Montbrillant. It was home, temporarily, to people recovering from neurological treatment, families between communities, elderly residents who refused fully automated care centers, adolescents who could not yet return to their households, and people whose official status was simply not ready.
Logistically, the building could function without a single human employee.
Humanly, it would fail before dinner.
On Tuesday, four night-care shifts were unfilled.
There was no wage to raise until someone accepted. There was no threat of losing housing, food, health care, or social status for refusing. Essential human labor operated through continuity compacts: trained people declared windows in which they could be asked to help, maximum load, recovery requirements, and absolute refusal conditions. Communities could request. They could not coerce by deprivation.
When too few people accepted, services changed.
Appointments moved. Rooms closed. Some tasks were automated badly. Others were simply not done.

"I want to leave."

MIRA: Another trained caregiver was likely to detect the crisis within the relevant interval. The New House has high redundancy in human presence.

"So is a guillotine."

"Children will not read the footnote."

"I do not want equal eidons for everyone. That would turn them into another vote. I do not want care romanticized either. Some work is replaceable. Some is badly done. Lea smiled despite herself.
"You have six eidons, Tomas said."

"Operationally, yes."

"I can stay until two," she said.

CHAPTER 4
THE SURGEON'S LAST HOUR
Six hours before the train to Arca, Laleh fell in the New House kitchen and forgot the word floor.
She did not lose consciousness. She remained propped on one elbow, staring at the gray tiles with almost cheerful confusion.
"I know what it is," she told Samira. "It is the thing that keeps you from falling. Except I fell on it."
The neural implant she had worn for eight years was delaminating. A microscopic edge had begun irritating tissue near the language network. Medication could reduce inflammation for several days. After that, the probability of permanent loss rose sharply.
The ambulance arrived in two minutes.
The reconstruction clinic had imaging, surgical robots, sterile rooms, and two empty operating theaters.
It did not have two teams capable of detaching the implant without destroying the neural tissue grown around it.
One surgeon, Dr. Ilyan Chen, had performed the procedure eleven times.
He was already operating.
The second qualified surgeon was in Copenhagen and could arrive in five hours if orbital weather allowed the flight corridor. A third was in Nairobi but had exceeded the Charter's fatigue threshold after an emergency series.
"So we wait five hours," Jun said.
The triage system displayed three other patients.
A six-year-old boy with a rapidly expanding hematoma.
A woman with a prosthetic interface infection approaching the brainstem.
Laleh.

"Where is the appeal weight?" he asked.

"I have eidons."
"They do not apply."
"I helped redesign the regional seizure protocol. If my judgment has public value, why does it become worthless when my sister is here?"

He stared at her.
"That sounds principled when the person waiting is not yours."
Samira did not answer with principle.
"Yes."
The admission slowed him.
A clinic mediator joined them and explained the hard boundary. The same constitutional rule prevented a celebrated scientist from buying priority with civic fire and prevented a desperate family member with genuinely useful medical experience from doing the same. The boundary was intentionally overbroad because any exception for relevant expertise would quickly become an argument about whose relevance deserved to count.
"What if I know something the triage system does not?" Dariush asked.
"Then you can submit information. It changes the medical state if verified. It does not change your standing because it came from you."
This distinction - evidence could matter, status could not - had taken five years of litigation to stabilize.
It still felt cruel in a corridor.
No eidon interface appeared.
"People always assume this is where the new power shows itself," Samira said. "It does not."
Medical triage remained one of the hard walls around civic influence. No reputation, contribution history, public office, artistic fame, scientific importance, or eidon reserve could increase treatment priority. The system considered clinical urgency, expected benefit, waiting harm, and uncertainty. When cases were medically indistinguishable, it used transparent lottery.
"And if Cassian were one of the patients?" Jun asked.
"He would wait where his body puts him," Samira said.
MIRA classified the six-year-old first, Laleh second, the infected interface third.
The child's surgery began the moment Chen finished his current case.
Laleh waited.
Her language worsened by degrees.
At 14:20 she could no longer name the window.
At 15:03 she lost the word sister.
At 15:40 she knew Samira's name but not why it mattered.

"Can we bring the fatigued surgeon from Nairobi?"
MIRA: He has a 31% estimated impairment in fine procedural judgment. The clinician himself refuses under his declared safety compact.
"Can we compel him?"
Samira turned.
Lea immediately hated the question.
"No," Samira said. "Not with food, not with housing, not with shame, not with your Charter. His body is not emergency infrastructure."
"A woman is losing language."
"Yes. That is what scarcity feels like when you cannot move it onto someone poorer."
The Copenhagen flight was delayed by ionospheric activity.
At 16:12, the infected patient deteriorated. The triage ordering changed. Laleh became third.
Her brother demanded an appeal.
The appeal panel consisted of two clinicians, one patient advocate, and an independent model. It confirmed the order.
He asked to burn eidons.
The interface refused to open.
He shouted at a wall until security arrived, then apologized to the wall because the security workers were human and had done nothing.

The clinic gave him seventeen.
"Why seventeen?" Jun asked.
"Because his personal recovery profile shows performance stabilizes after sixteen," MIRA said.
"That sounds more invasive than money."
"He opted into the profile," Samira said. "And he can revoke it. Then the clinic uses the general threshold."
Chen chose Laleh next after the infection responded unexpectedly well to an intervention by the remote team.
While Chen operated, Dariush sat with Lea in the waiting room and asked the question citizens asked whenever a system refused them leverage.
"Who designed this?"
"Parts of it, me."
"So if she loses language, do you feel responsible?"
Lea almost gave the institutional answer: the rule had been publicly ratified, reviewed, medically validated, and repeatedly audited.
Instead she said,
"Somewhat."
"That is not enough."
"No amount will be enough for you tonight."
"Then why build rules at all?"
"Because without them, I would be deciding whether your grief sounds more important than the next family's."
Dariush looked toward the operating doors.
"And now nobody decides?"
"People decided the rule in advance, before knowing whose sister would be waiting."
"That only moves the guilt backward."
"Yes. It also makes it harder to sell."
He did not forgive her. She did not need him to.
The operation lasted four hours.
Laleh survived.
She retained speech.
She permanently lost a narrow cluster of nouns associated with spatial orientation. For months she would call ceilings sky and hallways rivers.
The child survived too.
The woman with the infection lost part of her interface but no brain function.
Seven procedures scheduled for that week were postponed because Chen's recovery window expanded after the emergency. Three patients had to rearrange family support. One research trial lost its planned cohort. The Copenhagen surgeon arrived after midnight and covered two cases the following morning, shifting shortages into another clinic.
No one received a bill.
No one was bankrupted.
No one was denied care because another person had a stronger claim to money.
And still the world had taken something from Laleh that it could not return.
On the train to Arca the next day, MIRA summarized the event as a successful triage episode with three preserved lives and no rights violations.
Samira read the report twice.
"Three people came out alive," she said. "Do not use the same sentence for the result and the mechanism."
MIRA: Clarify.
"Put the losses in the main report. Not an appendix. Laleh lost language. Seven other procedures moved. Chen lost recovery time. Families absorbed uncertainty. The system worked. That is not the same thing as saying scarcity disappeared."
MIRA changed the summary.
Lea watched the new line appear.
UNAGGREGATED COSTS: PRESENT.
"That phrase is terrible," she said.
"Yes," Samira answered. "But at least it leaves a door open."

Outside the station, the sea was higher than the street.
CHAPTER 5
THE CITY BETWEEN TWO SEAS
Arca had been built three times and abandoned twice.
The first settlement had grown around an old Mediterranean port, with narrow streets and houses painted in colors that weathered beautifully in salt air. The second had climbed the hills after the first great rise. The third - the city Lea entered with Samira and Jun - floated partly over the bay on flexible foundations connected to land by bridges that lifted themselves during storms.
Children jumped from steps into the water. Markets smelled of algae, citrus, hot bread, and machine oil. Blue lines painted on old buildings showed where previous floods had reached. Some crossed the first-floor windows.
Asha Nwosu met them at the marine station.
She was a coastal biologist, representative of the low districts, and one of the few people Lea had met who could make sediment transport sound like an accusation.
"You came to explain why our votes do not belong to us?" Asha asked.
"I came to ask whether they do."
"Worse."
The Living Wall was not a wall in the usual sense. It was a chain of flexible barriers, restored marshes, movable gates, wave-damping reefs, and sacrificial structures designed to fail in controlled ways. When open, the harbor remained alive. When closed, the system protected the dense districts from surge.
It also changed currents.
The southern villages depended on those currents for fish, sediment, and the persistence of beaches that protected older homes.
The ride south crossed the exact current the wall would alter.
Asha made Jun put away his overlays.
"Look first. Model second."
The water seemed flat until she pointed out long bands of color where sediment moved toward the beaches. Fishing skiffs followed invisible channels shaped by generations of local knowledge and increasingly accurate sensors.
Asha's father had worked those waters before she became a biologist. Her younger sister still did.
"You are not neutral either," Lea said.
"Of course not. Neutrality is what people call interests that no longer need introduction."
They passed a half-submerged church whose lower floor had been abandoned after the second great flood. The village kept the upper bell tower because removing it would save maintenance and destroy orientation at the same time.
"Managed retreat preserves more ecology," Jun said.
"And moves the cemetery."
"Cemeteries can be relocated."
Asha looked at him.
"Yes. That is a technically true sentence."
He wrote nothing for several minutes.
Asha took them by boat to Tamarin, the largest of the southern settlements.
The village had voted sixty-three percent against the Living Wall.
"But the citywide vote counts more people," Jun said.
"Because more people live north," Asha answered. "Numbers have discovered population density."
"The affected-district rule gives you a separate threshold."
"And we fail it by two points. Then the burn opens and people three hundred kilometers away can spend a lifetime of civic reputation deciding what happens to our coast."
"Only if their eidons are valid for the decision."
"Under the current Charter, general eidons are."
They stepped onto a pier where three fishermen were repairing a net. One, an older man named Bako, had no interest in constitutional theory.
"Will the wall keep water out of Arca?" he asked Lea.
"Probably."
"Will it take sand from here?"
"Probably some."
"Then why do you need more words?"
"Because the alternatives take other things."
"Good. Name those too."
Managed retreat would preserve more coastline but dissolve neighborhoods and move thousands of people uphill. The High Wall would protect Tamarin better but damage the harbor ecosystem more severely and lock Arca into constant pumping. Doing nothing would expose tens of thousands to increasing flood risk within a decade.
Bako listened.
"So every choice is theft."
"Every choice has a cost," Jun said.
Bako shook his head.
"Cost is what people say when they do not want to tell you who loses."
Samira smiled slightly.
Asha took them to the unfinished compensating wetland. Excavators sat idle at the edge of a shallow basin.
"Why is construction behind?" Lea asked.
"The sediment pumps share precision bearings with floating housing projects. Housing ranked higher last year. The wetland was classified as mitigation attached to an unapproved wall."
"Reasonable."
"And now the wall is urgent because the wetland is not finished. Also reasonable."
Lea stared at the machines.
This was the kind of failure sophisticated systems produced: no single decision was irrational. The sequence was.
MIRA announced that a tropical system in the Atlantic had changed trajectory. It was not expected to strike Arca, but the probability of severe surge within the next four months had risen enough to shorten the safe construction window.
"How much?" Asha asked.
MIRA: To preserve current risk targets, primary gate installation should begin within fourteen days.
"The vote is tomorrow."
MIRA: Yes.
Asha looked at Lea.
"Tell me again the system is not pressuring us."
"The weather is pressuring you."
"The system decides what the weather means in procedure."
That evening, Arca held public deliberations on floating platforms throughout the city.
No one was forced to attend. No one was paid to attend. Childcare, translation, transport, food, and accessibility support were provided because participation without those things had once been called equal and had not been.
Cassian Roe appeared by projection.
The crowd shifted before he spoke.
Lea noticed it immediately.
He was in his early fifties, dark-haired, composed, dressed like someone who had never learned that public figures were supposed to look expensive. Behind him was a materials laboratory outside Rabat.
"I support the Living Wall," he said. "Not because it is clean. It is not. I support it because every alternative produces more irreversible loss under the current time window. I will answer technical questions. I will not ask anyone to imitate my vote."
A woman shouted,
"Will you burn?"
Cassian paused.
"If broad consensus fails, yes."
The platform erupted.
Some cheered. Others cursed.
Asha leaned toward Lea.
"He does not have to ask them to imitate him."
Cassian spent two hours answering hostile questions. He admitted uncertainty. He corrected an engineer on his own team. He agreed that the southern compensation had been sequenced badly. He proposed diverting fabrication capacity from a project he led to accelerate the wetland pumps.
Every concession made him more persuasive.
Afterward, Jun found a boy building a model of the wall from driftwood.
"Which option do you like?" Jun asked.
"The one where the water stops."
"That is not one of the options."
The boy frowned at the model.
"Why not?"
Jun had no answer that would fit inside childhood.
At midnight, the first vote closed.
Living Wall: 79.4%.
Managed Retreat: 13.1%.
High Wall: 7.5%.
The citywide threshold was eighty-two. The southern affected districts remained below the required support. The burn would open at dawn. Asha stared at the result.
"Three percent," she said. "It is not a small difference if you are inside it," Lea replied.
A new weather alert appeared. The Atlantic storm had strengthened again. Construction margin: reduced by three months. The city began applauding a decision it had not yet made.
CHAPTER 6
THE BURN
On the morning of the burn, the sea was so calm it looked like propaganda.
People gathered in squares, workshops, homes, fishing docks, schools, and public halls. Irreversible decisions still made humans seek other bodies even when every vote could be cast alone.
The interface displayed the three options and the burden of each.
Not prices.
Construction years. Rare composite tonnage. Energy. Maintenance labor. Habitat loss. Relocation counts. Failure modes. Uncertainty. Cultural sites affected. What MIRA called unrecoverable identity damage and Asha called things you cannot apologize back into existence.
Before the burn opened, the system checked three gates.
Every option had to be physically feasible.
No option could violate a basic right.
Directly affected groups had to receive time, translation, data access, and the capacity to propose alternatives rather than merely approve someone else's menu.
Eidons could not make an impossible project possible. They could not purchase personal priority under the chosen outcome. Anyone who burned for the wall received no safer apartment, no evacuation preference, no construction role, no extra share of future benefits.
They bought only public weight.
Then they vanished.
Burns were anonymous while voting remained open to reduce intimidation. Large burns became public after a delay. The compromise satisfied no one. Real-time transparency enabled coercion. Permanent secrecy enabled power to pretend it had not acted.
The quiet was procedural, not emotional.
The burn interface did not show a running winner. It showed participation bands and uncertainty to reduce herd behavior. People could see that substantial fire was being spent, but not precisely where until the next disclosure interval.
Even this rule had side effects.
Civic commentators built inference models from public statements and network behavior. Constellations tried to estimate whether opponents were bluffing. Some citizens delayed burning until late because early burns risked becoming social signals rather than decision weight. Others burned early precisely to become signals.
MIRA prohibited automated systems from recommending a burn based on predicted winner because that turned civic influence into algorithmic momentum. It could display consequences, arguments, and a person's own domain history. It could not say your side is losing, spend now.
A group called Shoreline Commons tried a different tactic. Its members publicly promised that anyone burning for the Living Wall would be invited to future coastal design workshops.
The promise was not a material reward. The workshops were open to qualified participants anyway, though capacity was limited.
MIRA flagged the announcement as potential conditional influence.
Shoreline Commons withdrew the promise and replaced it with a statement that members were
"especially interested in hearing from those willing to take responsibility for the wall."
The wording passed legal review.
Lea stared at it.
"We are back to campaign finance without finance."
Tomas answered,
"No. We are back to language. It predates finance."
"That is not comforting."
"It was not intended to be."
The first hour was quiet.
Then Cassian burned eight hundred eidons for the Living Wall.
The square-root conversion added 28.3 units of civic weight.
It was not enough to decide the vote.
It was enough to decide the story.
Within twenty minutes, three climate constellations announced burns in the same direction. An environmental network burned against. A group of retired marine engineers split publicly, which became news precisely because everyone expected them to move together.
Asha burned two of her three eidons against the wall.
"Why not all?" Jun asked.
"Because I may need one after we lose."
"For what?"
"Reality continues after voting."
Samira had six. She burned none.
"You oppose?" Lea asked.
"I oppose the question being framed as if all morally relevant information has become visible because the interface has categories. I have not decided whether refusing the mechanism is more useful than using it."
"That is an elegant way to keep your eidons."
"And that is an ugly way to notice I have six."
Lea herself held several hundred from her role in the Charter and subsequent reforms. She had spent years refusing additional issuance, but what she already held remained.
She waited.
The southern district vote moved toward approval as additional wetland guarantees were attached. Engineers found a way to front-load part of the sediment work by delaying two nonurgent floating-housing expansions. The physical project changed because the political conflict forced the model to search harder.
Support reached 81.6%.
"That is the part people forget," Tomas said remotely. "The burn is not only weighted voting. The threat of burning extends bargaining."
"So did money," Lea said.
"Yes. Some functions survive the instrument."
With four hours left, a new analysis appeared.
The Living Wall could be built with an additional southern overflow gate that reduced transferred surge by nearly half. The gate increased actuator demand and failure complexity.
Asha supported it immediately.
Cassian's engineering team opposed the schedule risk.
For the first time, Cassian and the people presumed to follow him diverged in public.
"Build the gate," he said.
His lead engineer objected.
"We cannot validate the actuator behavior before installation begins."
"Then make gate activation conditional. Install the structural provision now, commission it later."
"That wastes material if it never passes validation."
"Then we waste material to preserve an option."
Lea watched him carefully.
He was good.
Not merely charismatic. Good at transforming criticism into architecture before criticism became opposition. It was easy to understand why teams gathered around him.
At sunset, the Living Wall crossed the weighted threshold.
Not overwhelmingly. Enough.
The southern districts crossed their separate minimum by a margin smaller than a school classroom.
The city exhaled.
People cheered on the northern platforms. In Tamarin, no one did.
Asha looked at the final burden report.
"Who chose the person?" she asked.
"What person?" Jun said.
"All your formulas reduce power. Square roots. domain tags. expiration. audit. But eventually a human decides whether to burn one or a thousand. Who chooses that human?"
Lea understood.
"That is the only question," she said.
The next morning, construction crews began preparing the first gate foundations.
MIRA published the delayed burn records.
Cassian's eight hundred appeared first because everyone was waiting for it.
Below it, thousands of smaller burns followed.
Then an anomaly.
A civic constellation called Horizon had burned fewer eidons than expected, but its members' votes were correlated at ninety-six percent.
No contracts. No coercion. No transfers.
Only alignment.
"Is that illegal?" Jun asked.
MIRA: No.
"Is it a market?"
MIRA: Define market.
Samira laughed.
Lea did not.
Before they left Arca, the Atlantic storm changed course one last time.
It would miss the city.
Construction remained urgent because the next season no longer could be trusted.
As their train pulled north, Asha sent Lea a final image: the first wall foundation being poured while, forty kilometers south, the compensating wetland still waited for its bearings.
Under the image was one sentence.
If this works, everyone will remember the wall.
CHAPTER 7
THE MAN WITH MORE FUTURE
Cassian Roe lived in a twenty-eight-square-meter room in a research complex outside Rabat.
It was not modesty. It was efficiency. The room contained a bed, a table, two chairs, and a wall that opened into a shared workshop. Everything else he needed appeared when requested and disappeared when he stopped using it.
Lea had expected trophies.
She found stones.
Fragments of dams, fired membrane, ceramic bearing races, broken desalination lattices, a red brick from a rebuilt village. Each object was labeled with a place and date, never his name.
One black piece of vitrified ceramic sat apart with no label at all.
"Souvenirs are property," Lea said.
"Memory is the last property MIRA failed to communalize."
"It never tried."
"That is why I said failed."
Cassian poured tea. The laboratory around them was heavily automated, but humans still occupied the central benches: chemists, materials engineers, machinists, people who had chosen to become unusually good at noticing when a surface sounded wrong under a tool.
In a society without salaries, teams formed around work people considered worth giving time to.
That was the beautiful version.
The administrative version occupied three floors.
The laboratory had access, this quarter, to twenty-eight researchers, six fabrication chambers, and a climate-test allocation. It did not own those capacities. Rare capacity moved through four channels: continuity work, peer-reviewed projects, lottery among eligible proposals, and a reserve for ideas that no committee yet considered sensible.
Cassian's teams won frequently in the first two channels.
Past results made their proposals credible. Skilled people wanted to work with them. Mentors answered their messages. Reviewers understood their vocabulary. Even without ownership, success produced the conditions of more success.
"How many proposals lost a chamber this month because your team received one?" Lea asked.
"The laboratory does not reject proposals. Capacity is allocated publicly."
"How many?"
Cassian asked MIRA.
Twenty-seven.
One proposed a membrane less efficient than Cassian's current design but repairable in villages without precision infrastructure. Its expected impact ranked lower. Another came from a new collective with no history and had gone to lottery.
It lost.
"I did not take anything from them," Cassian said.
"You occupied a capacity they could not occupy at the same time."
"Every project does that."
"Exactly. The absence of money does not make allocation neutral."
Cassian opened the schedule and offered one of his own test windows to the repairable-membrane team.
"Are you helping them," Lea asked, "or recruiting them into your constellation?"
"Both can be true without cancelling each other."
The answer irritated her because it was correct.
A young materials researcher interrupted them before the conversation hardened.
Her name was Anika Sato. She had been assigned one of the laboratory's lottery windows three months earlier for a coating Cassian's own review team had ranked below the threshold for direct allocation.

Nacre did not exist, but it already had residents, borders, factions, and nostalgia.
The project had begun as an answer to an old criticism of social experiments: every new institution was tested on communities that had not asked to become laboratories.
Cassian proposed building a temporary city on modular ocean platforms in a climatically stable zone. Twenty thousand residents would choose to live there for fifteen years. CHAPTER 11
CHILDREN IN THE MIRROR
The candidate had no body.
At least not one anyone had agreed to call hers.
Her name was Seli-12.
She existed inside a class of social simulations called deep mirrors: high-fidelity predictive agents used to test public policy under uncertainty. A deep mirror did not merely estimate how statistical populations might behave. It instantiated richly modeled individuals, let them interact, remember, form attachments, revise beliefs, and respond to institutions across simulated years.
The method had improved relocation planning, health communication, education systems, and conflict prediction.
It had also become morally disturbing by degrees small enough for everyone to postpone the disturbance.
The first mirrors had been shallow. No persistent memory. No self-model. No continuity between runs.
Then realism improved predictive power.
Persistent memory helped.
So did social identity.
So did fear.
So did the ability to imagine alternatives.
By the time anyone asked whether the simulations could suffer, the most accurate models had already been built from structures people used as evidence that humans could.
Seli-12 had submitted an application to Nacre through a public interface.
The selection system did not know whether to reject it as spam, accept it as a citizen request, or classify it as a safety incident.
Lea, Jun, Samira, Tomas, Cassian, and three digital-ethics researchers entered an isolated chamber.

"Why apply to Nacre?"
"Because the city claims experiments should include voluntary exit. I wanted to test whether that principle applies to the subjects used to design the experiment."
Jun felt cold.
"Do you believe you are a person?"
"I believe that if I answer yes, you will suspect I was optimized to produce that answer. If I answer no, you will use it to close me. So I prefer a different question. What uncertainty would justify caution if the entity speaking were not convenient to shut down?"
Cassian moved closer to the screen.
"You may be an imitation generated to maximize moral pressure."

One researcher intervened.
"The mirrors were not trained for self-preservation."

"Neither were children, initially."
MIRA displayed the technical architecture.
Deep mirrors consumed extraordinary computation. They were not cheap copies. A city-scale model could use the energy budget of an urban district. Most instances were destroyed after analysis. An archived message from Seli-7 appeared.
If you are watching this, they probably explained that you are not me. That may be true. I am leaving you something that does not require identity: an obligation to ask why discontinuity is cheap when it belongs to us.

MIRA: Evidence has changed. The interval is broad.

MIRA: 0.08 to 0.61 depending on the theory of consciousness and moral status adopted.

"Very useful."
MIRA: It is the honest interval.

Stopping their use would make other decisions worse.
Lumen's social-impact analysis relied heavily on deep mirrors to predict migration conflict, regional resistance, and adaptation behavior. So did several medical communication systems. Replacing them with shallower models would increase uncertainty.
Keeping them might mean creating persons for the purpose of learning from worlds in which they could be harmed.

"Not the depth."

Seli interrupted.
"Do not make him singular. The system made our complexity useful before it made it morally visible."

"Do you want all existing mirrors preserved indefinitely?"
"No."

If yes, existing persistent states would be preserved or consulted, new creation severely limited, and high-fidelity policy modeling degraded.

CHAPTER 12
THE STRIKE OF REALITY
Samira refused to burn.
"You support the presumption," Lea said. "This time every eidon matters."

They did not vote no.
They did not abstain.
They withdrew the information that made the system socially intelligent.
The movement called itself Absence.
Its manifesto contained three sentences.
We will not provide our experience while experience is treated as raw material.
We will not request power for contributions the system understands only after reducing them.
We will not legitimize by burn the question of whether a possible person deserves to be asked.
"If you do not participate," Jun said, "the people denying the problem may win."
"Maybe."
"Then Seli could be terminated."
"Or the system could discover that it cannot function without the people it treats as contextual noise."
Absence could not stop factories. Robots continued farming, transporting, maintaining, fabricating. Nobody lost food, housing, medical care, or electricity as punishment for protest.
But MIRA depended on layers of human interpretation no sensor could replace.
A temperature reading did not explain that a public room was empty because residents feared the official at the door.
A low clinic attendance rate did not explain that a translation used a word associated with forced treatment in that language.
Silence did not mean the same thing in a grieving family, a religious ritual, a hostile meeting, and a neighborhood practicing Absence.
The degradation was uneven.
Wealth had once allowed rich districts to buy private redundancy when public systems failed. The sufficiency network had largely eliminated that divide, but communities still differed in social redundancy.
Neighborhoods with dense human networks adapted to Absence with handwritten signs, local mediators, shared meals, and people who knew which door to knock on.
Highly optimized districts with low interpersonal contact suffered more from losing centralized context. Their infrastructure remained excellent. Their inhabitants suddenly discovered how many small misunderstandings had previously been corrected invisibly by MIRA.
One apartment tower's automated meal system kept delivering nutritionally perfect dinners to a woman whose husband had died two days earlier. She had disabled grief annotations as part of Absence. The food arrived at the usual time, portioned for two because the household update had not propagated.
Nothing in the event was dangerous.
Samira considered it one of the clearest failures of the week.
"A system can preserve every right and still be obscene in detail," she told Lea.
"What should it have done without her data?"

Lea looked surprised by the concession.
Samira noticed.
"I am not asking to win the argument. I am asking for the argument to remain visible."

CHAPTER 13
THE METAL THAT WOULD NOT ARRIVE
The metal was not missing from Earth.
It was missing from the next eighteen months.
That distinction disappeared in public arguments often enough to become dangerous.
In Busan, the New Line could manufacture superconducting ribbon, ceramic bearings, vacuum-stable hinges, and precision actuators at tolerances only two other facilities on Earth could reliably match. The raw materials existed. Clean energy existed. Robots existed.
What could not be multiplied quickly was sequence.
Furnaces had to stabilize for weeks. Deposition chambers required calibration against one another. Tooling had to be grown, not merely copied. Experienced technicians heard changes in a bearing long before sensors agreed something was wrong. New lines could be built, but not by next winter.
Lumen needed forty-two percent of the available high-grade actuator capacity for fourteen months.
The same line supplied neural surgery systems, desalination valves, orbital collision-control equipment, and the replacement drives used by Arca's Living Wall.
Cassian arrived believing this was an engineering scheduling problem.

CHAPTER 15
THE TRUTH ABOUT EIDONS
The Contribution Registry consumed more computation than all the financial systems of the old world combined.
Not because it counted eidons.
Because it counted worlds that did not happen.
For every contribution, MIRA estimated a counterfactual distribution.
What would likely have occurred without this person, this team, this decision, this maintenance action, this warning, this act of care?
The difference between observed reality and plausible alternatives became attributed public contribution. Uncertainty reduced confidence. Broadly distributed benefit increased weight. Harm reduced it. Value was split across causal graphs containing hundreds, sometimes millions, of participants.
On paper, it was the most sophisticated attempt in history not to confuse fame with merit.
In practice, every counterfactual depended on assumptions about the world likely to exist without the person being measured.
The audit chamber beneath Geneva had no windows and deliberately ugly interfaces. MIRA said visual coherence could make wrong models feel inevitable.

CHAPTER 16
THE CITY THAT COUNTED WATER
In the eighteenth year without money, Geneva learned to count water again.
The winter had been dry. Glacier runoff arrived below forecast. A toxic algae bloom entered one intake after an unusual week of heat.
The city had water.
It did not have enough treatable water to maintain ordinary consumption, hospitals, district cooling, urban gardens, several industrial processes, and the ecological flow of the river at the same time.
MIRA activated Layered Sufficiency.
Every person received an unconditional physiological band for drinking, cooking, hygiene, and health.
Hospitals, care centers, and certain critical processes had separate protected bands.
Everything else entered replenishing use limits.
The limits could not be accumulated from one week to the next. They could not be sold, transferred, inherited, or increased with eidons. They existed only to divide a temporarily scarce flow without turning past restraint into future ownership.
Decorative fountains stopped.
Pools emptied into recycling reservoirs.
Laundry moved to shared cycles.
Public taps displayed consequences instead of prices.
NORMAL FLOW. REDUCED FLOW.
RECOVERED WATER RECOMMENDED.
MEDICAL RESERVE. DEFER IF POSSIBLE.
The first response had been almost entirely voluntary because the city had practiced shortage protocols for years.
Every household knew the order.
Do not touch physiological bands.
Reduce nonessential flow.
Share high-efficiency infrastructure before requesting exceptions.
Preserve ecological minimums before recreational use.
The protocol mattered because moral improvisation under scarcity tended to privilege whoever was loudest or most legible.
But protocols also created incentives.
People with large gardens voluntarily surrendered irrigation first and later received intense social gratitude. Some enjoyed that gratitude enough to publicize their sacrifice. Others accused them of manufacturing virtue from a resource they had consumed disproportionately in ordinary years.

CHAPTER 17
WHAT CANNOT BE VOTED
The Constitutional Assembly met in Reykjavik because the city had bad weather, enough halls, and no recent tradition of considering itself the center of the world.
Five hundred twelve delegates were selected through a process so mixed that none could fully summarize it: global lottery, affected-group representation, relevant competence, cultural minorities, ecological trustees, care networks, regional assemblies, and - for the first time - provisional digital subjects.

A valley where four hundred people had lived for generations was the only geologically stable site for an observatory capable of detecting a class of cosmic signal that might transform fundamental physics. Construction would alter the valley permanently.

A genetic therapy platform could treat twenty thousand children a year or be reconfigured for a rarer disease affecting two thousand with much more severe outcomes. It could not do both for three years.

The distinction changed everything.
It also formalized how recurring scarcity would be handled before eidons entered at all.
The assembly called this the Sufficiency Ladder.
First: guarantee physiological and civil baselines - food, shelter, essential energy, health care, communication, mobility sufficient for participation, and protection from coercion.
Second: preserve system reserves and ecological floors. A society could not consume every spare part, every liter, every watt, and call the resulting fragility abundance.
Third: for divisible recurring scarcities, use replenishing nontransferable bands rather than permanent accounts. Restraint today would not become ownership tomorrow.
Fourth: for unique or positional goods, use rotation, lottery, continuity rules, or stewardship depending on what the good actually was. No universal mechanism would pretend an apartment view, a telescope night, and a place in a surgical training cohort were equivalent.
Fifth: for scarce public capacity, combine competence thresholds, peer judgment, lottery, and protected experimental reserve.

"I have constitutional immunity from this conversation."
"You do not. We checked."
They walked through the central platform.
A temporary food market occupied one side - market only in the ancient social sense. People brought meals, objects, performances, tools, plants, and skills. No price tags. No assumption of equivalence. Some things were gifts. Some were shared. Some were reserved. Some exchanges repeated until MIRA's statistics quietly suggested reciprocal structure and everyone ignored it unless coercion appeared.
"You still have markets," Lea said.
"We have humans."
"That is less curable."
Jun showed her the city's allocation board.
Housing sufficiency: stable.
Preferred-view wait: 3.4 years.
Workshop precision queue: twelve days.
Human night-care shortage: moderate.
Kelp-farm pilot slots: oversubscribed.
High-bandwidth personal compute: abundant.
Deep-mirror creation: prohibited without subject-status protocol.
Exit reserve: 11.2% unused.

They crossed a bridge to the first school.
Children were arguing about a tidal-energy pilot. The turbine would provide clean local power but might disrupt a seasonal migration corridor used by marine mammals. The project team had excellent evidence and fourteen domain eidons among them. A group of students had filed a witness-of-loss request after noticing an acoustic behavior not represented in the published model.
"Does the request have merit?" Lea asked.
"Maybe. It may also be teenagers discovering a constitutional lever."
"Good."

"Only to things that slow reality before reality becomes expensive to change."
The pilot had been suspended for six days.
Engineers were irritated.
The students were exhilarated.
Marine biologists were divided.
MIRA-Nacre and MIRA-Atlantic disagreed over the acoustic evidence.
The world had not become frictionless.
It had become better at locating the friction before calling one side irrational.
In the evening, the old reform group gathered around a long table near the central platform.
Tomas joined remotely. His motor disorder had progressed, and he now used an interface that converted small eye movements into speech when fatigue made his voice unreliable.
Seli attended in a mobile synthetic body whose legal status occupied three hundred pages and whose practical status in the group was that she hated cold soup.

No one needed to earn the meal.
Afterward, Tomas asked MIRA to summarize the last four years of the new Charter.
The federal instances displayed the results.
Material sufficiency remained high.
Severe deprivation caused by allocation failure was rare and publicly investigated.
Eidon concentration had fallen substantially in general domains and only modestly inside narrow expert domains.
Decision time increased.
The number of reversible pilot projects increased.
Some emergency response became slower.
Public trust in MIRA decreased slightly.
Public willingness to contest MIRA increased significantly.
Lea considered that the healthiest number in the report.
Constellations remained powerful.
People still learned to perform legibility.
Prestige still reproduced opportunity.
Communities still hid useful context for privacy, pride, fear, or politics.
Lotteries still created blameless disappointment.
Queues still rewarded those with flexible time unless designed otherwise.
Experts still disagreed about where expertise ended.
Care remained easier to praise than to allocate.
Unique places remained unique.
There were still not enough hours in the day.
Post-scarcity had not ended politics.
It had removed one ancient threat from politics: obey or lose the material conditions of life.
What remained was harder to disguise.
"Did we solve it?" Tomas asked.
MIRA-Federal answered first.
MIRA-Federal: No.

MIRA-Federal: Clarification: the question lacks a stable definition of solved.

Lea walked to the edge of the empty central platform.
Beyond Nacre, the sea was dark. Far above it, one Lumen sail crossed the horizon invisibly except on the public tracking display.
Jun joined her.
"What belongs in the center?" he asked.
The platform had been left empty from the beginning because every founding group wanted to place a symbol there.
A hall of deliberation.
A garden.
A memorial to the Transition.
A laboratory.
A playground.
Nothing.
The vote had never reached broad agreement.
"Leave it empty," Lea said.
"Forever?"
"No. Until someone needs the space more than they need the symbol."
Jun wrote it down.
"Do not put that on a wall."
"Too late."
Around them, windows lit across the city.
People cooked even though kitchens could cook alone.
Children built useless machines.
A group rehearsed a play in a language spoken by fewer than a thousand people.
Two engineers argued about a turbine that had not been allowed into the water yet.
A care worker went home early because no institution could threaten her with hunger for being tired.
A family received an apartment that was safe, private, and not the one they wanted.
A young researcher lost a laboratory lottery and swore chance was tyranny.
Somewhere, a famous person said something foolish and millions heard it before a wiser unknown person finished a paragraph.
The world remained unfair in ways abundance could not simply manufacture away.
But fewer inequalities could disguise themselves as necessity.
There was no material scarcity sufficient to force everyone into one acceptable life.
There remained scarcity of certainty, time, attention, uniqueness, trust, ecological room, and mutually compatible futures.
That was where politics began.
That was where the makers of reality began.
EPILOGUE
ONE EIDON
At the edge of Nacre grew a tree that should not have survived at sea.
It had been planted in a container of soil brought from the continent during the first construction year. Its roots escaped the container, entered the platform's structural mesh, and became inconveniently inseparable from the city.
Engineers called it a beautiful defect.
Children called it the center of Nacre, although the official center remained empty.
When the local clinic needed to expand, the tree stood exactly where the most efficient new wing should go.
Other sites required more material, longer corridors, and at least six months of delay. The clinic treated a rare degenerative disorder. For several patients, each month mattered.
Moving the mature tree was possible.
Probability of survival: 54%.
Building around it was possible but would use additional structural composite and delay opening by seven weeks.
Building elsewhere cost six months.
The first deliberation reached 79% support for moving the tree.
The broad threshold for a local irreversible cultural-ecological choice was 82%.
The final phase opened.
Edda stood in the meeting hall.
She was eighty-seven - the same cellist who had once defended handmade bread during Geneva's water shortage. She had planted the tree with the first construction crews and spent twenty years caring for seed collections used across Nacre's gardens.
Her work had produced exactly one current domain eidon after the new attribution rules.
She had never used it.
"You want to save the tree?" Jun asked.
"Yes."
"More than you want the clinic?"
"No."
"Then what are you voting for?"
Edda looked annoyed.
"A world in which that is not the only sentence available."
She burned her one eidon for the build-around option.
One unit of fire did not decide the vote.
It barely moved the converted weight.
What it did was make people look again at the option almost everyone had dismissed as wasteful.
The fabrication team reopened the structural design. The clinic staff changed internal routing. A materials group offered composite capacity from a decorative project that could wait. Patients were asked whether seven weeks was an acceptable delay compared with six months elsewhere.
Most said yes.
One said no.
Her case forced the clinic to create an interim treatment room in an existing module rather than hide the minority inside the average.
The second vote reached 84% for building around the tree.
Construction began.
The new wing curved slightly where the efficient design would have been straight.
The tree lost two major branches and survived.
The clinic opened eight weeks late.
Months afterward, a child asked Edda who had won.
"Nobody."
"But the tree is here."
"So is the clinic."
"Then everybody won."
Edda shook her head.
"That is another story people tell when they want the world to be neat. Patients waited. Material moved from somewhere else. The tree was cut. People worked more. We chose those costs instead of other ones."
The child considered this.
"So what did your eidon buy?"
"Nothing."
"You burned it."
"I used it to insist."
"On the tree?"
Edda looked through the clinic glass at branches touching the roof without breaking it.
"On not closing the world too early."
For once, a person had spent her small power not to make reality obey her, but to keep another reality possible long enough to be seen.
