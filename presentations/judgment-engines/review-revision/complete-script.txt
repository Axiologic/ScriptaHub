What does a score permit?
An elegant explanation cannot rescue a code patch that fails its tests.
Judgment Engines asks how automated evaluators can earn authority over decisions.
Its central claim is that reliable judgment requires an engineered procedure.
The book connects explicit standards, checked evidence, and the consequences of a verdict.
Readers learn to examine the evaluator before trusting what its scores permit.

Make failure decisive
A flat average can let excellent presentation compensate for a critical failure.
The proposed alternative gives some criteria explicit dependencies or vetoes.
In the patch example, a failed test keeps release blocked.
Clarity can still be assessed without cancelling that functional failure.
The standard becomes an inspectable evaluation program, rather than a list of adjectives.

Evidence before opinion
The code chapters insist on running available checks before asking for opinions.
Tests provide behavioral evidence, but an existing suite can miss an important requirement.
Semantic review compares the change with its intended purpose and looks for missing cases.
These roles complement each other without turning the model into a pretend test runner.
The same division carries into evidence checking across the wider book.

Agreement can mislead
The evaluator itself then needs tests designed to expose misleading shortcuts.
A writing judge may reward length because longer answers once happened to be better.
Padding can break that connection while the judge keeps agreeing with itself.
Repeatability therefore differs from measuring the quality the task actually requires.
Likewise, instructions inside a candidate must not acquire authority over its assessment.

Keep judgment challengeable
When evidence is missing, a useful judge can abstain and request further review.
A meaningful appeal preserves the evidence and rules needed to challenge a verdict.
The book develops these responsibilities across software, science, education, and institutional decisions.
Start with Rubric Engineering and Decomposition to see how standards become operational.
Then follow the harness and governance chapters to examine what happens after scoring.
Read it when choosing an evaluator also means deciding how much authority to delegate.
