From Rules, Worlds: preserving the power to continue From Rules, Worlds: preserving the power to continue A research program connecting predictive memory, reusable operations and the evidence required to trust a simplified world. The obligation to continue From Rules, Worlds begins with a contrast between a mechanism that produces events and a storyteller who can simply announce them. Two accounts may describe identical visible outcomes while bearing different explanatory obligations. Saying that a ball passes through a gate does not supply the process that makes it happen. The book develops this intuition into a research program: what must a representation preserve so that its next claims follow from declared information and operations, rather than being introduced whenever convenient? This distinction does not divide existence into an absolutely lawful reality and an unconstrained realm of fiction. A text is itself a process, fiction may enforce strict conventions, and a real phenomenon may resist compression. Nor does physical dependence establish an efficient reduction between descriptive levels. A compact fundamental description need not make its consequences cheap to compute. Finite observations can also fit several mechanisms, leaving their ultimate identity unresolved. The program therefore asks operational questions without requiring agreement that the universe has a small computational core. Apparently changing rules require the same care. If rule updates and state updates are computable, their pair can be treated as an extended state updated by a fixed function, provided necessary inputs are included. This clarification proves neither finite memory nor efficient discovery of that function. New language, changed representations and changed mechanisms must remain distinguishable. The working proposal is narrower than a theory of mind: one component of understanding is the ability to continue correctly under relevant questions and transformations, within finite resources and a known domain. What a useful memory preserves A representation is sufficient only for a declared task. The research contract must specify the generator, observation channel, available actions, questions, horizons, error tolerance and resource budget. Changing these conditions changes which distinctions matter. A model that returns the final state is not automatically a substitute for one that must reveal the entire path. Likewise, a coarse prediction and an exact trajectory cannot be ranked fairly as if they delivered the same result. The central memory problem is to replace a growing history with an updatable predictive state. Different histories can share a state when their relevant future continuations agree. A modular counter illustrates why this may outperform retaining a fixed suffix: the useful information concerns a recurrent role, not simply the most recent symbols. Yet sufficiency at one moment is inadequate. The next representation must be computable from the current representation and permitted new inputs, without secretly retrieving the supposedly discarded past. This requirement is closure under updating. The observer&#x27;s predictive state need not resemble the generator&#x27;s hidden state in size or form. Partial observation may require a distribution over possibilities; eliminating velocity from a motion model can make position alone insufficient and create dependence on history. A simpler mechanism may thus be harder to predict than its short description suggests. Approximate memory merging adds another difficulty: closeness below a threshold need not be transitive. A chain of nearby histories cannot safely become one class without controlling error across the whole class. There is consequently no single best compression independent of purpose. The appropriate object is a cost–fidelity frontier: tradeoffs for which improving one declared resource or error requires worsening another. Accounting must include descriptions, tables, precision, storage and computation, rather than rewarding a short label that invokes an expensive mechanism. A capable system may move toward detail when questions change and toward compact representations when familiar tasks repeat. Forgetting becomes defensible when its lost distinctions do not undermine the promised continuation. When watching is not acting Two systems can look identical while responding differently to intervention. Two lights might follow a hidden common switch, or one might copy the other. Passive records need not separate these mechanisms, but forcing one light can. An abstraction intended for an acting agent must therefore relate actions as well as states. Compression optimized for observational prediction may discard precisely the distinctions needed to choose an intervention. Its causal cost is something to measure, not a defect that every compression must necessarily have. Prediction itself has several limits. Missing information, computational difficulty and difficulty inferring a mechanism are different problems. Accuracy must be specified over horizons and relevant cases; a favorable average may hide rare failures. A shortcut preserving a final answer may destroy intermediate behavior. General impossibility results also leave room for restricted classes where useful certificates exist. The question is always which task and guarantee are being claimed, not whether one representation deserves a universal label of intelligence or irreducibility. Controlled textual worlds extend this obligation to language. A sentence introducing a new rule must alter subsequent consequences coherently, including conflicts and unfamiliar combinations. Tests can separate extracting a semantic representation from executing it, compare paraphrases and require acknowledgement when the available information does not determine an answer. Similar wording may reverse a negation; different wording may express the same result. Operational meaning here concerns commitments about observations and actions. It does not exhaust human meaning, social intention, experience or consciousness, and success in these worlds would establish a bounded capability rather than a complete theory of understanding. Learning shortcuts without hiding their cost The program joins two kinds of compression. A predictive state answers what must be remembered; a reusable operation answers what calculation need not be repeated in its original form. Discovering a useful composition can enlarge the tasks an agent solves within its budget even when its fundamental computational primitives stay fixed. This functional novelty differs both from giving an existing operation a new name and from supplying an external service with a new capability. External assistance can be valuable, but it must be counted as assistance. Next-symbol prediction does not decide which memory mechanism performs the work. Context models, automata, hidden-state systems and other architectures can share that objective. Comparisons must distinguish extending a context from discovering recurrent state, and a supplied stack from a learned one. The same distinction applies to libraries: receiving useful abstractions demonstrates their utility, whereas discovering them also incurs search costs. A pretrained AI proposing an operation contributes prior knowledge; its success cannot be reported as discovery from local observations alone. Every retained operation needs a contract: accepted inputs, output meaning, exact or approximate equivalence, preserved intermediate observations, saved resources and behavior outside its domain. It may refuse or return to detailed computation when the contract no longer holds. A simple amortization model says that if discovery and verification cost C and each use saves a positive amount s, savings require N times s to exceed C. Real accounting must also include varying inputs, operation selection, storage and maintenance. A library of rarely reused answers can be less economical than the original procedure. Provenance would connect operations to their premises, observations, tests and failures, allowing dependent conclusions to be reconsidered when a premise changes. In text worlds, this means retracting affected beliefs while preserving independent ones, with unnecessary retractions and recomputation measured. Such a library would be a set of revisable commitments. Its value remains a research hypothesis: dependencies can be recorded incorrectly, and a proof about a formal model does not establish that the model matches the world. Statistical fidelity and formal property preservation retain different obligations. A program exposed to failure Constructed laboratories let researchers know the mechanism while restricting what the learner sees. Counters test recurrent memory; nested structures test stacks and composition; changing regimes test whether an agent recognizes returning patterns or continually relearns them. Supplying the correct structure provides a diagnostic benchmark. If it works but learning fails, discovery is the difficulty. If it also fails under hidden actions, information may be insufficient. If a learned representation consumes more resources than its comparator, its elegance has not become a system advantage. These laboratories need unfavorable cases, symbol recoding and controls without the sought regularity. Longer episodes with the same modulus do not establish the general concept of modular arithmetic. The research hypotheses are deliberately vulnerable: recurrent states may improve the cost–fidelity frontier; passive compression may sacrifice causal distinctions; reusable operations may cross a realistic amortization threshold; and useful memory may require both expansion and reorganization. Each can fail through lost rare distinctions, excessive search, costly maintenance, weak transfer or forgetting. Such failures identify the conditions under which a different architecture is preferable. The program also separates empirical promises from narrower propositions. Computable rule changes can be incorporated into extended fixed dynamics. Successive approximations with compatible interfaces have total-variation errors bounded by the sum of their individual errors, provided the later guarantee applies to what it actually receives. An exact deterministic predictive state must distinguish histories requiring different responses to the same future actions. These results clarify obligations and possible bounds; they do not prove efficient learning, certify operations outside their domains or identify an arbitrary world from finite observations. Evidence worthy of continuation Prediction quality, invariant-respecting generation, intervention effects and adaptation need separate measurements. Good probabilities do not ensure that a forbidden event never occurs. Zero observed failures are also not proof of impossibility: under independent trials with a common failure probability, the approximate upper bound at the stated confidence level after n failure-free trials is 3/n for large n. Dependent symbols from one trajectory cannot simply be counted as independent experiments. Proof, exhaustive checks over a bounded domain and statistical attempts to detect discrepancies support different conclusions. Fair evaluation separates training, model selection and final testing, then distinguishes new instances from new parameters and new mechanism families. Comparators need declared information access and resource accounting; ablations test whether each proposed component earns its cost. A claim register records exact assumptions, evidence and possible refutations, preventing a result in one family from becoming an assertion about universal understanding. Negative cases remain part of the contribution. Merely combining existing concepts is not scientific novelty; a new bound, algorithm, diagnosis or counterexample must do substantive work. Human and AI collaboration follows the same discipline. AI partners can propose models and counterexamples, but agreement among systems is not independent verification. Different methods, separated information and explicit responsibility matter more than consensus. Research should continue when it creates checkable distinctions, and narrow when advantages vanish after accounting or fail to transfer. The open question is not whether everything can be renamed as computation. It is what information and operations let a finite observer continue responsibly, what that ability costs, and which experiment would reveal that the representation has become too simple. What survives the initial intuition is therefore an obligation rather than a cosmological verdict. A representation may omit detail, invent a convenient internal vocabulary or take a computational shortcut, but it must preserve the consequences it promises and expose the conditions of that promise. The book leaves efficient state discovery, automatic contracts and the net value of an integrated architecture unresolved. Progress would consist of smaller achievements that can be checked: a state that updates correctly, a causal distinction recovered, a shortcut whose savings survive accounting, or a failure that identifies exactly what must be remembered next.
