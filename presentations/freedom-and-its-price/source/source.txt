Freedom that can be used The book begins with an imagined doorway. Several people have the same permission to pass through, yet their bodies, resources, dependencies and obligations make that permission unequally usable. When someone collapses nearby, their capacities to help also differ. The scene joins questions usually separated: what makes a person free, and what may others reasonably ask of that person? Freedom matters because people are vulnerable to one another’s power. Responsibility matters because actions, omissions and institutions shape lives beyond the actor’s own. Neither requires the fantasy of a completely independent individual. An open door is therefore a beginning. Noninterference protects against obstruction, capabilities concern what someone can actually do, and freedom from domination requires protection against arbitrary power. A kindly master still controls permission. Inner autonomy adds the ability to consider reasons and revise commitments. Time and reversibility matter too: an exit that remains available can protect a relationship even when nobody takes it. These dimensions cannot be replaced by counting trivial choices. Nor must every accepted commitment reduce freedom; a durable promise can enable action while becoming oppressive if reconsideration is made impossible. Traditions of inner liberation offer resources for enduring adversity, examining desire and acting within limits. The book reads them without treating diverse religious or philosophical traditions as interchangeable. A practice that strengthens resistance can also be imposed as training in submission. Similarly, psychological autonomy means more than immediate preference or relentless productivity: a person can freely endorse effort, dependence or delegation when the arrangement remains open to reflection. Silence may express learned futility rather than satisfaction. Preferences shaped by deprivation deserve both a hearing and genuine alternatives, not automatic dismissal by outsiders who claim to know the person’s real wishes. Permission, resources and power The history of liberty reveals who was allowed to count as a free person. Arguments once used to justify slavery or women’s exclusion could prevent capacities from developing and then cite their absence as proof of inferiority. Historical understanding does not erase the criticism available from excluded people themselves. Nor does abolishing a claimed right to own another person impose an injustice symmetrical with enslavement. Modern debates over security, consent, equal standing, harm and economic dependence expand the inquiry. They leave an enduring tension: individuals are socially formed, yet that fact does not make them collective property. Formal equality leaves reserves unequally distributed. The same financial mistake can be a nuisance for one person and a threat to food or housing for another. Money buys time to reconsider and recover. Research on scarcity, discrimination and residential opportunity helps identify mechanisms, but the book resists turning selected results into a universal psychology of poverty. Different experimental designs answer different questions; an offer to move is not identical to an actual move. Social explanation also need not deny effort or agency. It challenges the inference that an outcome transparently measures merit or that a successful person has justified the whole system. Work and citizenship make these distinctions concrete. A theoretical right to resign provides weak protection when departure is ruinous and complaints are futile. Workers need usable exit, voice and safeguards; responsibility for safety should come with authority and resources. The last person signing a form is not automatically the person who controlled the dangerous arrangement. Political life likewise requires more than choosing obedience or heroic disobedience. Procedures can exhaust complainants, public speech can be formally permitted yet effectively intimidated, and demands for participation can ignore limited time. Institutions should make challenge possible without assuming that every citizen can become a full-time investigator or bear unlimited personal danger. Responsibility within limits Debates about the brain unsettle simple stories of authorship without settling every practical question. Determinism is not fatalism, and randomness would not by itself make an action more genuinely mine. Laboratory findings about the timing of simple movements cannot establish that all deliberation is illusory. The book contrasts arbitrary experimental choices with consequential decisions while preserving the limits of that comparison. If ultimate responsibility requires creating the self from nothing, the demand generates a regress. Yet people can still respond to reasons, learn, exercise control and participate in practices of protection and repair. Rejecting ultimate self-creation does not require abandoning all accountability. Responsibility has several meanings that should not be silently exchanged. Causing harm, holding an assigned role, deserving moral blame, facing legal liability and being able to help are different relations. An innocent bystander may acquire a duty to rescue without becoming guilty of the accident. Retrospective judgment should consider control, accessible knowledge, foreseeable risk and deliberate ignorance; prospective duties also concern capacity to repair and the fairness of distributing the burden. Outcomes partly depend on luck. These distinctions support correction without humiliation and protection without revenge, while avoiding the opposite mistake of making an anonymous system responsible so that nobody has an identifiable task. The theological thought experiment pushes the relation between power and responsibility further. A creator’s greater capacity would strengthen, not erase, questions about preventable suffering. Appeals to free will must consider victims as well as wrongdoers; preventing an evil act need not remove the intention behind it. Stable natural laws do not by themselves demonstrate that every actual suffering is necessary. Compensation also does not automatically justify prior harm. These arguments do not decide whether God exists. They test proposed justifications and insist that victims cannot simply become material for someone else’s moral education. Uncertainty about ultimate explanations supplies no general excuse for human inaction. Dignity before performance The child reveals why dignity cannot wait for fully developed autonomy. Responsibility should grow with capacities, but rights are not rewards for obedience. Repairing a wrong differs from making shame the child’s identity. Adults must distinguish support from abandonment and appropriate risk from transferring burdens that children cannot understand. Evidence from delayed-gratification experiments also needs context: expectations about adult reliability and broader conditions complicate stories that reduce a life to willpower. Education should create credible opportunities to learn, including room for mistakes, while still protecting anyone harmed by the child’s actions. Homelessness exposes another misleading image of independence. Having no timetable may mean deprivation rather than freedom from constraint. Sleep, a stable address and secure shelter can turn nominal opportunities into practical possibilities. Assistance should not require a spotless biography, public gratitude or surrender to the helper’s beliefs. Services can have rules, but those rules need reasons, proportion and avenues of challenge. Evaluation must attend to safety, agency and housing rather than merely making visible suffering disappear. Shared public space creates real conflicts; a defensible response cannot reduce the vulnerable person’s role to becoming less noticeable. People who need help deciding retain the same basic claim to consideration. Communication difficulty is not proof of absent thought, and capacity may vary across decisions and time. Support should enable expression wherever possible rather than casually replacing the person’s will. Where uncertainty is profound, earlier wishes, clinical knowledge, burdens and careful procedures matter. The distinction between being a moral agent and being someone owed moral care is decisive: dignity does not depend on intelligence, usefulness or reciprocity. Carers also require support. Their exhaustion calls for sharing the work, not describing the dependent person’s existence as an excessive demand. Authority and its obligations The duties of researchers and philosophers illustrate the limits of authority. Research requires honesty about uncertainty, protection of participants and serious attention to foreseeable uses, without demanding that an investigator predict every consequence. Responsibility for dangerous applications belongs across the institutions controlling them. Philosophical inquiry owes readers clarity, fair treatment of opponents and awareness of what a simplified thought experiment leaves out. Neither discipline must prove immediate usefulness to justify existing. Neither receives universal political authority from expertise. The conditions that permit truthful work also matter: individual courage cannot permanently compensate for institutions that reward concealment. The book repeatedly asks who gains when a particular account of freedom or responsibility becomes dominant. Property can secure independence while restricting others’ access; family loyalty can sustain care while concealing unequal burdens. This question does not refute an argument merely by identifying its beneficiary. It tests what the language permits people to overlook. An especially troubling arrangement gives powerful people expansive freedom and vulnerable people expansive responsibility. Cooperation can benefit everyone, but that possibility does not establish that every actual distribution is fair. Claimed benefits need to be examined alongside control, burdens and the ability to refuse. Imagined intelligent machines extend the same inquiry. Assistance can enlarge a person’s capacities or quietly organize the available choices around someone else’s aims. A final click is not proof of meaningful deliberation. Reasons, alternatives, correction of data and effective challenge matter more than a ceremonial approval step. Responsibility concerns those who select objectives, deploy systems and arrange oversight, not simply the user nearest the resulting harm. Privacy protects space to reconsider; demands for transparency should scrutinize authority rather than expose vulnerable people indiscriminately. These are questions about possible arrangements, not claims that present machines possess consciousness or moral agency. Care without compulsory heroism Obligations to strangers are real even when they interrupt chosen projects. Severe harm that can be prevented at a small, accessible cost gives a strong reason to help; distance alone does not settle the value of a life. But an account of moral duty is not automatically permission for coercion. Effectiveness, consent, knowledge and competing relationships remain relevant. The book also rejects a verbal escape: a justified obligation can genuinely restrict freedom. There need not be a hidden benefit that makes every sacrifice disappear. The task is to justify and distribute such demands, not deny that they cost something. The conscientious person faces the opposite danger: becoming the permanent destination for everyone else’s unfinished duties. Emergency assistance can reasonably exceed an ordinary share, but emergency cannot become a staffing model. Rest, relationships and personal projects deserve protection before exhaustion, rather than receiving whatever time remains. Discomfort is not proof that a criticism is unfair, yet guilt can also be deliberately exploited. Hidden care often enables another person’s apparent self-sufficiency, and earlier consent does not authorize endless demands. Sustainable responsibility requires promises that can be kept, shared resources and legitimate refusal, without turning every inconvenience into exemption. A society worthy of these principles would need less compulsory heroism. It would align tasks with authority, make support and appeals usable, distribute care and learn from the experiences of people who cannot enter or choose to leave its institutions. Measures can reveal failures without compressing freedom into one supposedly natural score. Future people deserve consideration, but their interests cannot become an unlimited warrant to sacrifice those alive now. Returning to the doorway, the first need is to help; the next is to support the helpers and repair the conditions that made rescue necessary. Freedom becomes more credible when people can remain together without submission, and responsibility more credible when it asks for a reasonable share rather than universal salvation. The resulting position is a practical realism about freedom. Constraints, capacities and dependence have real effects, while the concepts used to assess them remain historically formed and open to criticism. A social construction can matter profoundly without becoming justified merely because it exists. Evidence helps establish what an arrangement does; moral argument must still explain what people owe one another. Keeping those tasks distinct allows revision without cynicism: listen to excluded experience, identify who controls the relevant conditions, protect dignity before performance, and ask whether the promised freedom can actually be exercised.
