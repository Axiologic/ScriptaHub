When dependence closes
Ecology of Predation asks when useful dependence becomes difficult to escape.
It follows resource capture from biological interactions into institutions and artificial intelligence.
The comparison is a bounded hypothesis, not a moral law borrowed from nature.
Its distinctive question is whether a relationship preserves another party’s ability to refuse and learn.
Readers can examine the conditions separating mutual benefit from increasingly one-sided dependence.

Follow the resource and the exit
A useful personal agent can accumulate memory that makes its owner increasingly dependent.
Changing providers may require more than exporting a collection of files.
The book asks whether learned functions, relationships, and active permissions can move too.
That is a specific mechanism through which convenience could make exit costly.
The example earns its biological comparison only when the resource, cost, and adaptation remain identifiable.

An analogy with conditions
The wider argument compares capture, counter-adaptation, cooperation, and the distribution of learning speed.
Biological patterns supply questions without deciding what human institutions should permit.
The proposed alternative protects understanding, refusal, apprenticeship, and meaningful changes of partner.
Its forecasts remain claims that future evidence could contradict.
This makes the book a framework to examine, rather than a universal explanation of conflict.

Read the conditions of freedom
Begin with the epistemic note, which states when the central analogy is legitimate.
Follow the agent example into the chapter on an ecology of freedom.
The full argument asks how useful dependence can remain negotiable over time.
It suits readers studying institutions, digital intermediaries, and the governance of artificial representatives.
Read it to identify which capacities a beneficial relationship must preserve in the other party.
