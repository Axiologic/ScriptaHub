The care that leaves the frame
Eden Was a Jungle examines how local goodness can export harm into the shared world.
Its opening follows an orchard saved by water diverted toward other people.
No villain is required for sensible private decisions to damage the valley.
The book asks what morality becomes when attention, cooperation, survival, and power remain unevenly distributed.
Readers enter a deliberately uncomfortable argument, then encounter the audit that tests its darkest claims.

Cooperation can enlarge the problem
The garden image challenges the assumption that cooperation automatically escapes competition.
A group may care deeply for members while passing costs beyond its boundary.
The book follows this tension through trust, states, political narratives, and technological power.
Its fictional situations make abstract mechanisms visible without pretending to be historical testimony.
These examples invite readers to examine whom an apparently generous arrangement leaves outside.

The dark argument faces its examiner
The final audit refuses to equate successful survival with moral entitlement.
A theory that reinterprets every kindness as predation may become impossible to disprove.
The book also asks how ruthlessness destroys trust and information that continued success requires.
This internal challenge matters because cold language can disguise an emotional preference for darkness.
The full reading experience holds the force of the thesis beside its limits.

Read the orchard and the audit
Begin with the orchard, where ordinary care produces consequences beyond its reach.
Then carry that problem through the larger institutions examined in the chapters.
Read the final audit as an essential part of the argument, not an optional qualification.
The book leaves a difficult question about goodness that can defend what it values.
Read it to examine moral power without granting either innocence or ruthlessness an automatic exemption.
