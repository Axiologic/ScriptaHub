# Too Convinced to Stop — targeted script and original art proposal

Status: pending root script/art review; no production changes.

Purpose: English book-first invitation,120–180seconds,16 preserved clips and4 replacements.

## Promise and source boundaries

WHY: ambition requires incomplete evidence, but evidence must still change consequential decisions. HOW: compare decision structures, separating outcome, sincerity, support and process integrity. WHAT: practical questions about disconfirmation, direct reporting, preserved records, reversible commitments and who bears costs.

The folding-chair prototype is an explicitly hypothetical illustration of source19/31/33/185/187/188/193/196, not a reported historical case or a safety instruction. One adverse observation is not proof of fraud, pathology, permanent impossibility or eventual commercial failure. Reporting alternatives are possible responses to the SAME report, not a controlled outcome study. No numeric specification or real personality is introduced.

## Complete proposed speech

### Scene1 — Too Convinced to Stop

1. Too Convinced to Stop asks how ambitious beliefs can remain answerable to evidence.

2. The book judges the process before success or failure rewrites the story.

3. It follows founders and institutions through confidence, authority, dissent, and the distribution of costs.

4. Its category of impossible certainty is explicitly nonclinical.

5. Readers gain questions for distinguishing bold experimentation from organizational protection against correction.

### Scene2 — Evidence can fall into debt

1. Imagine a team developing a folding chair before its claimed load is independently tested.

2. The book asks whether that interval buys learning or merely extends the protective story.

3. Evidence debt grows when confidence outruns what a project can presently substantiate.

4. A disappointing test helps only if its findings can still change the plan.

5. The distinction concerns how belief behaves under pressure, not whether ambition looks unusual.

### Scene3 — Judge before the outcome

1. Success can make earlier recklessness look wise, while failure can erase a responsible experiment.

2. The book therefore separates outcomes from the quality of decisions available beforehand.

3. The same adverse finding can reach decision makers directly, or disappear behind a protective story.

4. A failed venture is not automatically fraud or evidence of psychological pathology.

5. These distinctions make its organizational cases useful without reducing them to stories about exceptional personalities.

### Scene4 — A test must be allowed to matter

1. Begin with the nonclinical definition and the distinction between confidence and resistance to correction.

2. Then follow the cases alongside questions about evidence, dissent, reversibility, and distributed costs.

3. The practical chapters propose explicit tests, records, and protected routes for unwelcome information.

4. The original claim and failed test remain visible when a narrower proposal replaces them.

5. Read Too Convinced to Stop to examine ambition before hindsight decides which story everyone tells.

## Original art — a prototype that can answer back

Palette: terracotta upholstery, cobalt metal, pale birch, plum ink and small acid-yellow testing markers. Quiet backgrounds, one player heading, expressive makers with stable identities. No angry-villain shortcut.

Scene1: full workshop in three-quarter view. A maker assembles a real folding chair with hinged paired legs and a seat support; a second technically capable participant prepares a loading cradle. A physical tag reads “Agreed test load”, with no invented numeric rating. Empty record space awaits observation. Maker steps back so the observer can reach the mechanism. This enacts confidence creating a test before hindsight supplies a verdict. The same chair/tag persist. No diagnostic or criminal imagery under the nonclinical line.

Scene2: close material view of hinge and cradle. Speech explicitly introduces the imagined chair. A generic load descends in a supported sling; the joint bends and seat tilts during this illustrative test. The observer records “Not met” beside the unchanged “Agreed test load”, without a verdict about the company. Preserve criterion before observation. At the last two lines the record moves into a direct review tray and the maker pauses assembly. No repair or future market outcome.

Scene3: change from workshop to a wall-height publicity silhouette under “Later story”, alongside the dated test record. A hand moves the publicity sheet aside to recover the earlier record, explaining why hindsight cannot replace process evidence. At line3 show TWO POSSIBLE routes sequentially: the same report stops at a management-held cabinet, then the scene explicitly resets and the observer carries it through a separate review hatch to a seated decision maker. A physical “Possible response” placard marks the alternatives. Report wording stays identical. At line4 reviewer studies the finding without stamping fraud or diagnosis; the prototype remains on a low trolley. This compares reporting architecture, not two observed companies or guaranteed success.

Scene4: intimate review table with an open evidence ledger holding original tag and adverse result in sequence. A narrower proposal is added beside them, “Revised proposal / Not yet tested”; previous pair stays visible. A hand draws the chair trolley back from the dispatch threshold toward testing, preserving a reversible next step. An actual open book at the side has a tab “The test before the outcome”. End on retained original evidence and an empty new test slot. No claim revised proposal works or every venture ought to continue.
