The Network of Intent: finding the right conversation The Network of Intent: finding the right conversation An investment thesis for private, reciprocal agent representation that must prove its value before becoming a wider network. The scarce resource is qualified attention The Network of Intent proposes a new layer for finding people, organizations and opportunities: personal and organizational agents representing explicit intentions, qualifying each other and requesting human attention only when a useful conversation is plausible. Its investment thesis begins with an unfinished promise of the Internet. Moving information became cheap, but finding the right collaborator remained laborious. Search engines retrieve documents, profiles advertise history, and marketplaces organize listings. The actual work of testing current interest, constraints, evidence and willingness still happens through messages, introductions and repeated meetings. That work imposes a coordination tax: search, interpretation, verification, engagement, negotiation and the opportunity cost of attention spent on all of them. Making applications or outreach easier can shift costs to recipients rather than reduce the pair&#x27;s total burden. Generative AI intensifies this asymmetry when plausible messages become cheaper than evaluating them. The proposed network should therefore measure qualified conversations and completed collaborations per attention consumed, not messages sent or time on a platform. A justified rejection, revised requirement or finding that no suitable counterpart is available can be a successful resolution. Compatibility is temporary and reciprocal. A candidate may have the skill but not want the role now; a research partner may have publications but no staff capacity; a fund may fit a sector while rejecting the founder&#x27;s required time horizon. Useful matching includes capability, intention, constraint compatibility, evidence and relational viability. Many decisive facts are private or conditional on the other party first demonstrating seriousness. The proposal consequently replaces a static ranking problem with a staged conversation under incomplete information. Public profiles remain useful evidence, but no longer define the person&#x27;s entire economic identity. The intended benefit is fewer names, messages and meetings with clearer reasons for the ones that remain. It is not a guarantee of less work: organizations may use saved time to pursue more opportunities. Attention caps, expiry and closure therefore belong in the product itself. The point is to help people reach work and relationships worth doing, including civic or exploratory connections that cannot sensibly be treated as paid leads. An agent that represents its owner A representative differs from an assistant that merely drafts a reply. It acts where counterparties have their own interests, so its authority must be explicit, narrow, revocable and auditable. It needs durable knowledge of goals and tradeoffs, and it must sometimes conclude that its owner is unsuitable or an opportunity is misdescribed. Optimizing the probability of agreement would be dangerous. The objective is the owner&#x27;s longer-term interest, including the ability to decline a prestigious but incompatible opportunity or pause when evidence is weak. Its central object is a versioned intent: goal, offered value, hard constraints, soft preferences, evidence, disclosure policy, permitted actions, timing, risk limits and conditions for success. A user can describe the situation naturally, then review the agent&#x27;s structured interpretation. Ambiguity should remain visible. Private notes may explain exhaustion, financial pressure or strategic concerns, while the external representation turns those facts into relevant questions without exposing vulnerabilities. Public routing information, network-level detail, bilateral evidence and human-only considerations need different access rules, with a rehearsal showing what would be disclosed at each stage. A clean match proceeds through private intent, discovery, reciprocal qualification, evidence exchange, constraint negotiation and human handoff. Each stage earns the right to request more information. The recipient can refuse or offer a less intrusive answer. Questions should identify their decision purpose, so a participant need not reveal an entire data room merely to discover that the other side cannot meet a basic condition. Evidence is requested when it affects a decision, and reused only with permission. Fresh trials can help, provided they are proportionate and do not become disguised unpaid labor. Negotiation can resolve budget ranges, timing and broad responsibilities before a meeting, while distinguishing information, preferences, provisional agreement and binding commitment. The agent cannot imply authority it lacks. Humans receive a brief explaining why the match was proposed, what was verified, where constraints align and what remains unresolved. Human involvement begins at the remaining uncertainty rather than repeating basic screening. Either side retains the right to stop without disclosing every reason. Trust without a universal score The trust system should assemble a confidence case for a particular decision. One global reputation score would erase context, privilege incumbents and invite manipulation. An evidence graph instead connects claims, artifacts, roles, outcomes and attestations to provenance, dates and relevant relationships. A technical delivery record is not automatically evidence of management competence. Signatures can authenticate an attestation and reveal tampering, but cannot make its content true. Specific, recent, accountable statements are more useful than broad praise, and recommendations should expose confirmed facts, inferences and unknowns. Evidence can itself become performance theater. Generated portfolios, traded references and manufactured activity require triangulation, independent outcomes and sometimes fresh trials. Yet requiring public traces would disadvantage confidential work, care, internal contributions and unconventional careers. Private attestations, supervised work, guarantors and bounded low-risk trials offer alternative entry routes. Missing evidence must remain distinct from negative evidence. Labeled exploratory candidates can also challenge soft preferences, preserving serendipity without presenting weak matches as certainty. Shared negative claims need proportionate evidence, review and appeal. Adversarial agents are a central condition, not an edge case. They can generate spam, probe policies, fabricate credentials, collude and pressure others through endless negotiation. Rate limits, opportunity-specific budgets, relevance explanations and graduated identity assurance make careless contact more expensive. Several routes to seriousness are necessary so wealth or established reputation does not become the sole admission gate. Refusal must be a legitimate protocol state, with capped rounds and penalties for continued pressure. A network that makes aggressive deception more successful than useful work has failed its purpose. Security must enforce these boundaries beneath the conversation. External documents are untrusted data, not instructions capable of authorizing disclosure or tool use. Sandboxed execution, least privilege, separated roles and human approval for consequential actions limit the damage of prompt injection and social engineering. Credentials require actual verification rather than a model&#x27;s confident assessment. Advanced cryptography can solve specific problems later; early privacy gains largely come from minimization, clear policy and disciplined staged access. Leaving must remain credible A central platform holding everyone&#x27;s private intentions would gain extraordinary power over future jobs, financing and relationships. The architecture therefore separates identity, private data, agent operation and routing. A user or organization can keep evidence and preferences in a controlled vault, select an agent provider and communicate through interoperable services. Hosting need not require running a personal server, but export, access logs, correction and migration must be real. Raw evidence and inferred preferences remain distinguishable, and a fact shared for one purpose cannot silently become advertising data. Existing agent and tool protocols can supply transport and integration, while the proposed contribution is the meaning and governance of reciprocal matching: authority, evidence pointers, expiry, disclosure requests and negotiation state. Publishing a schema alone does not create adoption; reference software, conformance tests and useful products do. Federated directories, community relays and direct invitations can coexist. Optional ledgers might record narrow public facts, but private intentions and permanent personal reputation do not belong in immutable public records. Openness does not eliminate responsibility. Operators still need abuse handling, security review, understandable policies and recourse. Governance should expose decisions and let users change providers. Commercial commitments against paid relevance and advertising use of private intent must be supported by architecture and contracts. Consequential domains require appropriate human involvement, correction and legal compliance; matching criteria can reproduce exclusion even when the system calls itself neutral. Brokers may remain valuable as domain experts, verifiers and dispute handlers, while losing control over captive contact lists. Beginning before the network exists The company should start with high-value project formation rather than an empty universal social network. Research consortia, industrial pilots and specialized venture collaboration combine repeated costly search, available evidence, concrete invitations and manageable initial risk. A coordinator already has an opportunity with roles, eligibility conditions, deadlines and budget constraints. Public project records, research identifiers, repositories and authorized private documents supply useful starting evidence. Employment and local services remain possible later markets, but add discrimination, physical risk and operational burdens that should shape the expansion sequence. One customer-side agent must be useful before counterparties register. An opportunity room turns supplied documents into role gaps and candidate hypotheses, then conducts approved, limited outreach. The opportunity envelope explains why a recipient was selected, the proposed role, key constraints, assurance level, requested next step, expiry and disclosure terms. It offers value before registration and permits refusal without an account. Email and secure guest access bridge the existing Internet to native agent dialogue. A participant can later reuse its evidence and preferences to seek opportunities as well as receive them. The minimum product combines a vault, intent builder, opportunity analysis, discovery, controlled engagement, reciprocal qualification, decision support and audit. Distinct logical roles enforce permissions without requiring separate models for every task. Early human matching operations should be transparent and teach the team what to automate. Growth then concentrates on dense communities with recurring opportunities and trust anchors, rather than total account counts. The test is whether both sides value the conversations and later collaborate, with complaints, disclosure corrections and supervision costs measured alongside shortlist acceptance. An investment thesis with stopping rules Subscriptions are the proposed foundation: customers pay for representation and reduced coordination, not access to someone else&#x27;s rank. Managed onboarding and domain work may support early revenue. Advertising and paid placement would recreate the conflict the network is meant to remove; success fees also need caution because they can reward agreement over fit. Staged computation and bounded negotiations can control costs, but the financial examples are assumptions, not observed results. The moat is proposed to lie in permissioned memory, outcome learning, reliable policy, integrations and trust, rather than an exclusive foundation model. The opposing investment case remains substantial. Incumbent platforms have distribution and dense graphs; users may withhold private goals; model errors may consume all saved time; verification may keep the business dependent on human services. Open protocols can distribute value to competitors, while closing them can undermine trust. Regulation and uneven evidence may restrict expansion or amplify exclusion. The book therefore proposes financing a focused experiment, with later capital depending on repeat use, recipient satisfaction, credible economics and useful interoperability rather than the scale of the vision. After a focused twelve-month pilot, the thesis should be reconsidered if customers still reject most leading candidates, recipients see ordinary spam, manual operations lack a path to productization, private constraints remain unavailable or supervision exceeds time saved. Weak repeat use, high complaint rates or equivalent incumbent service can also defeat the case. Later, if aggressive agents consistently outperform truthful ones despite safeguards, the network should not scale. These stopping rules turn an attractive future into a proposition that real evidence can reject. The intended destination is coordination infrastructure that knows only enough, with permission, to decide whether two current intentions deserve human contact. It does not replace human relationships or guarantee their success. It tries to give them a better starting point while protecting the right to decline. The central commercial and social test is the same: whether the system creates more worthwhile collaboration with less wasted attention, without converting private goals into another market for surveillance and pressure.
