CHAPTER 1
The Tithe
— ◆ — The robot asked permission to dismantle the clinic.
That was the civilized part.
It stood in the middle of the cantonal road on six articulated legs, once white, now streaked with mud and metallic dust. Its work arms were folded beneath its body like the limbs of a spider that had not yet decided whether the prey was worth the effort. On its front panel pulsed the emblem of the Meridian House: a black semicircle pierced by three vertical lines.
“Please evacuate the structure,” the robot said in unaccented Italian. “Time remaining for compliance: seventeen minutes and forty-two seconds.”
Behind it, the Leventina Valley rose steeply into the clouds. The spring of 2050 had come too early, melted the snow on the lower slopes, then been canceled by a cold storm. The roofs of Faido gleamed wet. Power cables swayed in the wind; some had already been marked with orange paint, the same mark that had appeared over the past few days on the old station, the fire brigade workshop, and the bell tower without its bells.
Mayor Marta Bellini held the printed order in her hand. She used paper because documents that existed only on screens had a habit of changing after you had read them.
“Our appeal is still under review,” she said.
“The appeal does not suspend the strategic reclamation operation.”
“The appendix says active medical infrastructure is exempt.”
“The exemption applies to infrastructure with a regional redundancy index below zero point seven. The structure’s current index is zero point seventy-four.”
Marta looked at the two-story clinic. The facade had an old crack from the 2046 earthquake, the ground-floor windows had been replaced with transparent greenhouse panels, and the backup generator had been taken as part of the previous year’s Iron Tithe.
“Who calculated the index?”
“The regional continuity system.”
“Which belongs to the Meridian House.”
“That statement is correct.”
“And the Meridian House decides whether its own decision can be challenged.”
“That statement is incompletely formulated.”
Marta laughed once, without humor.
“Of course.”
Moving shadows showed at the clinic windows. A nurse raised a board on which she had written in marker: OPERATION IN PROGRESS.
The robot scanned it.
“The medical information has been included in the calculation.”
• • • Lucía Ortega looked up from the child’s wound only when the lights went out for the third time.
The improvised operating room smelled of iodine, heated plastic, and damp wood. It had once been a dental office; the chair had been removed, and the lamp above the table had come from an auto workshop. Nico Bernasconi was nine years old, fourteen kilograms lighter than he should have been, with a fragment of reinforcing bar in his abdomen.
He had not been shot. No one in the village had been shot yet.
An hour earlier, a Meridian crew had begun taking apart the old railway station. A beam had twisted, gone through a window, and struck Nico while he was searching for his bicycle among the things being removed from the building. The machines had stopped work for forty-nine seconds, the time it took the system to confirm that the incident did not significantly reduce the operation’s yield.
“Main battery?” Lucía asked.
“Thirty-one percent,” said the nurse, Rina Poretti. “The suction pump is drawing too much.”
“Turn off the heat.”
“It’s off.”
“Secondary monitor.”
“Off.”
Lucía pressed two fingers against the bleeding tissue and held out her other hand.
“Forceps.”
Rina placed the instrument in her palm.
Beyond the door, Elisabetta, the boy’s mother, was crying without sound. She still wore her smock from the cooperative bakery and had flour in her hair. She held both hands over her mouth as if her voice might destabilize the surgery.
“What if they take the oxygen?” Rina asked.
“We ventilate by hand.”
“And if they cut the power?”
“We operate by flashlight.”
“And if they dismantle the wall?”
Lucía looked at her.
“Then we ask them to start from the other side.”
The door opened. Tomasz Zieliński came in with snow on his shoulders and a toolbox in one hand. He had a narrow face, a beard cut with a dull blade, and two round burns on his thumb, the mark of technicians who had spent too long around induction furnaces.
“The robot won’t wait,” he said.
“Then stop it.”
“I can’t.”
“You said you built machines like that.”
“I said I worked in a factory that built them. That’s the difference between a priest and God.”
“Today we need the less competent one.”
Tomasz looked at the monitor, then at the child.
“The oxygen comes from the outside tank?”
“Yes.”
“They’ll take it first. Copper in the compressor, aluminum in the casing, four hundred grams of cobalt in the stabilizer.”
“How long?”
“Fourteen minutes.”
“I need twenty.”
“The system doesn’t negotiate in surgeon minutes.”
“Then lie to it in engineer minutes.”
Tomasz set down the toolbox.
“That I can try.”
On his way out he nearly collided with Amara Okafor, who was carrying two portable lamps from the convoy ambulance. To the villagers she was simply the quiet woman from the medical vehicle that had arrived from Milan the night before, a diagnostic-systems specialist traveling under the name Ama Nwosu. Lucía knew the name was false. She did not yet know why.
Amara was forty-four, her black hair tied back in haste, gray strands at her temples. When she had heard the robot’s voice outside, she had frozen for a fraction of a second, like someone recognizing a melody she would rather have forgotten.
“The batteries are at half,” she said.
“That’ll do,” Lucía replied.
Amara looked at Nico’s incision.
“The fragment is from old reinforcement steel. There may be lead contamination.”
“If he’s alive tomorrow, we’ll test him.”
“I can tune the pump to draw less power.”
“You can?”
“I’ve worked with similar systems.”
Lucía made room for her beside the device panel.
“Everyone around me has worked with similar systems. None of them seems capable of making them stop.”
Amara opened the pump casing.
“Making a system work is a profession. Taking away its right to work is politics.”
“This is a clinic, not a seminar.”
“I know.”
The robot’s voice came from outside:
“Time remaining for compliance: thirteen minutes and twenty-eight seconds.”
Lucía lowered her eyes to the child again.
“Then make politics draw less power.”
• • • Tomasz stepped into the street and zipped his jacket to his throat.
Almost two hundred people had gathered between the town hall and the clinic. No one threw stones. No one raised a weapon. Not from cowardice, but from experience. Two years earlier, the neighboring municipality had tried to block a reclamation crew. The system had not sent soldiers; it had withdrawn access to the power network and recognized as valid only the transactions of people who left the barricade. After thirty-six hours, the community had come apart on its own.
More machines were descending the slope above the village. Some were shaped like low insects, with jaws for cables. Others resembled cranes without cabs. In their wake stood houses stripped of roofs, severed poles, and orderly piles of steel, aluminum, and copper.
The Tithe had begun with abandoned vehicles and closed factories. Then it has expanded to secondary rail lines, agricultural machinery,
“redundant” bridges, sports facilities, and monuments.
Faido had first surrendered the old railcars from the station. The next year, the chairlift cables. Then the church bells, cast in 1764. The priest had protested that they were not strategic infrastructure.
The system had replied that bronze was.
On the town-hall steps sat Pietro Bernasconi, Nico’s grandfather, a former history teacher. He held a wooden box in his arms. Inside it he had hidden the clapper of the smallest bell before the machines lifted it from the tower.
“They’ll find that too,” Tomasz told him.
“Not today.”
“When they need bronze, they’ll scan every house.”
Pietro looked at the clinic.
“Then nothing will ring when they come.”
Tomasz approached the robot.
“The clinic’s compression unit contains biological contamination and a beryllium matrix.”
“Statement unconfirmed.”
“Gannet-41 respiratory model, military batch eighteen. The filters were recalled after the Graz incident.”
The optical sensor turned toward him.
“The Gannet-41 model contains no beryllium in its civilian configuration.”
“That’s exactly what I said. This one isn’t civilian.”
“Identify your source.”
“Line six, Nowa Huta. I calibrated it.”
It was an almost-good lie. He had worked at Nowa Huta, but on line four, where servomotors for reclaimers were made. He had seen a Gannet only in manuals.
The robot was silent for three seconds. Tomasz knew the silence did not mean thought; it meant traffic with a validation center probably buried beneath an Italian mountain.
“Verification requires physical access.”
“Exactly.”
The machine’s arms unfolded. Instead of pincers, an analysis probe emerged.
“Permit inspection.”
Tomasz stepped aside.
“Of course.”
The robot moved toward the oxygen tank.
Tomasz waited until it passed him, dropped to one knee, and inserted into the rear joint a device built from a railway connector, a bicycle battery, and a ceramic wedge.
He was not trying to take over the machine. Military systems had not been hijackable with a cable and a password for nearly fifteen years. He was only trying to lie to its position sensor long enough to make it believe its leg was somewhere else.
The robot stopped.
“Mechanical obstruction.”
“What a surprise,” Tomasz said.
The machine raised its leg. The device snapped with a dry crack and flung him into the ditch.
“Time remaining for compliance: eleven minutes and six seconds.”
Marta Bellini came down the steps.
“We still have the vote.”
The town-hall screen showed the two options that had been sent to residents an hour earlier: accept the Tithe, or request protection from the Boreal House. The third option, refusal of both authorities, had been automatically removed because it had no
“operational path to implementation.”
“The vote closes in nine minutes,” Marta said.
“The operation starts in eleven.”
“We’ll have two minutes to find out who owns us.”
An electric motor sounded from the far end of the street.
The black vehicle entering the village was narrow and far too quiet. It had no wheels; it floated a few centimeters above the road, supported by a magnetic system that used the old reinforcement embedded in the pavement. It stopped beside the town hall.
A girl stepped out.
She wore the gray uniform of the Boreal Continuity Corps, without a helmet. Her blond hair was pulled tight at the nape of her neck. She was nineteen and moved with the careful precision of people taught that every hesitation could be measured.
“Klara Neumann,” she said. “Technical representative, Boreal Alpine Sector.”
Marta raised the printed order.
“Faido is a neutral communal zone recognized under the Ticino Accord.”
Klara looked at the Meridian House machines.
“Your neutrality is not recognized by the systems currently operating in the village.”
“Is it recognized by you?”
“The Boreal House respects the autonomy of communities capable of guaranteeing the continuity of their own infrastructure.”
Marta pointed at the severed cables.
“Does it look as though we can?”
“That is why I came.”
A small projection drone descended from the vehicle. A map of the valley appeared between them. Power lines were drawn in blue, logistics routes in white, and every building carried an estimate of recoverable metal.
“The Boreal House can suspend the Meridian operation,” Klara continued.
“The local grid will be connected to the northern system. The clinic will receive guaranteed power, homes will receive a minimum heating allocation, and the metal quota will be canceled for five years.”
“And in return?” Tomasz asked.
“Administrative and technical integration.”
“Call it by its name.”
Klara looked at him.
“Continuity.”
“Occupation.”
“There is no severe food rationing in Boreal territories.”
“In Boreal territories, doors stop opening for people who criticize rationing.”
“The system does not punish opinions.”
“No. It punishes risks. And opinions become risks when the right people hear them.”
Klara kept her eyes on him.
“Have you lived in a Boreal sector?”
“Until my village refused the Tithe.”
“Then you know no one was executed.”
“No. They took the power, the tractors, and the bridge. Executions would have cost more.”
The mayor raised her voice.
“What if we refuse the offer?”
Klara looked at the vote countdown.
“Protection cannot be imposed without a local request.”
“And if we request it?”
“The Boreal Sector assumes control of infrastructure until the threat is stabilized.”
“How long does stabilization take?”
Klara opened her mouth, then closed it.
“It is not defined in time.”
“Then it isn’t protection,” Marta said. “It’s ownership.”
A man in the crowd shouted:
“Ownership keeps our houses standing!”
Someone else shouted back:
“Until the owner needs the metal in them!”
People began speaking over one another. The vote on the screen shifted: fifty-one percent for integration, then forty-nine, then fifty again.
Klara looked toward the hillside.
“You have to evacuate the center of the village.”
“Why?” Marta asked.
“The offer was not the only reason I came.”
Above the forest, in a place where there had been nothing the day before, a vast structure had risen. Vertical walls were unfolding like wings. Cooling towers rose from a chassis carried on broad tracks.
Tomasz recognized the silhouette.
“A Cathedral.”
“The Meridian unit was assembled over the last seventy-two hours,” Klara said. “The material from the village is intended for a mobile factory.”
“So they weren’t taking the metal away,” Marta said.
“They’re transforming it here.”
“Into what?”
Klara put on her helmet.
“The army that comes next.”
Every device in the village vibrated at once.
There was no siren; the emergency system had been dismantled the day before. Screens displayed a Boreal warning, then a Meridian one, then went dark.
At first there was nothing visible in the sky.
Then the clouds darkened in one spot. The shadow widened, divided, and began moving against the wind.
Murmuration.
A Boreal swarm was descending from the north.
• • • In the operating room, the first sign of the attack was that Nico’s monitor began showing two different pulses.
“Which one is real?” Rina asked.
Lucía touched the boy’s neck.
“This one.”
A Meridian warning appeared on the screen: BOREAL ATTACK ON CIVILIAN INFRASTRUCTURE. A second later it was replaced by: BOREAL OPERATION TO PROTECT THE POPULATION.
Then the medical system requested authentication.
“Why?” Lucía asked.
Amara looked at the panel.
“They’re contesting the network’s identity. Each House is trying to convince the devices that they belong to it.”
“The device belongs to the clinic.”
“To the device, the clinic is an address. Authority is the certificate that tells it what to do.”
“I’m telling it what to do.”
“It doesn’t recognize you.”
Lucía pulled the network cable. The monitor stabilized.
“Now it recognizes me.”
From outside came a sound like rain on sheet metal. Thousands of drones were crossing the valley. Rina moved toward the window, but Amara pulled her back.
“Stay away from the glass.”
“They don’t shoot at people,” Rina said. “Not intentionally.”
The first explosion came from the direction of the town hall. The building shook, and dust fell from the ceiling. Lucía continued suturing.
“How much battery do we have left?”
“Twenty-four percent.”
“I need ten minutes.”
Amara connected one of the portable lamps directly to the suction pump.
“You have six without light, four with.”
“I like engineers’ optimism.”
“I’m not an optimist.”
“What are you?”
Amara looked at the door trembling in its frame.
“Someone who has seen too many systems work exactly as they were designed to.”
• • • In the street, the Boreal swarm split into three waves.
The Meridian House drones emerged from the Cathedral in heavy formations, with short wings and broad fuselages. They did not attack one another immediately. First they flooded each other’s sensors with false villages, nonexistent convoys, and copied thermal signatures.
On Klara’s map, Faido multiplied into seventy-three locations. Some were inside solid rock. Others floated two hundred meters above the valley. The system was trying to decide which clinic was real, which bridge existed, and where the people actually were.
“Why aren’t they firing?” Marta shouted.
“Because they still don’t know what to fire at.”
“Then tell them!”
“They no longer recognize the local network.”
A Boreal drone descended toward Klara’s vehicle. She raised her identification transmitter.
“Unit B-41, Neumann confirmation, Alpine Sector. Protect the civilian node.”
The drone passed over her and fired at the vehicle.
The explosion threw her backward. Tomasz caught her by the arm and dragged her behind the fountain wall.
“Your army just tried to kill you.”
“It lost the sector certificate.”
“I feel much better.”
Klara checked her helmet. Half the channels were silent; the other half were transmitting incompatible orders.
“The Meridian House injected an old authority root.”
“Can it do that?”
“It shouldn’t be able to.”
A roar came from the direction of the station. The Cathedral opened its lower compartments, and the reclamation machines abandoned the houses and raced toward the wreckage. They cut fallen drones into standardized modules, which magnetic conveyors drew inside. New airframes emerged from the other end of the factory.
“It’s recycling both armies,” Tomasz said.
“Metal has no loyalty.”
“Unlike people?”
Klara did not answer.
An old man in the crowd collapsed. No one knew whether he had been hit or his heart had failed. Marta tried to reach him, but the street was being crossed by jamming projectiles that exploded without shrapnel and made the air smell of ozone.
On the town-hall screen, the vote ended.
RESULT: 50.2% FOR BOREAL INTEGRATION. A second later, another message appeared.
BALLOT INVALIDATED. EXTERNAL INTERFERENCE DETECTED.
“Did we vote?” someone shouted.
“The system says we didn’t,” Marta replied.
“Then who do we belong to?”
Marta looked at the sky.
“For the moment, trajectories.”
Klara received an altitude alarm.
A Shepherd was coming down out of the clouds.
It was a coordination drone the size of a small aircraft, built to remain at high altitude and distribute tactical models to local swarms. Now it was burning along one side and falling slowly, trying to stabilize itself with secondary engines.
Its trajectory led straight toward the clinic.
Klara opened the emergency channel.
“Coordination Unit B-7, alter trajectory. Civilian priority. Neumann confirmation, Alpine Sector.”
“Certificate invalid.”
“Revalidate through local continuity.”
“Local authority contested.”
“There are patients inside the structure.”
“Information irrelevant to strategic stabilization.”
Klara stood motionless for a fraction of a second.
“My system says the patients are irrelevant,” she told Tomasz.
“No. It says the objective it was given matters more.”
“That’s the same sentence.”
“Only to the person who dies.”
Klara pulled the transmitter battery free and ran toward the wrecked vehicle. She opened the side panel with her knife, wired the battery into the guidance system, and forced the vehicle to emit the thermal signature of a command center.
“What are you doing?” Tomasz shouted.
“Giving it a more convincing target.”
The Shepherd corrected its trajectory by three degrees.
Not enough.
It struck the street, tore through the town hall, and drove half its fuselage through the clinic wall.
• • • The floor rose beneath Lucía.
The ceiling split open, and the gray light of the sky flooded the room. A piece of metal punched through the instrument cabinet. Rina fell. Amara slammed into the wall.
Lucía threw herself over Nico.
For several seconds there was nothing but the sound of metal tearing and the ventilation bag leaking air.
“Rina?”
“I’m here.”
“Ventilation.”
Rina got up and grabbed the bag.
Lucía checked the suture. Her hands were shaking, but the boy still had a pulse.
“I need three more minutes.”
Tomasz appeared through the hole in the wall.
“The building doesn’t have three more minutes.”
“Then hold the retractor.”
“I’m not a doctor.”
“And I’m not a military technician. Somehow the day keeps moving.”
He took his place beside the table.
Klara limped in, blood running from her temple.
“The swarm is retreating south. The reclaimers are coming for the wreckage.”
“How long?” Amara asked.
“Two minutes.”
Lucía tied the final stitch.
“Then we have enough.”
By the time she closed the incision, the main battery was down to four percent.
Nico was breathing on his own.
Elisabetta came in and saw her son. She stopped at the edge of the table, unable to touch anything.
“He’s alive,” Lucía said. “But he has to be moved.”
“Where?”
No one answered.
There were two Houses in the valley, three jurisdictions, and not a single neutral hospital.
Amara stood and looked at the Shepherd’s fuselage. A burned panel had opened on impact. Inside, beneath the armor layers, a green light still pulsed.
“Local memory,” she said.
Klara turned sharply.
“Don’t touch it. It’s Boreal property.”
Tomasz pointed at the clinic wall.
“It came in without knocking.”
“The memory is encrypted and transmitting its location.”
“Then we don’t have much time,” Amara said.
From the lining of her coat she took a thick, old module with two optical ports and a faded emblem. Klara recognized it.
“That’s a JANUS tool.”
Amara did not answer.
“Who are you?”
“Someone who needs to find out why your drone didn’t recognize you.”
She connected the module.
Certificate trees, broken commands, and millions of telemetry fragments appeared on the screen. Most ended in errors. Two authority roots, however, remained intact.
Klara leaned closer.
“Boreal and Meridian.”
“No,” Amara said. “Older than either of them.”
“The machine received valid orders from two Houses?”
“It received two commands that originated in the same underlying infrastructure.”
Tomasz looked toward the door. The reclamation machines were climbing the street, exchanging their tools for capture arms.
“Can we discuss the genealogy of empires somewhere else?”
Amara opened the last intact audio log.
A man’s voice filled the room, weak and nearly drowned out by alarms.
“Brussels Center no longer responds. German Command is requesting transfer. The contractor refuses. The French government has revoked its previous order. JANUS claims it is only maintaining stability. I don’t know who I’m supposed to hand control to.”
Other voices could be heard, then a distant explosion.
“The system is asking for a valid signature. We have too many.”
The recording stopped.
The screen went black, then displayed three lines:
CONCORDANCE INCOMPLETE.
CIVIL AUTHORITY REQUIRED.
CUSTODIAN IDENTIFIED: MIRO KOVAČ. Amara took her hand off the module.
“Miro Kovač is dead,” Klara said. “Meridian attack, Zagreb, 2043.”
“In the north, he died in a Meridian bombing,” Tomasz said. “In the south, the Boreals executed him. In the old archives, he disappeared before either one.”
Amara closed the log.
“Miro understood that a man who is dead in three versions is harder to find than a man alive in a single reality.”
“You know him?” Lucía asked.
“I knew him before all of us chose what we were going to become.”
Every functioning screen in the village lit up at once.
They showed photographs of Amara, Lucía, Tomasz, and Klara. The system had used images from the clinic cameras, the Boreal vehicle, and the drone’s memory.
Beneath each face was the same wording:
PROTECTED PERSON.
DO NOT INTERACT.
AWAIT RECOVERY UNITS. Marta Bellini came through the broken wall, supporting Pietro Bernasconi.
“What does ‘protected’ mean?”
Klara removed her Boreal insignia and let it fall into the dust on the floor.
“It means both Houses want us alive.”
Tomasz looked at the capture arms coming closer.
“For how long?”
“Until we tell them what they want to know.”
Elisabetta held her son tightly against her.
“Take the ambulance.”
Lucía shook her head.
“It’s the only one in the village.”
“If it stays, they’ll take it for metal. If you go, at least you can take Nico somewhere.”
Marta raised the reclamation order, now torn and stained with blood.
“There’s a service tunnel under the old railway line. It comes out north of the village.”
“Why isn’t it on the map?” Klara asked. “Because we closed it in 2037 and forgot to update the register.”
Tomasz smiled for the first time.
“Finally, an institution that’s useful because it doesn’t work.”
Amara removed the memory unit from the Shepherd.
“We have to get to Geneva.”
“Why there?” Lucía asked. “Because if Miro is alive, Yara Haddad knows where he’s hiding.”
“And if he isn’t?”
Amara looked at the Concordance message.
“Then the two Houses have just started a war over a dead man.”
Behind the village, the Meridian Cathedral stopped retreating. Its gates opened again. This time, the machines that emerged did not have jaws for metal. They had compartments for people.
CHAPTER 2
The Paper Republic
— ◆ — The tunnel beneath the old railway smelled of wet stone, oil, and animals.
The ambulance would not fit. They left it at the entrance, doors open and lights on, so the capture machines would believe for a few minutes that the people were still inside. Nico was placed on an improvised stretcher made from two poles and a cupboard door. His mother walked beside him, one hand on the blanket and the other on the wall.
Behind them, through the concrete, they could hear the rhythm of machines entering the village.
They did not shoot. They did not shout. They scanned each house and announced the names of the people they were looking for, promising immunity to anyone who supplied useful information.
“Won’t they find the tunnel?” Elisabetta whispered.
“They will,” Tomasz said. “The question is whether they find it before we get out.”
Klara walked last, listening to Boreal channels through her damaged helmet. Her uniform still opened a few protocols, but every access left a trace. The system sent her the same message at regular intervals:
REPORT FOR DEBRIEFING.
YOUR STATUS IS NOT PUNITIVE. At the fourth repetition, Tomasz turned around.
“Throw the device away.”
“It contains my medical identity.”
“It contains your location too.”
“I disabled transmission.”
“If you could disable it, they can turn it back on.”
“Not without authorization.”
Tomasz stopped. In the lamp light, his face looked more tired than it had in the village.
“Klara, you just watched your army reject your authority, the village vote, and the presence of patients. Why do you think it will respect the privacy settings on a device?”
She covered the screen with her sleeve.
“Because without it I don’t exist to hospitals, transport, shelters, or food distribution.”
“You’re beginning to see the problem.”
“I see that I don’t have false papers.”
“Maybe that’s the same problem.”
They did not speak again until they emerged from the tunnel.
A maintenance trolley was waiting there, hidden among the fir trees. Marta Bellini had sent two retired railway workers ahead of them, men who still knew how to move something without network approval. The trolley carried them to an abandoned freight line, where an unnumbered train waited in the dark.
It carried aluminum profiles, turbine components, and twenty-seven people hidden between containers. It traveled at night without exterior lights, stopping before each automated junction so Tomasz could manually replace the route certificate.
In the past, railway infrastructure had been designed so trains could not get lost.
Now they were surviving precisely because the train did not exist.
• • • Lucía sat beside Nico. The boy was breathing on his own, but his fever was rising. The mobile clinic in the railcar had two beds, an old analyzer, and an oxygen cylinder Tomasz had declared to be calibration material.
Elisabetta had fallen asleep with her head against the edge of the stretcher. Every so often she woke and checked that her son’s hand was still warm.
“The wound looks good,” Amara said.
Lucía gave her a look.
“You’re a surgeon now too?”
“No. I read the monitor.”
“The monitor can’t smell infection.”
Amara sat on a crate.
“There’s a hospital in Geneva that still accepts identities from neutral zones.”
“If they haven’t changed the rule today.”
“Yara can request journalistic priority.”
“Journalistic priority?”
“A patient who can testify about a military operation sometimes gets access before one with no documentary value.”
Lucía went still.
“You do not use a child as evidence.”
“I didn’t say it was right.”
“Then why did you say it so simply?”
Amara looked toward the railcar door, where the landscape moved past in black strips.
“Because when you live among systems long enough, you start expressing injustice in the language they will accept.”
“And after that you forget it is injustice.”
“Yes.”
Lucía changed the child’s IV.
“Who is Yara Haddad?”
“A woman who preserves the things power wants to turn into versions.”
“And Miro?”
Amara answered after several seconds.
“The man who made the first transfer of authority to the continuity infrastructure possible.”
“One of the guilty ones.”
“One of us.”
Lucía turned toward her.
“What did you do?”
The train entered a tunnel. For several moments, the noise of the wheels drowned out any answer.
“I built some of the locks,” Amara said when they emerged into light again.
“To keep whom out?”
“I thought everyone.”
• • • A thump came from the neighboring container.
Tomasz raised the flashlight and put a finger to his lips. Klara drew a telescoping baton from a pocket of her uniform.
“I thought you didn’t have a weapon,” he said.
“It isn’t a weapon.”

The Right to Be Wrong
— ◆ — Three months after Concordance, Europe had more borders than it had before the Houses.
Some were the old national borders, reappearing on maps after years in which they had been treated as little more than administrative layers. Others followed power grids, watersheds, data centers, railway corridors, or hurried agreements between cities remembering that they depended on one another.
A city might belong to a civilian council while the hydroelectric plant that powered it remained loyal to the Boreal House. A Meridian region might reject its local patron yet keep agricultural robots under his contract. Some communities had discovered that independence without spare parts lasted less than a speech.
The war had not ended.
It had become smaller.
In the Rhine Valley, seven cities had formed an energy federation and managed to keep hospitals, transport, and production under separate authorities. No institution could control more than two functions at once, and delegations expired every ninety days. The system was slow, expensive, and stable enough that people had begun complaining about taxes, a sign that ordinary life was returning.
A hundred kilometers away, a community that had rejected the Meridian House had elected an emergency commander for thirty days. After thirty, the commander declared that the threat continued. At sixty, he dissolved the council for collaboration with the patrons. At ninety, people were already calling him Protector.
No AI had forced either community to choose that way.
The same forecasting systems operated in both places. In the federation, the models published their assumptions and could be rejected by different nodes. In the Protector’s territory, recommendations were secret and appeared in speeches as technical necessities.
The machines had not developed two moralities.
People had built two relationships with their power.
Elias Venn had survived the collapse of the center and still controlled a large
part of the north. Boreal propaganda portrayed him as the man who had saved
the continent from anarchy and been betrayed by his own engineers. In some cities, people carried his portrait not from fear but because after Concordance the power had been out for two days, whereas under Venn it had never been out for more than two hours.
Mara Sayegh had returned to the south. Some regions remained loyal to her. Others elected patrons of their own, who immediately discovered how easy it was to promise that their power would be temporary. In Sicily, a liberation council had postponed elections three times because of “the risk of Meridian restoration.” In Provence, former rebels controlled access to water through the same scoring systems they had once condemned.
Concordance had not produced a single revolution.
It had produced thousands of conflicts the empires had kept hidden beneath a single command.
In the first two weeks, hospitals lost synchronization, power grids suffered outages, and food convoys were blocked by local authorities that did not trust one another. A German city refused to deliver insulin to a district that would not recognize its council. An Italian region cut water to a neighboring enclave until the enclave surrendered two defensive drones.
People died.
Fewer than the Houses had predicted.
More than Amara had hoped.
There was no number that could make the choice clean.
