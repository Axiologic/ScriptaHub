Progress meets the next constraint
What We Still Have to Solve asks why technological progress leaves important problems unresolved.
Its opening hospital depends on electricity, water, deliveries, trained staff, and public trust.
A heatwave can strain several of those systems at once.
The book follows these connections instead of treating each crisis as an isolated subject.
Its distinctive question is what becomes the next constraint when an intervention succeeds.

Cheaper is only the beginning
Consider the book's example of electricity generation becoming dramatically cheaper.
That improvement still needs grid connections, transformers, skilled crews, and workable permits.
Otherwise, falling production costs can coexist with slow connections and high household prices.
The bottleneck has moved, so buying more generation no longer addresses the whole problem.
Readers learn to distinguish missing technology from infrastructure, incentives, and authority that cannot coordinate.
The chapters follow these changing constraints through climate, health, poverty, security, and AI.

Power opens a water question
Cheap power also changes which water supplies become practical.
Desalination can expand supply, but electricity is only one requirement.
Membranes, pipelines, brine disposal, and access rules still shape the outcome.
Producing fresh water does not decide which households can receive it.
The book's method keeps those consequences inside the original promise of a solution.

A system that can change course
The constructive direction is a civilization able to detect mistakes and change course.
That means preserving evidence, protecting challenge, and testing assumptions before scaling commitments.
AI could widen that feedback, rather than decide society's values.
Begin with Why Solving One Bottleneck Reveals Another, then follow The Corrigible Civilization.
Read it to judge a solution by the complete service people actually receive.
