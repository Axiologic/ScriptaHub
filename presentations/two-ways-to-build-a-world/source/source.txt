Two Ways to Build a World — Ten-minute edition Two Ways to Build a World Ten-minute edition The book revisits Riane Eisler’s partnership–domination distinction without relying on a peaceful prehistoric golden age. It treats the two patterns as ways to coordinate people: one can make refusal costly, while the other makes contribution, reciprocal rules, and contestable authority worthwhile. Neither is pure, and neither belongs to one period or ideology. The question is which material, social, institutional, and technical conditions make each pattern more likely. 1. The idea that refuses to die Eisler’s historical account remains contested, yet her analytical contrast can still be useful. The book separates an empirical question about migration and prehistoric violence from a more general question: why does one social order reward coercion while another rewards contribution? From binary to coordinate system Partnership and domination describe dimensions of relationships, not whole civilizations. Examine who can leave, speak, appeal, observe decisions, impose sanctions, and convert one advantage into many forms of control. A hierarchy can be necessary and still remain bounded and contestable. Two roads to status Influence can grow through dominance, backed by fear and the capacity to impose costs, or through prestige, granted because others value competence. Prestige can later be converted into coercive office. The crucial design problem is preventing one kind of status from governing unrelated domains. Read this chapter in the complete edition 5. Equality is something people do Egalitarian orders require active counter-power rather than an absence of ambitious people. Coalitions, criticism, courts, labor organization, replication, exit, and protected voice can restrain would-be monopolists. Formal consent says little when refusing destroys a person’s livelihood and membership at once. Read this chapter in the complete edition 6. Cooperation is not innocence Durable cooperation needs boundaries, memory, monitoring, and proportionate sanctions. Shared resources cannot be governed by goodwill alone, yet enforcement need not become unchecked central command. The institutional task is to make rule-breaking answerable without giving enforcers permanent arbitrary power. Read this chapter in the complete edition 7. The tribal trap Human solidarity can coexist with exclusion of outsiders. In-group cooperation is not automatically partnership across group boundaries. The book asks whether rules protecting members also constrain how they treat strangers, rivals, and vulnerable people beyond the group. Read this chapter in the complete edition 8. Surplus, storage, and chokepoints Stored resources and narrow gates can make power durable. Whoever controls grain, land, payment rails, cloud infrastructure, or discovery can command more than their visible holdings suggest. Technology may move chokepoints rather than abolish them. Scale changes the game Personal trust weakens as strangers and specialized roles multiply. Large-scale cooperation needs procedures, records, rights, and institutions that replace knowledge of each individual. Scale does not force domination, but it raises the cost of keeping authority visible and contestable. Read this chapter in the complete edition 11. Every age has its power technology Horses, writing, guns, printing, networks, and models change who can coordinate, monitor, persuade, and resist. The same invention can strengthen concentrated command and distributed cooperation. Political outcomes depend on the surrounding rules, not the tool alone. How partnership fails Cooperative orders can drift toward oligarchy, procedural overload, charismatic capture, moral purity, or free-riding. Success creates assets worth capturing. AI can become an empire machine AI can lower the cost of prediction, persuasion, surveillance, classification, and administrative action. Combined with concentrated data and infrastructure, these capabilities may turn many chokepoints into one computational layer. The book treats this as an institutional risk, not an unavoidable destiny. Read this chapter in the complete edition 17. AI can lower the cost of partnership The same tools can help people read rules, inspect contracts, translate, verify claims, deliberate, and coordinate locally. Their value for partnership depends on traceability, access, portability, and the ability to contest machine-assisted decisions. Read this chapter in the complete edition 18. Designing a non-utopian future A serious design starts with human ambition, tribalism, error, crisis, and exploitation. Make domination expensive, visible, narrow, and reversible; make cooperation easier to sustain at scale. The closing claim is architectural: institutions can change which form of coordination is cheaper.
