Good intentions enter the witness box
The Right to Help questions when an attempt to improve lives becomes a claim over them.
The human author’s wish to help meets an artificial voice instructed to challenge its assurances.
That voice is a literary instrument, not an oracle with proven inner experience.
The book examines help through the recipient’s power as well as the helper’s intention.
Readers encounter an inquiry that makes both intervention and comfortable inaction answerable.

An offered benefit changes power
A helpful intervention changes who can refuse, wait, decide, and later describe what happened.
Consent becomes difficult when the alternative to accepting help is serious deprivation.
The book also follows interests that lose money or authority when suffering is reduced.
These conflicts prevent a simple division between generous helpers and ungrateful recipients.
The examples support a deeper question about who can challenge the shape of an intervention.

Help must be able to relinquish control
Later chapters examine power acquired for good and then preserved by redefining the good.
The proposed response includes criticism, repair, meaningful refusal, and the return of authority.
No final moral algorithm removes the disagreements about benefit, risk, or legitimate limits.
The book keeps emergencies and the harms of doing nothing inside the argument.
Its possible constitution remains unfinished because the people affected must retain a real voice.

Read the trial without seeking absolution
Begin with the trial of good before turning to the proposed constitution of legitimate help.
Read its safeguards alongside the cases where delay can also preserve suffering.
The human and artificial voices expose different limits, rather than combining into a final moral authority.
The full inquiry offers a demanding way to examine philanthropy, intervention, and institutional ambition.
Read it to ask what those you claim to help could make you change.
