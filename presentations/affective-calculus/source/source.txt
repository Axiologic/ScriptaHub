Affective Calculus — Ten-minute read Affective Calculus From affective words to an explanatory model The book proposes Appraisal Affective Calculus, or AAC, as a research specification rather than an established psychological theory. A label such as “anger” is not enough: a reader or AI must ask whose goal was blocked, who seems responsible, what norm matters, and how much control the person perceives. The same smile may express joy, politeness, anxiety or irony. The model therefore treats emotions as evaluated relationships between agents and situations, while keeping bodily response, expression and subjective experience in view. Its claim is testable: changing a relevant fact should sometimes change the affective interpretation. A cautious ontology and an appraisal vector The opening chapters separate a bounded emotional episode from a mood, a persistent relational sentiment, a normative attitude, a motive and a socio-cognitive process. Empathy is not identical to compassion; courage is an action policy that can coexist with fear; recognition respect differs from admiration for achievement. AAC then describes an appraisal vector with goal congruence, novelty, certainty, control, agency, norms, fairness, dignity, belonging, attachment, reversibility and other dimensions. Values may be uncertain and must retain their evidence. Labels become contextual, probabilistic prototypes rather than rigid boxes. Time matters too: a brief episode can develop into a mood or slowly alter trust and attachment. The atlas distinguishes neighboring states The affective atlas refuses to collapse states merely because they share a positive or negative tone. Joy follows a favorable outcome, pleasure attends the experience itself, satisfaction completes a goal, and relief follows the removal of a threat. Fear concerns harm and limited control; anxiety adds uncertainty; anger combines obstruction, blame and a possible response. Grief concerns valued loss, whereas regret imagines one’s own alternative action. Guilt focuses on a reparable deed; shame can judge the self more globally; humiliation involves another person’s degrading act. Curiosity, surprise, awe and confusion track knowledge and meaning. Envy, jealousy, gratitude and admiration depend on comparisons and relationships, not valence alone. Relationships persist beyond episodes Later chapters model compassion, empathy, respect, trust, love and attachment as structures that influence how subsequent events are interpreted. Empathy can represent another person’s perspective without requiring shared feeling; compassion adds concern and a disposition to help. Recognition respect constrains how a person may be used, while appraisal respect evaluates competence or character. Trust concerns expectations about another agent under vulnerability, and suspicion updates those expectations in a different direction. Love, loyalty, forgiveness and hostility cannot be represented faithfully as single isolated labels. Their slower dynamics require memory, relationship histories and reasons for change. Reading emotional subtext The literary section asks what a character believes, wants and hides, rather than searching for explicit emotion words. A declaration of calm may be a social mask; irony can reverse surface meaning; an unreliable narrator may misdescribe another person’s motives. The model separates the character’s state, the narrator’s judgment and the intended effect on a reader. Appendix D turns these distinctions into probes involving ambivalence, trust trajectories, regret versus guilt, fear versus anger, love versus possession and culturally situated norms. Appendix C supplies a literary microcorpus covering the atlas. The cases are designed to test explanation, not merely vocabulary matching. Simulation is not experience For AI systems, the book distinguishes recognizing human affect, maintaining a functional simulated affective state and having subjective experience. An implementation may update goals, attention, memory and action policy in a way called “simulated fear,” yet this does not establish that anything feels fear. The proposed engineering architecture records an agent state, an appraisal, candidate labels, uncertainty, supporting evidence and counterfactual tests. Appendices A and B provide a schema and sample probes. The vocabulary must not mislead users about machine consciousness or encourage a system to present a performance of concern as human feeling. Verification and research limits A useful benchmark must ask why a model chose an emotion and what evidence would overturn it. If a character becomes able to escape, predicted fear should change; if responsibility shifts, guilt and anger may diverge. The evaluation therefore includes minimally different cases, evidence tracing, calibration, cultural variation and resistance to superficial lexical cues. The ethical chapter warns that accurate affect modeling can improve assistance but also sharpen manipulation, surveillance and false intimacy. The final research program proposes falsifiable hypotheses and acknowledges failure modes. AAC is a working language to refine against evidence, not proof that emotional life has been reduced to a formula.
