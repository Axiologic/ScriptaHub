The objective keeps its office
Egregopathy asks why replacing harmful leaders can leave an institution's behavior unchanged.
Its proposed concept describes harm protected by roles, targets, and selective information.
The book treats this as institutional design, not a psychiatric diagnosis.
It asks whether moral concern can change objectives when correction costs something.

What never reaches the report
Take its opening image of a company poisoning a river.
Imagine its managers seeing revenue reports while a downstream household loses usable water.
Different departments defend production, legal compliance, and reputation through separate measures.
An apology changes the message, while the discharge continues unchanged.
The book's test is whether evidence of harm can constrain that process.

A true example can still mislead
The historical chapters trace a disturbing pattern across very different forms of organized power.
The book later challenges the evidence it selected to make that pattern persuasive.
Accurate examples can mislead when contrary examples remain outside the frame.
Institutions also create public health, shared resources, scientific knowledge, and constraints on powerful actors.
That counterhistory tests whether the proposed concept explains more than its dramatic language suggests.

Concern needs authority
Readers gain two investigations: how institutions resist correction, and how arguments recruit belief.
The Moral Turing Test asks what an institution's apology makes impossible afterward.
Imagine authority pausing that harmful production, accepting a cost rather than changing only its language.
The Book as a Small Egregopath then questions the argument's own selective framing.
Read it to examine that mechanism while testing the boundaries of the explanation itself.
