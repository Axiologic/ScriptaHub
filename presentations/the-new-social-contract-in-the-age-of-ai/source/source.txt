The New Social Contract in the Age of Artificial Intelligence — A ten-minute reading The New Social Contract in the Age of Artificial Intelligence A ten-minute reading Why a new contract? The book asks what happens to rights and bargaining power if artificial intelligence makes production far less dependent on human labor. Its abundance is a working assumption, not a forecast or a promise of unlimited resources. A cheaper digital answer does not create unlimited electricity, land, water, or human time. The proposal therefore tests who controls productive infrastructure and whether people can challenge a provider on which they depend. Its twenty-one clauses are a political design for debate, not binding law. Read the full section People before productivity The first clauses give every person equal status regardless of economic usefulness. Administration may be delegated to AI or human operators, but collective goals remain subject to legitimate political control. A right to leave a provider matters only if identity, records, and essential services can move with the person. A nominal alternative cannot counter a monopoly over the conditions of everyday life. The book seeks revocable mandates, portable services, and independent ways to contest exclusion. Read the full section Material security and shared gains The material floor is defined by the functions needed for a dignified life, not by limitless consumption or a fixed sum for everyone. Funding and actual delivery must both pass scrutiny. The book considers taxes, social capital, public ownership, cooperatives, and mixed arrangements for sharing automation gains. Necessary work still deserves fair conditions and pay; scarce goods still require public allocation rules. Reciprocity does not license making hunger or medical abandonment routine sanctions. Read the full section Attention and experience Personalization can shape which ideas become noticeable without formally banning them. The proposed right to attentional autonomy includes reducing personalization and consulting independent sources. Privacy limits the arbitrary combination of information from different parts of life. People may build local and virtual communities, but access to dignified physical conditions cannot be coercively replaced by virtual experiences. These clauses protect the ability to form and reconsider preferences, not a state monopoly over truth. Read the full section Power that can be challenged A meaningful appeal requires reasons, correction of facts, effective review, and a remedy. An automated decision does not absolve its operator or public authority. The book separates those who set goals, operate infrastructure, and oversee rights, because concentrated technical power can defeat a written guarantee. Serious risks warrant proportionate safeguards, while emergency powers need defined purposes, time limits, and independent review. A system shutdown is not a general license to suspend rights. Read the full section Ecology, reproduction, longevity Abundance remains subject to ecological budgets and losses that money cannot always replace. The demographic chapter examines the tension between finite carrying capacity, cumulative births, and very low mortality without endorsing coercive control over bodies. Reproductive choices require information, accessible services, and freedom from pressure; children retain their own rights. If radical longevity becomes possible, longer lives must not turn office, wealth, or expertise into permanent barriers for newer generations. This is an institutional scenario, not a medical prediction. Read the full section A future that can revise itself Research and exploration matter beyond immediate profit, but potentially grave harms require staged tests and independent evidence. Future generations need recoverable infrastructure, accessible knowledge, and institutions they can change. Space exploration cannot substitute for obligations to Earth or erase physical limits. Possible artificial consciousness calls for evidence-based review of moral status, not automatic personhood for every program. The book favors a civilization with several paths forward over one locked into a single mandatory mission. Read the full section Implementation is the test The final chapters ask who funds each guarantee, who can enforce it, and why powerful actors would comply. They compare technocratic patronage, private capital with redistribution, predominant public ownership, and a polycentric mixed order. The preferred mixed order needs real alternatives, not formal diversity alone. Fiscal shocks, leadership changes, minority protection, ecological pressure, attention, and access for the next generation become stress tests. The twenty-one draft articles remain a proposal to revise. Abundance is legitimate when it reduces dependency rather than merely making it comfortable. Read the full section
