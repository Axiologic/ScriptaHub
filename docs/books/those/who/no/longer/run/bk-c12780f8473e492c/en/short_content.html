Those Who No Longer Run

A ten-minute edition — this synopsis includes the ending.

A civilisation built around retreat

Arua is a tidally locked planet: permanent daylight burns one hemisphere while darkness freezes the other. The Nivari live in the narrow Twilight Belt between them. Their lateral vision, powerful hind limbs and sensitive auditory sails belong to a prey species. Flight once meant survival. Underground cities, living infrastructure and orbital mirrors have made an enduring peace possible, but their safety depends on systems that few inhabitants can question.

The Great Hearing administers fear as well as resources. The Chamber of Measure allocates food, registers families and regulates reproductive rights. Population control appears as careful stewardship of a finite world. Yet delayed birth permissions can consume a lifetime, and official statistics exclude communities whose survival would complicate the promise of universal protection. Social exclusion is hidden inside administrative order.

Sava Ilen, a demographer, believes institutions can be repaired through truthful records. Her partner Oru Ven maintains the root network that carries water and nutrients. Their daughter Nemi grows up among their disagreements and loyalties. Sava’s brother Taren has an unusually delayed flight response. Therapy treats his difference as a defect; a coming crisis will give it another name.

The animal inside the ceremony

At the Festival of Retreat, mechanical shadows rehearse collective escape. A real saryx enters the square. The crowd’s practised movements become panic, and people die. Taren hesitates, then turns towards the predator and saves Nemi. A frightened person becomes a public image of courage. The difference between freezing and choosing to stay will gradually disappear from the heroic story.

Beyond the registered city lies Vaor, with hunger, precarious shelters and people denied ordinary recognition. Ecological collapse compounds their danger. The old removal of predators has damaged the living systems on which the settlements depend. Oru works to restore sterile edge hunters to a failing ecosystem. His ecological restoration requires observation and restraint, while the authorities prefer a world that looks controlled.

Taren begins organising collective defence against animals and raiders. Training gives people a way to survive together; discipline makes that ability repeatable. Daran Veyr understands its political potential. The First Face becomes a symbol around which a movement can gather. Protection, military organisation and the promise of belonging begin to reinforce one another.

When protection becomes a weapon

During a solar storm, Vaor’s isolation exposes the dependence of everyday life on water, heat and transport. A child, Sio, is born without official registration and sleeps in a filter box. Taren helps keep him warm. Such small acts of care coexist with the armed identity taking shape around him.

The central authorities attempt to restore order with veyrin mist. Their intervention is described through technical risk estimates and the language of nonlethal force. Oru dies protecting Nemi, and the official account cannot contain the human cost. Sava loses her partner. Nemi learns that evidence can preserve a person whom a casualty total makes anonymous.

Taren’s resistance wins a decisive battle at Deaf Lake, using knowledge inherited from Oru’s infrastructure work. Victory creates a new problem: the defenders now possess power over others. His Code forbids killing after surrender, harming civilians and using food as an instrument of domination. Individual responsibility must survive collective fear, but a written rule cannot enforce itself.

The costs inside victory

The movement divides society into Faces, Hands, Memory and the Protected. Fighters gain prestige, material advantages and reproductive privileges. The people who build, heal and maintain the world remain essential, yet their contribution is increasingly judged from the warrior’s viewpoint. The first outlines of a caste system appear within a promise of liberation.

At Lur, forces acting in the movement’s name massacre people who have surrendered. Nemi preserves the evidence. Taren holds a trial and punishes those responsible, resisting the claim that an entire community can inherit guilt. Military justice matters, but its necessity also reveals how far his organisation has moved from the intimate work of keeping neighbours alive.

Evidence also implicates Daran in manufactured provocations that helped create the movement’s need for a saviour. During their confrontation, Taren kills him. He cannot honestly describe every part of his decision as necessity. Personal hatred and political advantage share the act. Removing a manipulator does not remove the institutions, loyalties or incentives he helped build.

Who holds the sky?

The White Winter makes control of orbital mirrors a means of political coercion. Changing the distribution of light changes harvests, hunger and bargaining power. Sava and Nemi travel to Ivarra, where the Gardens of Kinship offer comfort under conditions of captivity. Some residents value the shelter; others have lost years of contact with their families. Benevolent language cannot make detention voluntary.

Archives reveal that the old authorities had monitored Taren’s unusual response long before the festival. His life was shaped by institutional selection as well as Daran’s manipulation, without being wholly determined by either. Nemi seeks shared control of the mirrors rather than another exclusive owner. Technical access becomes a constitutional question.

Taren’s army enters Ivarra through old infrastructure. Violence, rescue and looting unfold together. Raal dies protecting children from a saryx; Taren’s young son Sen survives with a burn and Raal’s necklace. The heroic account will honour sacrifice while narrowing the complicated person who made it.

A transfer of authority establishes five nodes: the Custodianship, the Chamber, Memory, the Hands and civilian representation. Emergency powers are limited by time, public records and shared authorisation. When a hostile force uses civilians as leverage, Taren refuses to cut their air and accepts greater losses among his own fighters. Constitutional limits acquire meaning through a costly choice.

A trial cannot finish the past

The trial of Seln Aru examines detention, falsified risk records and infrastructure decisions whose combined consequences were concealed behind separate administrative acts. Witnesses refuse easy symmetry. Therapy could help a patient and make him available to power. A guardian could protect residents and imprison them. Different intentions do not erase unequal responsibility.

Seln receives imprisonment, not the execution that would turn judgement into a simple reversal of violence. His later criticism of the new constitution remains valuable, even to people harmed by his rule. Nemi insists on preserving its source. Public accountability requires more than choosing which side deserves to speak.

The five-node system soon confronts accidents, resource conflicts and deadlocks. Taren intervenes in emergencies and accepts review afterwards. Exceptions accumulate. The Law of the Living expands as each crisis leaves another precedent. Sava and Nemi try to preserve the original circumstances, but institutional memory can justify repetition as readily as it can warn against it.

Equality at the examination door

Hereditary titles are abolished. Rank now depends on service and examinations, yet commanders’ children receive better nutrition, education and instructors. Private academies turn family advantage into apparently neutral merit. Social mobility remains formally available while unequal preparation determines who can use it.

Reproductive privileges return as compensation for military risk. Genetic selection follows the traits rewarded by the new order: delayed alarm, dense bones, early fertility and shorter sails. Vessa, a physician, tries to separate treatment from enhancement and publish medical risks. Private clinics rename aggression as stability under stress. Parental choice and public incentives reshape bodies together.

Sava guarantees a first birth and makes allocation more transparent, but poor harvests force restrictions on second births. An unauthorised pregnancy can be supported only by shifting costs to the community. Freedom, food scarcity and reproductive autonomy remain entangled. Better procedures do not abolish the material limits from which coercion grows.

The son who inherits a role

Sen earns high examination scores, defends hungry civilians and demands recognition for overlooked workers. Yet his name gives his objections power that anonymous people lack. He can be fair in individual encounters while learning that the Faces are the world’s only true adults, because they accept danger.

When Sen kills a rebel commander to prevent a reactor disaster, Vessa calls it a defensible choice in a situation many people helped construct. Calling the act simply good would erase everyone else’s responsibility. Sen wants moral certainty; his family offers the harder work of understanding consequences.

Taren tries to arrange a private conversation with his son, without uniforms. Another emergency intervenes. He dies in his sleep before they speak. Grief becomes a mandate when Sen interprets his father’s unfinished notes as work he must complete. Sen is elected Custodian through valid procedures, while the founder’s name, military loyalty and public mythology make alternatives difficult to imagine.

The bodies that institutions reward

Decades later, the Vertical Custodianship has restored stable peace. The Faces administer force, the Hands production and Memory the records. The Protected have become the Interior People, guaranteed shelter but given little influence over frontier decisions. Occupational hierarchy becomes residential segregation, and most Faces have a Face parent.

Margin Campaigns regulate competition for tunnels and colonisation rights. Violence is scheduled and measured; military service offers status and larger families. Sen enforces the Code sincerely, making the institution’s moral authority harder to challenge. Genetic risks nevertheless emerge in selected lines. His marrow cancer exposes costs that arrive long after the rewards that encouraged them.

His daughter Arven wins the succession examination. She is capable and well prepared; most competing candidates are relatives. Meanwhile, children learn frontal confrontation and lose skill at retreat. Eye surgery improves forward focus by narrowing peripheral vision. Nemi, kept alive by medical technology, remembers that an abandoned response can still be useful. Collective memory preserves contradictions that public monuments simplify.

An opening is not an answer

A probe arrives from the Leth system, twelve light-years away. Its living tissues repair themselves, but neither its purpose nor its possible passengers can be identified. First contact begins in uncertainty. The Fleet sees strategic risk; Sio, now a specialist in organic systems, insists on distinguishing observations from claims of hostile intent.

A propulsion gate and interstellar expedition win the required votes. Their resource allocation burdens the Interior People while fleet families receive extra birth rights. Protest is permitted until it obstructs infrastructure, then suppressed without reported deaths. Recruitment turns an uncertain alien organism into an armoured enemy. Procedural legitimacy does not resolve who pays or whose imagination defines the unknown.

Arven also sees expansion as an outlet for a crowded society. Nemi asks that the expedition carry people able to withdraw if their presence causes harm. The first ship honours Raal, yet its manifest repeats the established hierarchy. Sio goes because someone must recognise living tissue as more than a possible weapon. He carries Oru’s principle: never close both valves; leave a path for those who come afterwards.

As the gate opens, Nemi refuses a final story of destiny. Her society carries its unresolved contradictions into space. A child hears that Leth is a new world and realises it is already old for its inhabitants: the newcomers are the travellers. The children look upward without seeking shelter. Whether that change is freedom, danger or both remains open.
