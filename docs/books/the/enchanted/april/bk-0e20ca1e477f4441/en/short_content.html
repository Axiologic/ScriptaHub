The Enchanted April

Novel by Elizabeth von Arnim (1922)

Four English women rent a small Italian castle for April. Elizabeth von Arnim follows what happens when they step away from the habits, duties and reputations that define them at home. The novel sets a month of sunshine against London routines and asks what companionship can change.

An advertisement becomes a possibility

In a dreary London club, Lotty Wilkins notices an advertisement offering an Italian castle to people who appreciate wistaria and sunshine. The invitation feels impossibly remote from her careful, overlooked life with Mellersh. She approaches Rose Arbuthnot, whose charitable work and troubled marriage have left little room for joy. The women imagine sharing the cost of the castle and then seek two more guests. Mrs. Fisher brings age, certainty and the authority of her memories; Lady Caroline Dester, called Scrap, hopes to hide from relentless social attention. The arrangements expose practical worries about money, propriety and strangers, but the women choose to go.

San Salvatore and the difficult company of strangers

The journey ends at San Salvatore, where the garden, light and sea give Lotty and Rose the space they had hoped for. Lotty responds immediately with gratitude and an expansive confidence that irritates more guarded people. Mrs. Fisher expects respect, quiet and control over the rooms; Scrap wants solitude rather than yet another circle of admirers. Their first days are therefore not a simple idyll. Meals, bedrooms, invitations and conversation become small disputes over who may decide how April is spent. Lotty keeps imagining that the place will change them, but the novel makes those changes uneven and personal.

Private lives begin to open

As the days pass, each woman tests a different kind of freedom. Rose struggles with her longing for Frederick, her husband, and the fear that his attention belongs elsewhere. Scrap discovers that being admired is not the same as being known. Mrs. Fisher clings to a past populated by famous writers and to the dignity that past gives her. Lotty’s delight can be intrusive, yet it also reaches toward people who have stopped asking for tenderness. The garden does not erase pride, jealousy or grief; it gives the guests time to recognize them. Their changing relations, rather than a dramatic external adventure, carry the middle of the novel.

The lives left behind come to Italy

The women’s refuge changes when people connected to their ordinary lives arrive. Mellersh Wilkins’s presence tests whether Lotty’s new ease can coexist with her marriage. Frederick’s arrival brings Rose’s hopes and doubts into the open. Mr. Briggs, the castle’s owner, is drawn to Scrap, while other encounters unsettle the careful distance she maintains. Mrs. Fisher too is pushed beyond her settled judgments. Von Arnim treats these meetings with irony: affection can make people generous, but self-interest and misunderstanding remain close at hand. San Salvatore becomes less a sealed escape than a place where old relationships can be reconsidered.

The end of April

The final chapters weave the guests’ separate hopes together. Frederick and Rose confront the gap between what they assumed and what they can tell each other. Scrap’s wish for freedom meets Briggs’s devotion, making her future less easily settled than the romances others imagine for her. Lotty continues to trust in the garden’s influence, while Mrs. Fisher’s certainty softens into a different awareness of those around her. The book’s ending does not turn April into a permanent escape. The guests leave on the first of May, but the scent of the acacias follows them down the hill: the experience has entered the lives to which they must return.
