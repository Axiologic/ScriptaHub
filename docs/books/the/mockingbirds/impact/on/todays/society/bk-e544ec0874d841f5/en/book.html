About Book

This essay reads Harper Lee’s To Kill a Mockingbird through its continuing questions about racial injustice and moral responsibility. Tom Robinson’s trial reveals how prejudice can determine a verdict despite evidence. Atticus Finch’s defense offers a model of acting with integrity under pressure, while Scout and Jem show how children absorb and question community beliefs. The book compares Maycomb’s legal and social hierarchy with present-day disparities in justice, education, and employment.

Later chapters examine empathy as a practice, the novel’s use in classrooms, and the cultural conversations prompted by film, theater, literature, and media. The essay argues that reading can sharpen awareness but must be joined to dialogue and action. It also asks whose stories adaptations emphasize and whose experiences remain overlooked. The mockingbird becomes a symbol of the duty to protect vulnerable people.
