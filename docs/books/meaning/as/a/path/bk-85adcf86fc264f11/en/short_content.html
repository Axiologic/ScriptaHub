Meaning as a Path

A short introduction to the research and the book

Can language become something a machine can check?

A language model can produce a convincing answer while leaving us uncertain what it actually understood. In a robot, a regulated workflow or a scientific process, that gap matters: an action may depend on the difference between what a sentence says and what someone has guessed it means. Meaning as a Path investigates a different way to approach that problem. It describes original research conducted by Sînică Alboaie, and invites scientific collaborators, investors and funders to examine the direction, its evidence and its limits.

The central proposal is to treat meaning in a defined world as answers to that world's questions. Who acts? What happens? To whom, when and under what condition? An unanswered question stays unanswered. Two sentences can express the same event in different words and still reach the same set of answers. This gives the reader a way to inspect an interpretation, rather than relying on the fluency of a response.

A route through meaning

The book builds this idea through the metaphor of a sky, a probe and a pilot. A word has a position in a high-dimensional space. The reader moves a probe through a sentence, learning how types of words change its route. The resulting arrangement can be read against the roles defined for a world. The images are approachable, but the book also explains what the representation must do and how the reader is trained from examples.

That distinction matters. A sentence is not just a bag of familiar words, and an identical word can play different roles in different contexts. The chapters explore nested sentences, ambiguity, multiple worlds and ontologies. They show why sentences of different lengths may lead to the same interpretation, and what remains visible when the reader cannot account for part of an input.

Evidence, questions and engineering choices

The research reports experiments in constructed worlds and on a real question set. The book discusses paraphrases, nesting, previously unseen words, and logical operators such as negation and conditions. It also compares the learned role structure with small-model and lexical baselines under the stated experimental conditions. These are research results in bounded settings, not proof that every open-ended text can be understood reliably.

Engineers can follow a ten-step account of the algorithm, its representation alternatives and the choices surrounding sparse codes. The book describes where training examples and role definitions come from, and why the questions chosen for a domain shape what can be understood. It also asks what measurements and further work could strengthen or change the conclusions. The public research status is linked from the full text.

From sentences to action

One application explored is a semantic interlock for robotics: before an instruction reaches an action, a separate layer can expose its interpretation and identify missing or uncertain information. The book also considers procedures, regulations and reports in enterprise and highly regulated industries, where traceable interpretations and explicit boundaries can matter as much as speed.

Other directions include personal assistants for defined worlds, document processing and scientific research automation. The compact reader described in the book suggests a possible route for moving more intelligence to edge devices. For particular bounded tasks, a specialised system may take over work otherwise assigned to a large model. That possibility should not be confused with a claim that the reader replaces an LLM on unrestricted language.

System 1, System 2 and the research ahead

Large language models can be viewed as a form of System 1: fast, generative and broadly capable. The direction described here points toward specialised System 2 systems that work with natural language and can operate as software on their own, alongside a System 1, or in a loop of mutual improvement. The book examines the role of verification and alignment while distinguishing current evidence from proposals and disciplined speculation.

Further chapters consider novelty, bias, representations and a possible system that discovers. They also draw a parallel with A Thousand Brains, while keeping an engineering account separate from an assertion about how the brain works. More advanced elements of the wider research are not yet public. The purpose of this edition is to make the direction and its present basis accessible for serious evaluation.

Where to begin

Read the opening chapters for the questions, the sky and the probe; turn to the experimental chapters for evidence; then explore robotics, regulated workflows and the ten-step algorithm for concrete engineering consequences. The final chapters make the boundaries explicit. Together they offer a way to judge not just what the system does today, but what kind of neurosymbolic architecture this research could make possible.
