Chapter two — Earth’s blue

Earth's nitrogen- and oxygen-rich atmosphere scatters short wavelengths of sunlight more effectively than long ones. The chapter introduces Rayleigh scattering and explains why a long path through the atmosphere can leave sunsets warmer in color. It also considers aerosols and other conditions that complicate the simple blue-sky explanation.

Chapter three — Light and particles

The chapter places visible light within the electromagnetic spectrum and examines wavelength, reflection, absorption and scattering. Rayleigh and Mie processes help explain why molecules, droplets and larger particles produce different appearances. Understanding their size relative to light's wavelength gives readers a practical way to interpret changing sky colors.

Chapter four — A moving canvas

Solar angle changes the distance light travels through air, altering the balance of colors from dawn to noon to sunset. Clouds, moisture, altitude and seasonal conditions add further variation. The chapter shows how ordinary observations record the interaction of sunlight, atmosphere and weather rather than a fixed property of the sky.

Chapter five — Air quality in color

Pollutants such as particulates, nitrogen oxides and ozone can alter scattering and reduce visibility. Haze and smoke may dull the sky or intensify certain sunsets, while also affecting health and ecosystems. The chapter connects what people see overhead with emissions, wildfire smoke and the need to measure and improve air quality.

Chapter six — Weather’s visible signs

Humidity, clouds and storms change how light reaches observers. Cumulus, stratus and high clouds produce different textures and colors, while dust and severe weather can create striking skies. The chapter treats sky appearance as a clue to atmospheric processes, with limits: color alone is not a complete forecast.

Chapter seven — The year overhead

The height of the Sun and the character of the air change with the seasons. Summer, autumn and winter bring different mixes of clear air, moisture and cloud cover. Latitude, altitude and urban pollution make the pattern local rather than universal. Attentive observation can reveal how the same place changes through the year.

Standardized observation helps track atmospheric conditions, pollution and light even when the human eye misses subtle shifts.
