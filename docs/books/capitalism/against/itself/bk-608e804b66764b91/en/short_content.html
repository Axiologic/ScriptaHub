Capitalism Against Itself

This ten-minute edition follows the argument of all sixteen chapters. The book asks whether capitalism can reform the forces that give it power. Its answer is conditional: reforms can preserve a system while restricting parts of it, but they usually begin with organized counterpower, not spontaneous moral improvement. The difficulty is keeping that counterpower alive after reform makes the original danger less visible.

1. Capitalism is not a moral agent

The first chapter defines capitalism as an institutional order of private productive ownership, markets, investment for expected return, and wage labor. It is not a person with intentions. Competitive pressure can reward a firm for polluting when others pay the cost, then reward cleaner production after the law changes the cost. Markets already depend on company, property, contract, bankruptcy, and monetary rules. Reform changes this institutional constitution; it need not abolish market coordination. The test is which valuable incentives a rule preserves and which harmful practices it needlessly permits.

2. Reform arrives under pressure

Major reform generally becomes possible when refusing it grows expensive. Britain’s nineteenth-century parliamentary changes followed mobilization, elite divisions, and fear of instability. Labor bargaining likewise grew from workers’ need for collective leverage, employers’ responses to conflict, and state concern with its wider costs. Calling reform “internal” can mislead: workers, voters, competitors, courts, and regulators belong to an economy while opposing its current allocation of power. Their conflicts can change rules without destroying the order. The settlement remains vulnerable if the organizations that won it later disappear.

3. The New Deal restricted a system to preserve it

In the American crisis of the 1930s, bank failures, unemployment, and political anger made the old arrangements difficult to defend. The New Deal did not replace private enterprise with central planning. Deposit insurance, securities oversight, labor rights, minimum wages, working-time limits, and social insurance altered what private actors were allowed or required to do. Some arrangements were later revised or weakened. The historical case shows that a capitalist order can sacrifice particular practices while retaining its deeper structure; it does not show that reform is permanent or automatically produced by markets.

4. Labor changes who can say no

A worker and an employer may both sign a contract voluntarily while facing radically different alternatives. Unions, sectoral bargaining, works councils, and labor law alter the unit of negotiation. Different countries sustain very different bargaining coverage, showing that worker voice is an institutional choice. Unions can also protect insiders or resist useful change; employer power can suppress wages and organization. The chapter’s point is reciprocal constraint, not the sanctity of either side. Individual virtue may fail when a responsible firm bears costs rivals can avoid; common minimum rules can prevent competition from selecting against restraint.

5. Capitalism has more than one constitution

Sweden’s postwar Rehn-Meidner model combined solidaristic wage bargaining, active labor policy, and macroeconomic restraint. Germany’s social market economy combined private enterprise with competition policy, social insurance, vocational institutions, collective bargaining, and employee representation. Neither arrangement erased capital ownership. Both show that the American corporate model is not the only possible capitalist structure. Coordinated systems can become rigid or protect insiders, while liberal systems may reallocate resources faster. Transplanting one visible rule can fail when complementary institutions—training, finance, housing, insurance—do not travel with it.

6. Other systems can reform, or fail while trying

The book then looks outside capitalism. British constitutional change, the Ottoman Tanzimat, and China’s post-1978 market reforms changed important mechanisms while preserving core institutions. Soviet perestroika shows another possibility: reform can unsettle an old coordinating order before a new one can function. Three failure modes follow: cosmetic change leaves power untouched; blocked change lets a system decay; destabilizing change removes necessary coordination too quickly. Reform is not moderation for its own sake. It is institutional surgery under uncertainty, with no guarantee that the patient survives.

7. The competitive morality trap

A firm that voluntarily bears an environmental, labor, privacy, or safety cost while its competitors legally avoid it may lose customers or investment. Responsible conduct can become a disadvantage when customers cannot observe the difference or will not pay for it. Brands, workers, investors, and industry standards sometimes reward restraint, but voluntary action is fragile where defection is profitable and hidden. A durable solution changes payoffs through standards, liability, taxes, bargaining rules, or other institutions. Those rules also have costs and can be captured. The absence of a common rule is itself consequential when costs can be exported.

8. Wealth can become rule-making power

Economic inequality changes character when wealth buys durable political influence. Lobbying, litigation, campaigns, expert networks, media ownership, associations, and career paths can shape policy without a secret conspiracy. The book presents Gilens and Page’s debated American study as evidence against assuming equal influence by default, not as a universal law. Technical regulation creates another dependence: agencies need industry knowledge, while firms can fund expertise and influence details for years. Reformers must repeatedly defend boundaries; interested actors may need only a few obscure exceptions. The problem is maintaining public capacity to understand and govern complex industries.

9. Success can end competition

Competition rewards successful firms, yet success can bring scale, data, networks, intellectual property, distribution control, and access to capital that make later entry difficult. Antitrust law recognizes that private ownership cannot include an unlimited right to eliminate the market itself. Concentration is not automatically monopoly; scale may reduce costs. The danger appears when it creates durable power over prices, access, rivals, suppliers, or workers. Profit from invention and rent from an uncontestable position can coexist in one firm. Large incumbents can also afford years of litigation, so slow enforcement may become ineffective even when rules are formally equal.

10. Mobile capital meets territorial states

States tax and regulate within borders, while assets, intellectual property, profits, digital services, and production can move. Capital mobility does not make national policy impossible, but the option to leave changes bargaining power. Domestic labor or environmental reform can shift a harmful activity elsewhere rather than end it. International coordination can limit rule arbitrage, yet distant institutions may weaken democratic control or freeze poor rules. National autonomy preserves experimentation but can intensify jurisdictional competition. The workable scale of governance depends on the scale of the activity and the location of counterpower.

11. Future costs have weak representatives

Firms, voters, and governments can discount costs that arrive after their own decision horizon. Climate risk is one case among antibiotic resistance, soil loss, infrastructure, education, cybersecurity, and financial tail risk. Ownership can reward care for an asset’s resale value, but it cannot price costs borne chiefly by outsiders or future generations. Future citizens cannot strike, sue before they exist, or move their capital. Institutions must represent them indirectly, which raises a tension: too little authority leaves damage ignored; too much insulated authority can become unaccountable technocracy. Neither markets nor short electoral cycles solve this automatically.

12. Reform can erode without repeal

Institutions can lose their function while their legal text remains. Budgets shrink, enforcement slows, thresholds fail to keep up with inflation, courts reinterpret scope, and technology outruns old categories. Historical institutionalism calls some of these processes drift, conversion, and layering. The American federal minimum wage illustrates drift when its nominal value stays fixed as prices rise. Capture concerns whose interests an institution serves; drift concerns failure to adapt. Both may occur together. Crisis-era public attention fades, while regulated industries retain permanent staff to watch and contest the rules. Reform therefore requires maintenance, not a single legislative victory.

13. Success can make restraint look unnecessary

When prevention works, the missing disaster may be mistaken for proof that prevention was unnecessary. Deposit insurance, bank safeguards, labor standards, safety codes, and maintenance can all face this problem. Their costs remain visible while their benefits are events that did not occur. Costs are often concentrated among actors able to organize; benefits are spread among people who may not know the institution exists. The book’s reform paradox runs from crisis to coalition, restraint, reduced harm, fading memory, erosion, and renewed vulnerability. Updating rules, measuring outcomes, and adversarial review can help, but each safeguard also needs scrutiny.

14. When does a reformed system change names?

Capitalist economies already include public pensions, schooling, health systems, central banks, labor standards, progressive taxation, and selected state ownership. Counting government programs alone does not identify the system. The book proposes three useful thresholds: who owns most productive assets, whether markets or administrative plans allocate most production and investment, and who holds meaningful residual control. Between laissez-faire and full administrative control lies a broad design space. Digital taxation, auditing, portable benefits, public infrastructure, and new ownership forms may enlarge it. Labels should clarify mechanisms rather than prevent analysis.

15. What would self-limitation require?

The answer is not unusually kind executives, but institutions that function when powerful people pursue their interests. The chapter names independent counterpower, contestable markets, adaptive rules, useful transparency, and governance at the right jurisdictional scale. Each remedy can produce its own failure: licensing may protect incumbents; labor protection may discourage hiring; privacy compliance may favor large firms. A self-limiting order must detect when its own restraints create new concentrations of power and revise them. Courts, agencies, markets, unions, auditors, and experts all need oversight or adversaries. No center should judge only itself.

16. A test for reformability

A system is reformable when serious changes in power can occur through recognized procedures without destroying the whole order. The book asks whether opposition can organize, failure can become known, rules can change without confiscating every legitimate expectation, new rules can be enforced, unintended effects can be measured, and decisions can later be revised. Capacity and context matter: the same law may work in one administration and become a market for bribes in another. Capitalism has advantages in decentralized ownership and the possibility of entry and failure, but can consume them through concentrated wealth and weak public institutions.

Where the argument ends

Every durable system needs organized opposition. Competition contests incumbents; antitrust contests monopoly; labor organization contests employer power; courts, elections, journalism, audits, and bankruptcy contest other forms of permanence or secrecy. None is pure, and each can be corrupted. The book’s practical standard is recoverability: can a society detect concentration, restore contestability, rebuild bargaining power, correct a failed regulator, and reverse a damaging reform without collapse? Capitalism can reform from within only when “within” includes actors able to say no and institutions strong enough to make that refusal matter after the crisis is forgotten.
