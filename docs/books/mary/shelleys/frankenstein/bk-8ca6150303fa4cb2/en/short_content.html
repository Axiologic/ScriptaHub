Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein

Ten-minute read · 2024

Creation and responsibility

This essay retells Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein with humour while keeping its central moral problem in view. Victor Frankenstein discovers how to create life, but his first response to the living being is horror and abandonment. The creature must learn what it is and how to survive without the care of its maker. Their conflict turns a scientific triumph into a story about responsibility, rejection, and revenge. The book also connects Shelley’s questions to artificial intelligence and biotechnology, asking what creators owe to what they bring into the world. Read the full introduction.

Victor’s dangerous dream

Victor grows up in Geneva, fascinated by natural forces and old alchemical writing. At the University of Ingolstadt, he turns his curiosity into an obsession with the boundary between life and death. He isolates himself from family and friends, gathers the materials for a human body, and works until his experiment succeeds. Yet the result frightens him. Rather than ask how to protect or teach the being he has made, Victor flees. The chapter treats his scientific ambition as inseparable from the care he refuses to provide. Read the full chapter.

A creature asking to belong

After Victor abandons him, the creature encounters a world that judges his appearance before hearing his voice. He secretly watches a family, learns language and human customs, and hopes that understanding will win him a place among people. Rejection instead turns loneliness into anger. When Victor learns that his young brother William has been killed and Justine is blamed, his silence adds another injustice to the first. The creature asks Victor for a companion who might share his isolation. Their meeting asks whether monstrosity lies in appearance, harmful acts, or the failure to recognise another being’s need for care. Read the full chapter.

The promise of a companion

Victor agrees to make a second creature, then fears what another life might mean. His promise is entangled with guilt over his first creation and anxiety about consequences he cannot control. Far from home, he begins the work but destroys it before it can live. The creature sees this as a second betrayal and promises to be with Victor on his wedding night. The chapter turns the question of scientific responsibility into a choice made under fear: Victor considers possible harm, but again leaves the creature without an answer to its isolation. Pursuit and final reckoning

Victor misreads the creature’s threat and prepares to defend himself at his wedding. Elizabeth becomes the victim. Grief then sends Victor after the creature across Europe and into the Arctic, where his pursuit consumes the rest of his life. The creature survives its maker, but its revenge has not given it companionship or peace. Their final encounter leaves no simple victor. The chain of loss began with an act of creation, yet every later choice deepened it. Read the full chapter.

Why the warning endures

The essay closes by returning to ambition, empathy, and the duties that accompany new knowledge. Victor’s experiment shows that making life is not the same as accepting responsibility for it. The creature’s exclusion shows how quickly fear can become cruelty on both sides. Shelley’s novel, as read here, remains a way to discuss the ethical consequences of powerful technologies without forgetting the people affected by them. Read the final reflections.
